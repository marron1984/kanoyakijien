# Nara's Trees: A Guide to the Ancient Giants of Park and Forest

Behind and above the temples, behind the deer and the stone lanterns, Nara is a city of trees. They are so large and so present that visitors often fail to register them as individual organisms — they become background, a green frame for the architecture, part of the scenery rather than the subject. This is a mistake. Nara's trees are among the most remarkable in Japan. Some have been growing since before the temples were built. The primeval forest that covers the mountain behind Kasuga Taisha has been protected from logging for over a thousand years — one of the oldest conservation decrees in recorded history. The camphor trees that shade Nara Park are centuries old, their trunks wider than cars. The cryptomeria cedars that line temple approaches are so tall and straight that they seem to hold up the sky.

To walk through Nara with attention to its trees is to engage with a different layer of the city's history — one measured not in centuries of human events but in the slow, patient accumulation of growth rings, bark layers, and root systems that have anchored this landscape through every change the human world has brought.

## Kasugayama Primeval Forest: A Thousand Years Untouched

The most important collection of trees in Nara — and arguably in all of Japan — stands on the slopes of Mount Kasuga, directly behind Kasuga Taisha shrine. The Kasugayama Primeval Forest is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, designated not for its architecture or cultural artefacts but for the forest itself. Logging has been prohibited here since 841 CE, making this one of the longest-standing conservation areas on Earth.

The result of eleven centuries without cutting is a forest of extraordinary maturity. The canopy rises 30 to 40 metres above the forest floor, supported by massive trunks of Japanese cedar (sugi), camphor (kusunoki), Japanese cypress (hinoki), and numerous broadleaf species. The understory is dense with ferns, mosses, and shade-tolerant shrubs. The atmosphere inside the forest is noticeably different from the open park — cooler, darker, with a distinctive humidity and the sound of water moving through unseen channels.

### Walking the Forest

The Kasugayama Primeval Forest Yuen walking trail (approximately 9 kilometres) circles through the forest from behind Kasuga Taisha. The trail is moderate in difficulty and takes 3–4 hours. Along the way, you pass trees of extraordinary size and age, many unmarked and unnamed — they are simply part of the continuous forest, growing as they have for centuries.

**Notable trees on the trail**:
- Several massive Japanese cedars with trunk circumferences exceeding 6 metres
- Ancient camphor trees whose root systems have merged with the rocky terrain
- Giant Japanese zelkova (keyaki) with spreading crowns visible from the trail
- Ancient chinquapin trees (shii) whose dense, evergreen canopy defines the forest's character

**Best time**: Early morning, when light filters through the canopy at low angles and mist often lingers in the hollows. Autumn and spring offer the most comfortable walking temperatures.

## The Camphor Trees of Nara Park

If the primeval forest represents wild, untouched tree life, the camphor trees of Nara Park represent something different — ancient trees that have grown in the presence of humans, shaped by the grazing of deer and the activities of temples, yet achieving a grandeur that rivals any wild forest giant.

Camphor trees (Cinnamomum camphora) are evergreen, and in Nara they reach sizes that are difficult to comprehend until you stand beside them. The largest specimens in the park have trunk circumferences of 8 to 10 metres and canopy spreads of 30 metres or more. Their bark is deeply fissured, their branches contorted into shapes that suggest centuries of wind, growth, and patient endurance. In summer, they provide vast pools of shade where deer rest and visitors shelter from the heat.

### Where to Find the Largest Camphor Trees

**Around Todaiji**: The approach to the Great Buddha Hall passes several enormous camphor trees. The tree near the Kagamiike (Mirror Pond) is particularly impressive — its canopy extends over a substantial portion of the pond's edge.

**Tobihino Field**: The open meadow south of Kasuga Taisha is dotted with camphor trees that grow in isolation, their full canopy shapes visible. These trees, standing alone in deer-grazed grassland, have an architectural quality — each one is a distinct form against the sky.

**Nandaimon Gate area**: Several large camphor trees stand near the Great South Gate of Todaiji. Their roots have partially lifted the stone paving around them, a visible demonstration of the slow power of tree growth over centuries.

## The Cryptomeria Avenues

Japanese cedar (Cryptomeria japonica, known as sugi) is the tree that defines temple approaches throughout Japan, and Nara has some of the finest cryptomeria avenues in the country. These trees grow tall, straight, and relatively narrow, creating living corridors that channel the eye and the spirit toward the sacred site ahead.

### Kasuga Taisha Approach

The main approach to Kasuga Taisha passes through a corridor of mature cryptomeria, many of them 200–300 years old. Combined with the stone lanterns that line the path, the effect is of walking through a space that is simultaneously natural and designed — the trees providing the vertical dimension, the lanterns the human scale.

### Saidaiji to Akishino-dera

Less known but equally beautiful, the approach to Akishino-dera temple passes through a grove of tall cryptomeria that filters the light to a green-gold tone on sunny afternoons.

## Wisteria Trees at Kasuga Taisha

The ancient wisteria (fuji) growing within the Kasuga Taisha compound are not large in the conventional sense — wisteria is a vine, not a tree — but their age and cultural significance place them among Nara's most important plant specimens. The Kasuga wisteria have been cultivated for centuries, and some specimens are believed to be over 200 years old. When they bloom in late April to early May, their cascading purple flower clusters are among the most celebrated floral displays in Nara.

## The Nara Park Zelkova

Japanese zelkova (Zelkova serrata, known as keyaki) is one of the most beautiful deciduous trees in Japan, prized for its vase-shaped canopy and autumn colour. Several magnificent specimens grow in Nara Park, particularly along the road between Kofukuji and Todaiji. In autumn, these trees turn to shades of orange, copper, and russet that glow in late afternoon light.

## Cherry Trees: The Ornamental Forest

Nara's cherry trees — approximately 1,700 in the park alone — are younger than the camphor trees and cedars, but they form an integral part of the urban forest. Most are Somei Yoshino, the cultivated variety planted across Japan in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, but older specimens of Yamazakura (mountain cherry) and several other species can be found. The cherry trees are notable not just for their spring blossoms but for their year-round presence — their dark bark, their branching patterns visible in winter, their green canopy in summer.

## Reading Nara's Trees: What to Look For

### Trunk Circumference

In Japan, tree age is often estimated from trunk circumference. As a rough guide, a camphor tree with a circumference of 5 metres is likely 300–400 years old; one exceeding 8 metres may be over 500 years old. Cryptomeria grow more slowly in girth — a 3-metre circumference may represent 200–300 years of growth.

### Root Systems

The exposed root systems of Nara's ancient trees are works of art in themselves. Look for the massive buttress roots of camphor trees, which spread across the ground like frozen rivers, and the raised root plates of cedars growing on slopes.

### Bark Textures

Each species has a distinctive bark. Camphor bark is deeply fissured and rough. Cryptomeria bark peels in long, fibrous strips. Cherry bark is smooth and marked with horizontal lenticels. Zelkova bark flakes to reveal orange inner layers. Learning to read bark is the first step in tree identification.

### Crown Shape

The shape of a tree's crown tells its history. Trees that grew in open space — like the isolated camphor trees of Tobihino Field — have broad, symmetrical crowns. Trees that grew in forest competition — like the cedars of Kasugayama — are tall and narrow, having invested their energy in upward growth toward light.

## Seasonal Tree Highlights

**Spring (March–May)**: Cherry blossom season dominates, but also watch for the fresh green leaves of deciduous trees emerging — the effect known as shinryoku (new green).

**Summer (June–August)**: The camphor trees are at their most impressive, providing vast shade canopies. The primeval forest is at its most atmospheric, dense and humid.

**Autumn (October–December)**: Zelkova, maple, and cherry trees turn colour. The contrast between the turning deciduous trees and the evergreen camphor and cryptomeria is striking.

**Winter (December–February)**: The architecture of deciduous trees — their branching patterns, trunk forms, and bark textures — is fully revealed. This is the best season for appreciating tree structure.

## Where to Stay

Kanoya ryokan sits within the sphere of Nara's great trees — close enough to the park that the camphor canopy is visible, close enough to Kasugayama that a morning walk to the primeval forest is an easy undertaking. The traditional Japanese relationship with trees — as living presences rather than mere resources — is reflected in the care given to the ryokan's own garden, where trees are cultivated not for display but for the quality of shade, sound, and seasonal change they bring to the space.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How old are the oldest trees in Nara?

The oldest trees in Kasugayama Primeval Forest are estimated at 800–1,000 years old. Some camphor trees in Nara Park may be 400–600 years old. Exact ages are difficult to determine without core sampling, which is generally not performed on protected trees.

### Can I walk through Kasugayama Primeval Forest?

Yes. The Kasugayama Yuen walking trail is open to the public and takes approximately 3–4 hours to complete. It begins behind Kasuga Taisha shrine. No guide is required, but the trail is not lit and should be walked during daylight hours.

### What is the biggest tree in Nara?

The largest trees by trunk circumference are the camphor trees in Nara Park, several of which exceed 8 metres around the trunk. The tallest trees are the cryptomeria cedars in Kasugayama Forest, some exceeding 40 metres in height.

### Why was Kasugayama Forest protected?

The forest was designated as sacred by the Kasuga Taisha shrine in 841 CE, and logging was forbidden. The forest was considered the dwelling place of the shrine's deities. This religious prohibition has been continuously maintained for over 1,100 years.

### Is there a guided tree walk in Nara?

Formal guided tree walks are occasionally organised by the Nara Park management office and local nature groups. Ask at the Nara tourist information centre for current offerings. Self-guided walks using this article's information are possible at any time.
