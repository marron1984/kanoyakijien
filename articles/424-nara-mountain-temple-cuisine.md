# Mountain Temple Cuisine: Eating at Nara's Highland Temples

In the mountains south and east of Nara city, Buddhist temples have been feeding monks, pilgrims, and travellers for over a thousand years. The food they serve — shojin ryori, the vegetarian cuisine of Japanese Buddhism — is not a modern wellness trend or a dietary restriction reluctantly accommodated. It is a culinary tradition as refined, seasonal, and technically demanding as any in Japan, developed over centuries by cooks who believed that preparing food was itself a form of spiritual practice.

Nara Prefecture's mountain temples occupy a particular place in this tradition. The Shugendo ascetic practices of Yoshino and Omine, the ancient monasteries of the Katsuragi range, and the highland retreats scattered through the Yamato Alps all developed distinctive approaches to cooking that reflect their geography, climate, and spiritual orientation. Eating at these temples is not just a meal — it is an encounter with a philosophy of sustenance that predates modern cuisine by a millennium.

## What Is Shojin Ryori?

Shojin ryori (精進料理) translates literally as "devotion cuisine" — food prepared with the same diligence and mindfulness applied to religious practice. The term shojin means "to advance with vigour toward enlightenment," and the cuisine is understood as a support to that advancement.

The rules are specific:

**No meat, no fish**: All animal products are excluded. No meat, poultry, fish, or shellfish. Eggs and dairy are also traditionally absent, though some modern interpretations are more flexible.

**No pungent vegetables**: The five "forbidden" pungent plants (gokun) — garlic, green onion, chives, leeks, and rakkyo (Japanese shallot) — are excluded from strict shojin ryori. These are believed to inflame the passions and disturb meditation. In practice, many temple kitchens now use some of these ingredients in milder forms.

**Seasonal and local**: Ingredients are drawn from what the mountain provides in each season — wild vegetables (sansai), mushrooms, roots, nuts, seeds, tofu, and preserved foods.

**No waste**: Every part of every ingredient is used. Vegetable trimmings become stock. Soybean pulp (okara) becomes a side dish. This principle — mottainai, the avoidance of waste — is both ecological and spiritual.

## The Mountain Pantry

Temple cooks in Nara's highlands work with an ingredient palette that reflects the mountain environment:

### Wild Vegetables (Sansai)

Spring is the peak season for sansai, and Nara's mountains produce an exceptional variety:

**Warabi (bracken fern)**: Young fiddlehead fronds, simmered in dashi and soy sauce or served as tempura. Requires careful preparation to remove bitterness.

**Zenmai (royal fern)**: Similar to warabi but with a denser, more resilient texture. Often dried for year-round use.

**Taranome (angelica tree shoots)**: The tender buds of the Japanese angelica tree, prized for their slightly bitter, aromatic flavour. Classic tempura ingredient.

**Fuki (giant butterbur)**: The stems are simmered; the flower buds (fukinoto) are served as tempura in earliest spring — one of the first harbingers of the season.

**Udo (Japanese spikenard)**: A unique vegetable with a crisp, juicy stem and a slightly resinous flavour. Served raw, blanched, or as tempura.

### Tofu and Soy Products

Tofu is the protein backbone of shojin ryori, and mountain temples serve it in forms rarely seen elsewhere:

**Goma-dofu (sesame tofu)**: Not actually tofu — it is made from ground sesame seeds and kuzu (arrowroot starch), cooked to a silky, pudding-like consistency. The texture is extraordinary: dense, smooth, and faintly nutty. This is the signature dish of mountain temple cuisine, and Yoshino's kuzu starch makes the local version particularly fine.

**Koya-dofu (freeze-dried tofu)**: Tofu that has been frozen, thawed, and dried, creating a spongy texture that absorbs cooking liquids. Named for Mount Koya but used throughout the mountain temple tradition. Simmered in sweetened dashi, it becomes tender and flavourful.

**Yuba (tofu skin)**: The thin film that forms on the surface of heated soy milk, lifted off and served fresh, dried, or wrapped around other ingredients. Delicate, creamy, and rich in protein.

### Kuzu (Japanese Arrowroot)

Nara's Yoshino region is Japan's premier source of kuzu starch, a refined white powder made from the root of the kudzu vine. In temple cuisine, kuzu serves as a thickener, a binding agent, and the base for goma-dofu and kuzu-kiri (transparent noodles). It also appears in medicinal preparations — dissolved in hot water with ginger, it is a traditional remedy for colds and digestive distress.

The production of hon-kuzu (genuine kuzu starch) is laborious — the roots are crushed, washed, and the starch settled and refined over weeks in cold mountain water. The result is a starch of remarkable purity, entirely different from the cheaper alternatives often sold under the same name.

### Preserved Foods

Mountain temples cannot rely on fresh ingredients year-round. The traditional preservation methods — drying, salting, pickling, and smoking — are themselves culinary techniques:

**Dried shiitake**: Concentrated umami. Dried mushrooms are reconstituted in water, and the soaking liquid becomes a rich stock.

**Pickled vegetables**: Temple pickles follow seasonal rhythms — spring greens pickled in salt, summer cucumbers in miso, autumn daikon in rice bran (nuka).

**Dried vegetables and beans**: Kiriboshi daikon (dried shredded daikon), kanpyo (dried gourd strips), various dried beans — the pantry of preservation that sustains temple kitchens through winter.

## Where to Eat Mountain Temple Cuisine in Nara

### Yoshino Mountain (Mount Yoshino)

Yoshino is the most accessible location for mountain temple food in Nara Prefecture. Several temples and shukubo (temple lodgings) serve shojin ryori:

**Chikurin-in**: A temple lodging with a celebrated garden attributed to Sen no Rikyu. The shojin ryori meal served to overnight guests is multi-course, seasonal, and beautifully presented. Advance reservation required.

**Sakuramoto-bo**: Another Yoshino shukubo offering overnight stays with evening and morning temple meals. The food is simpler than Chikurin-in but authentic and satisfying.

**Yoshino restaurants**: Several restaurants along the main approach to Yoshino serve kuzu-based dishes — kuzu-kiri noodles, kuzu mochi, and goma-dofu — in settings that, during cherry blossom season, overlook one of Japan's most famous flower landscapes.

### Hase-dera Area

Hase-dera, east of Nara city, sits in the mountains of eastern Nara Prefecture. The area around the temple includes small restaurants serving vegetarian and semi-vegetarian mountain cuisine, often featuring local somen noodles (Miwa somen, from nearby Sakurai) alongside mountain vegetables.

### Muroji Area

Muroji, the "women's Koya," is a mountain temple deep in the forested hills of eastern Nara Prefecture. While formal shojin ryori meals require advance arrangement, the area's small restaurants serve rustic mountain food — sansai tempura, tofu dishes, mushroom rice — that reflects the temple cooking tradition in a more casual format.

### Katsuragi Mountains (Western Nara)

The temples along the Katsuragi range, including Taima-dera, have connections to mountain asceticism and vegetarian cooking. Restaurants near Taima-dera serve local specialties including kuzu dishes and sansai sets.

## The Meal Structure

A formal shojin ryori meal at a mountain temple follows a multi-course format:

**Saki-zuke (appetiser)**: A small seasonal offering — perhaps a piece of goma-dofu with wasabi, or a sansai preparation.

**Nimono-wan (simmered dish)**: Vegetables simmered in kombu-based dashi. The soup is delicate, relying entirely on vegetable umami.

**Yakimono (grilled dish)**: Grilled tofu, roasted vegetables, or a preparation using fu (wheat gluten).

**Agemono (fried dish)**: Tempura of seasonal vegetables, particularly sansai in spring and mushrooms in autumn.

**Sunomono or aemono (dressed dish)**: Vegetables dressed in vinegar, miso, or sesame.

**Gohan (rice)**: Mountain rice, sometimes cooked with seasonal ingredients (bamboo shoots, chestnuts, mushrooms).

**Tome-wan (final soup)**: A simple miso soup to close the savoury portion.

**Pickles**: Temple-made pickles, usually three varieties.

**Dessert**: Seasonal fruit or a kuzu-based sweet.

The meal is served on lacquerware or pottery, often in a private room. Silence or quiet conversation is appropriate — the meal is understood as an extension of temple practice.

## The Philosophy of Temple Cooking

The Tenzo Kyokun, a 13th-century text by the Zen master Dogen, establishes the principles of temple cooking that remain influential today. Dogen argued that the cook (tenzo) holds one of the most important positions in a monastery, and that cooking should be performed with the same attention and care as meditation or sutra study.

Key principles include:

**Sanshi no kokoro (three minds)**: The cook should maintain a joyful mind (kishin), a nurturing mind (roshin), and a magnanimous mind (daishin).

**Equal respect for all ingredients**: A radish leaf deserves the same care as a rare mushroom. Nothing is inherently superior or inferior.

**Attention without attachment**: Cook with full attention but without ego — the food is not for personal expression but for the nourishment of the community.

These principles produce a cuisine that is technically refined but never showy — food that satisfies without excess, that nourishes without indulgence.

## Planning Your Visit

**Best season**: Spring (April-May) for sansai and cherry blossoms in Yoshino. Autumn (October-November) for mushrooms and foliage. Winter for the austere beauty of mountain temples in snow.

**Reservations**: Shukubo stays and formal shojin ryori meals require advance booking, often several weeks ahead. Some restaurants accept walk-in guests for lunch sets.

**Access**: Yoshino is reached by Kintetsu Railway from Nara or Osaka (approximately 90 minutes from Nara). Hase-dera and Muroji require train plus bus or car. A day trip from Nara city is feasible for any of these destinations.

**Dietary notes**: Shojin ryori is naturally vegan. However, some preparations may use dashi with katsuobushi (bonito) — if strict veganism matters, confirm when booking.

Visitors using Nara city as their base — staying, for example, at Kanoya ryokan in Naramachi — can easily combine a morning of city temples with an afternoon mountain excursion for temple cuisine, returning in the evening to the city's comforts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need to be Buddhist to eat at a temple?

No. Temple cuisine is served to all visitors regardless of faith. At shukubo, you may be invited to join morning prayers, but this is optional.

### Is shojin ryori filling enough?

Yes. The combination of rice, tofu, vegetables, and multiple courses provides a satisfying, complete meal. The portions are moderate but numerous.

### Can I visit mountain temples as a day trip from Nara city?

Yes. Yoshino, Hase-dera, and Muroji are all accessible as day trips by train and/or bus. Allow a full day for a relaxed visit including a meal.

### What is goma-dofu?

Goma-dofu is a dish made from ground sesame seeds and kuzu starch, cooked into a smooth, dense block. Despite the name, it contains no soy. It is the signature dish of mountain temple cuisine and worth trying at any opportunity.
