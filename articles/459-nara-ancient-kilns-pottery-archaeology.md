# Nara's Ancient Kilns: Pottery Archaeological Sites

Beneath the surface of Nara Prefecture lies a ceramic history that predates the city's founding as Japan's capital by centuries. The hills surrounding the Yamato Plain contain the remains of ancient kilns — anagama tunnel kilns and noborigama climbing kilns — where potters fired the ceramics that served Japan's earliest states. These archaeological sites, some excavated and open to visitors, others visible only as subtle contours in forested hillsides, document a technological and artistic evolution spanning more than 1,500 years. For visitors with an interest in ceramics, archaeology, or the material foundations of Japanese civilization, Nara's kiln sites offer an experience that no museum can replicate: the encounter with the actual places where fire transformed earth into culture.

## The Ceramic Timeline

### Jōmon and Yayoi Foundations

Japan's ceramic tradition is among the oldest in the world. Jōmon pottery, dating to approximately 14,000 BCE, represents some of the earliest fired ceramics produced anywhere. By the Yayoi period (roughly 300 BCE to 300 CE), potters in the Yamato region were producing functional vessels — storage jars, cooking pots, ritual vessels — using techniques that had evolved over millennia. These early wares were fired at relatively low temperatures in open pits or simple kilns, producing pottery that was porous, earthen-colored, and functional rather than decorative.

The Nara area has yielded Yayoi-period ceramic finds at numerous sites, confirming that pottery production was established in the region long before it became the political center of Japan.

### Sueki: The Continental Revolution

The most important technological leap in Japanese ceramic history occurred in the fifth century CE, when Korean potters brought the sueki tradition to Japan. Sueki represented a fundamentally different approach to ceramics. Fired in reduction atmospheres at temperatures exceeding 1,100 degrees Celsius — far higher than any previous Japanese pottery — sueki was hard, thin-walled, and often grey or blue-grey in color. It was shaped on a potter's wheel, another continental introduction, and fired in anagama tunnel kilns built into hillsides, where the natural updraft created the sustained high temperatures required.

The introduction of sueki kilns to the Nara region was transformative. The Yamato rulers, who were consolidating political control over the Japanese archipelago during this period, needed ceramic vessels for ritual offerings, storage, and trade. Sueki provided all of these, and the kilns that produced it became strategic assets.

### The Nara Period: Imperial Ceramic Production

When Nara became Japan's permanent capital in 710 CE, the demand for ceramics intensified dramatically. The new capital required enormous quantities of roof tiles for its temples, palaces, and government buildings. It needed tableware for the court, storage vessels for the treasury, and ritual ceramics for the great Buddhist temples then under construction. This demand stimulated a period of kiln-building and ceramic experimentation that produced some of Japan's most significant early pottery.

## Key Kiln Sites in the Nara Region

### The Suemura Kiln Complex (Osaka-Nara Border)

The Suemura kiln complex, located in what is now the city of Sakai in southern Osaka Prefecture but historically connected to the Yamato political sphere, was one of the largest sueki production centers in ancient Japan. Active from the fifth through the tenth century, the complex contained hundreds of individual kilns spread across the Izumi Hills. While technically in Osaka, Suemura's production was intimately linked to the Nara capital's needs, and its ceramics are found in abundance at Nara-period archaeological sites.

The Osaka Prefectural Chikatsu Asuka Museum displays finds from the Suemura kilns and provides context for understanding the scale and sophistication of the operation. For visitors based in Nara, the museum is accessible as a day trip and offers a powerful introduction to ancient Japanese ceramic production.

### Nara Hills Kiln Sites

The hills immediately east and south of Nara city contain numerous kiln sites dating from the sixth through eighth centuries. These kilns produced sueki vessels and, crucially, the roof tiles (kawara) needed for the capital's ambitious building program. The construction of Todai-ji alone required millions of individual roof tiles, and the kilns that supplied them left archaeological traces that have been partially excavated.

Some of these sites are accessible with local knowledge or guided tours. The Nara Cultural Properties Research Institute periodically conducts public excavation events and lectures that provide guided access to active archaeological sites.

### Heguri and Oji Kiln Sites

The towns of Heguri and Oji, west of Nara city along the base of the Ikoma mountain range, contain kiln sites that produced sueki and early glazed ceramics. The Heguri kilns are particularly notable for their production of green-glazed ceramics influenced by Chinese Tang dynasty prototypes — an early step toward the Nara sansai tradition.

### The Minami-Yamashiro Kiln Region

South of Nara, in the Minami-Yamashiro area (now part of southern Kyoto Prefecture but historically part of the greater Nara cultural sphere), extensive kiln remains document production that served both the Nara and early Kyoto capitals. These kilns demonstrate the transition from sueki to the medieval Japanese ceramic traditions that would eventually produce the famous Seto, Tokoname, and Bizen wares.

## Nara Sansai: Three-Color Glazed Ware

### Continental Inspiration

One of the most distinctive ceramic products of the Nara period is Nara sansai — three-color glazed ware inspired by the Tang Chinese sancai tradition. These ceramics, glazed with lead-based formulas that produced green, amber, and cream/white colors, represent the most sophisticated glazing technology available in eighth-century Japan. The glazes were applied to pre-fired sueki bodies, and the pieces were then fired a second time at lower temperatures to melt the glaze coatings.

Nara sansai vessels were luxury items, produced for the court and major temples. Examples preserved in the Shōsō-in treasury and excavated from Nara-period sites show a range of forms including offering dishes, incense burners, and decorative vessels. The glazes were applied in controlled patterns — sometimes dripped, sometimes brushed, sometimes separated by incised lines that prevented the colors from running together.

### Archaeological Evidence

Kiln sites producing Nara sansai have been identified at several locations in the Nara area. The discovery of kiln furniture (the stilts, setters, and spacers used to separate pieces during firing), glaze waste, and waster shards (defective pieces discarded by the potters) at these sites confirms local production rather than Chinese import. This archaeological evidence is significant because it demonstrates that Japanese potters had mastered sophisticated glaze chemistry by the eighth century — a technical achievement that would influence all subsequent Japanese ceramic development.

## Roof Tile Production

### The Scale of Demand

The Nara capital's construction program created a demand for roof tiles that is difficult to overstate. The Heijō Palace complex, the great temples, and the residential districts of the aristocracy all required tiled roofs, and the production of these tiles was an industrial operation by any standard. Archaeologists have estimated that the construction of Todai-ji required approximately 100,000 tiles for the Great Buddha Hall alone, and the entire temple complex consumed many times that number.

### Tile Kilns and Their Remains

Tile-producing kilns were among the largest in the Nara region. These anagama kilns were built to accommodate the flat, heavy forms of roof tiles, which required different stacking and firing techniques than vessels. The remains of tile kilns are often identifiable by the presence of broken tile fragments — some bearing decorative end-cap designs (onigawara and nokihiragawara) — scattered in the surrounding soil.

The Nara Palace Site Museum (Heijō-kyū Museum) displays roof tiles recovered from the palace site, including decorated end-cap tiles bearing lotus, demon-face, and floral motifs. These tiles demonstrate that even the most utilitarian ceramic products of the Nara period were produced with aesthetic attention.

## Visiting Kiln Sites: Practical Information

### What to Expect

Ancient kiln sites in the Nara area range from fully excavated and interpreted sites with signage and visitor facilities to unexcavated remains that are essentially unmarked locations in forested hillsides. Even at developed sites, what you see is typically a ground-level view of the kiln's outline — the firing chamber, the flue, and sometimes the stoking area — represented by stone foundations, discolored soil, and descriptive panels.

The experience requires imagination to transform an excavated trench into a mental image of a working kiln. But for those who bring that engagement, kiln sites offer something that finished ceramics in a museum case cannot: the encounter with the production context and the physical reality of ancient craft.

### Museums That Contextualize the Sites

**The Nara National Museum** houses exceptional examples of Nara-period ceramics, including sueki, Nara sansai, and roof tiles, displayed with archaeological context.

**The Nara Palace Site Museum** focuses on the Heijō Palace and its material culture, including ceramics excavated from the palace grounds.

**The Nara Prefectural Museum of Art** occasionally mounts exhibitions on regional craft traditions with archaeological components.

For visitors staying in central Nara, combining a museum visit with a trip to a kiln site creates a layered experience — seeing the finished product first, then visiting the place where it was made. Guests at Kanoya ryokan can plan such itineraries with ease, as the Nara National Museum and the Palace Site Museum are both accessible by walking or short bus ride, and excursions to outlying kiln sites can fill a rewarding half-day.

### Guided Options

The Nara Cultural Properties Research Institute and local volunteer guide groups occasionally offer guided walks to kiln sites, particularly during archaeological awareness events. These guided visits provide expertise that transforms an otherwise opaque landscape into readable archaeological narrative. Checking with the tourist information center at Kintetsu Nara Station for scheduled events is worthwhile.

## The Continuity of Clay

Nara's ancient kilns are not merely archaeological curiosities. They are the foundational layer of a ceramic tradition that continues to this day in the Akahada-yaki workshops of the Nara hills, in the contemporary studio potters who draw on historical glazing techniques, and in the museum collections that preserve the finest achievements of 1,500 years of firing and forming. Walking the hillsides where ancient kilns once burned, picking up a shard of sueki from the path, and feeling the thinness and hardness of a ceramic fired over a millennium ago is an experience that connects the hand to history with an immediacy that no photograph or text can achieve.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What types of pottery were made in ancient Nara?
Ancient Nara's kilns produced sueki (high-fired grey stoneware), Nara sansai (three-color glazed ware), roof tiles for temples and palaces, and various utilitarian vessels. Production ranged from the fifth century through the ninth century and beyond.

### What is Nara sansai?
Nara sansai is a three-color glazed ceramic tradition from the eighth century, inspired by Chinese Tang dynasty sancai ware. The lead-based glazes produced green, amber, and cream colors on pre-fired stoneware bodies, and the pieces were luxury items for the imperial court and major temples.

### Can I visit ancient kiln sites in Nara?
*Yes, several ancient kiln sites in the Nara area are accessible to visitors, ranging from fully excavated and interpreted sites to unexcavated hillside locations. The Nara Palace Site Museum and Nara National Museum provide context, and the Nara Cultural Properties Research Institute occasionally offers guided archaeological walks to kiln sites.*

### What is sueki pottery?
Sueki is a high-fired grey stoneware introduced to Japan from the Korean Peninsula in the fifth century. Fired at temperatures exceeding 1,100 degrees Celsius in anagama tunnel kilns, sueki was harder and thinner than any previous Japanese pottery and represented a major technological advancement.

### Where can I see Nara-period ceramics in a museum?
The Nara National Museum, the Nara Palace Site Museum (Heijō-kyū Museum), and the annual Shōsō-in Exhibition all display significant collections of Nara-period ceramics with archaeological context and interpretive information.
