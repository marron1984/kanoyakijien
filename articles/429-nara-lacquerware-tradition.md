# Nara's Lacquerware Tradition: From Temple to Table

The Shosoin treasure repository at Todai-ji contains lacquered objects from the 8th century — musical instruments, boxes, furniture, vessels — that remain lustrous and intact after more than twelve centuries. Their survival is not accidental. Urushi, the natural lacquer made from the sap of the Japanese lacquer tree (Toxicodendron vernicifluum), is one of the most durable coatings known. Properly applied, it resists water, heat, acid, and decay. It is also, when polished, extraordinarily beautiful — a depth of colour and surface quality that synthetic alternatives have never matched.

Nara's relationship with lacquerware began when the city was Japan's capital (710-784) and the imperial court demanded the finest craft production. That relationship continues today in workshops, studios, and shops where Nara's lacquerware artisans maintain a tradition that connects the Shosoin's ancient masterpieces to the bowls and trays on contemporary dining tables.

## What Is Urushi?

Urushi is the refined sap of the lacquer tree, a species native to East Asia. The sap is collected by making small incisions in the bark of living trees — a process similar to rubber tapping — and gathered in small quantities over the summer months. A single tree yields only about 200 grams of sap in its lifetime before it must be felled.

Raw urushi is a grey-brown liquid that hardens not by drying (evaporation) but by polymerisation — a chemical reaction triggered by humidity and the enzyme laccase in the sap. This means that urushi cures in moist conditions, the opposite of most paints and varnishes. The cured film is a natural polymer of extraordinary durability.

The properties of cured urushi:

- **Waterproof**: Completely impervious to water
- **Heat resistant**: Withstands temperatures up to approximately 300°C
- **Chemical resistant**: Resists acids, alkalis, and alcohol
- **Antibacterial**: The surface inhibits bacterial growth
- **Beautiful**: Produces a deep, warm lustre that improves with use and age

These properties made urushi the ideal material for protecting and beautifying objects in Japan's humid climate, and its use extends from everyday tableware to the most precious temple treasures.

**Caution**: Raw urushi causes contact dermatitis (a rash similar to poison ivy) in most people. Fully cured urushi is safe to touch and eat from. Objects in shops and museums are fully cured and present no risk.

## Nara's Lacquerware History

### The Nara Period (710-784)

When the capital was established at Heijo-kyo (Nara), the court imported lacquerware techniques from Tang Dynasty China and Korea while also developing distinctly Japanese approaches. The Nara government established craft workshops (Tsukumo-tsukasa) that employed lacquer artisans alongside metalworkers, textile weavers, and other specialists.

The Shosoin collection — preserved at Todai-ji since 756 — contains the finest surviving examples of Nara-period lacquerwork:

**Raden (mother-of-pearl inlay)**: Shells cut into decorative shapes and set into the lacquer surface. The Shosoin's raden pieces feature intricate designs of birds, flowers, and geometric patterns with a luminous iridescence that remains vivid.

**Maki-e (sprinkled picture)**: Gold or silver powder sprinkled onto wet lacquer to create designs. This technique, which became the most celebrated of all Japanese lacquer arts, was refined during and after the Nara period.

**Kingin-e (gold and silver painting)**: Fine lines of gold and silver painted onto the lacquer surface, used for detailed pictorial designs.

**Rankaku (eggshell inlay)**: Crushed eggshell pressed into lacquer to create a crackled white surface, sometimes used as a ground for further decoration.

These techniques demonstrate a level of sophistication that required generations of accumulated skill — evidence that lacquerwork in Japan was already highly developed before the Nara period began.

### Medieval and Edo Periods

After the capital moved to Kyoto, Nara's lacquerware production continued to serve the city's powerful temples and shrines. Temple furnishings — offering tables, sutra boxes, shrine fittings, Buddhist statuary — required expert lacquerwork for both protection and beautification.

During the Edo period (1603-1868), Nara lacquerware production expanded to include everyday items — tableware, trays, boxes, containers — for the merchant and townsman classes. This democratisation of lacquerware created the market that sustains Nara's craft today.

### Nara Shikki: The Local Style

Nara lacquerware (Nara shikki) is characterised by several distinctive features:

**Nara-nuri**: A plain, undecorated lacquer finish that emphasises the quality and depth of the urushi itself rather than surface decoration. Colours are typically vermillion (shu-nuri), black (kuro-nuri), or the natural transparent amber of ki-urushi. The beauty is in the material and the form, not in applied ornament.

**Shosoin patterns**: Some Nara artisans specialise in reproducing or adapting the decorative patterns found in the Shosoin collection — raden, maki-e, and geometric designs derived from the Silk Road aesthetic of the 8th century. These pieces carry the visual DNA of Nara's golden age.

**Temple-quality work**: The ongoing relationship between lacquer workshops and Nara's temples maintains standards of craftsmanship that might otherwise erode. When Kasuga Taisha or Kofuku-ji requires lacquerwork for restoration or new commissions, the work is done by local artisans to exacting historical standards.

## The Making of a Lacquer Bowl

A single lacquered bowl passes through numerous stages over several weeks or months:

**1. Wood turning (kiji-shi)**: The base form is turned on a lathe from a block of seasoned wood — typically zelkova (keyaki), horse chestnut (tochinoki), or Japanese cypress (hinoki). The turner shapes the exterior and interior, leaving the walls thin but uniform.

**2. Drying and preparation**: The raw wooden form is dried slowly to stabilise the wood and prevent later warping. Imperfections are filled with a paste of urushi mixed with rice flour and powdered clay (kokuso).

**3. Foundation coating (shitaji)**: Multiple coats of raw urushi mixed with various powders are applied and dried between each coat. Each layer is sanded smooth before the next is applied. This process builds a stable, uniform foundation for the finish coats.

**4. Middle coating (naka-nuri)**: Refined urushi is applied in thin, even coats using a wide, flat brush. Again, multiple coats with drying and sanding between each.

**5. Finish coating (uwa-nuri)**: The final coats of the highest-quality urushi, applied in a dust-free room with controlled humidity. The finish coat determines the final colour, depth, and lustre of the piece.

**6. Polishing (roiro)**: For the highest-quality work, the cured surface is polished with charcoal and increasingly fine abrasives, then hand-polished with deer-horn powder and the artisan's palm until it achieves a mirror-like depth.

A single bowl may receive 20 to 30 individual coats, with drying time between each. The total production time for a high-quality piece can exceed three months.

## Where to See Lacquerware in Nara

### Shosoin Treasures (Annual Exhibition)

Each autumn (late October to mid-November), the Nara National Museum holds the annual Shosoin Exhibition, displaying a rotating selection of treasures from the Todai-ji repository. Lacquerware is always prominently featured. This is the best opportunity to see 8th-century lacquerwork in person — the depth, colour, and condition of these pieces after twelve centuries is astonishing.

### Nara National Museum

The permanent collection includes lacquerwork from various periods, displayed alongside other Buddhist art. The museum provides excellent context for understanding lacquerware's role in temple culture.

### Todai-ji Museum

Houses objects associated with the temple's history, including lacquered Buddhist implements and ceremonial objects.

### Naramachi Workshops and Shops

Several lacquerware workshops and shops in the old merchant quarter sell both traditional and contemporary Nara shikki:

**Ikegami Lacquerware**: A long-established Naramachi workshop producing Nara-nuri tableware and decorative objects. The shop displays the production process and sells directly from the maker.

**Craft shops along Naramachi streets**: Multiple shops stock lacquerware alongside other Nara crafts. Quality varies — look for pieces described as hon-urushi (genuine urushi) rather than synthetic lacquer imitations.

### Nara Prefectural Crafts Centre

Displays and sells work by certified Nara artisans, including lacquerware. Staff can explain techniques and assist with selection.

## Buying Lacquerware: What to Know

**Genuine urushi vs. synthetic**: Authentic urushi lacquerware is expensive because the material is scarce and the labour is intensive. Synthetic lacquer (cashew-based or polyurethane) imitates the appearance but not the durability, feel, or depth of real urushi. Always ask whether a piece is hon-urushi (genuine lacquer) or not. Prices for genuine urushi bowls typically start at ¥5,000-8,000 and can exceed ¥50,000 for decorated pieces.

**Use it**: Lacquerware is meant to be used, not displayed behind glass. Eating from a genuine urushi bowl is a sensory pleasure — the material is warm to the touch, light in the hand, and improves with years of use as the surface develops a deeper lustre.

**Care**: Hand wash only (no dishwasher). Avoid prolonged exposure to direct sunlight, which degrades the lacquer over time. Do not use abrasive cleaners. Dry with a soft cloth.

**Mending**: Cracked or chipped lacquerware can be repaired using traditional kintsugi (gold repair) or urushi-based mending techniques. Repair is part of the tradition — well-mended pieces are considered more beautiful and valuable than unmarked ones.

Guests at Kanoya ryokan may encounter lacquerware directly — traditional ryokan in Nara serve meals on lacquered trays and in lacquered bowls, bringing the craft from museum to table in the most natural way possible.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is lacquerware safe to eat from?

Yes. Fully cured urushi is food-safe, antibacterial, and has been used for tableware in Japan for thousands of years. Only raw, uncured urushi causes skin irritation.

### How long does genuine lacquerware last?

With proper care, indefinitely. The Shosoin collection demonstrates survival of over 1,250 years. Household lacquerware typically lasts generations.

### What is the difference between Nara lacquerware and Wajima or other regional styles?

Nara lacquerware (Nara shikki) tends toward elegant simplicity — plain finishes that showcase the urushi itself, plus Shosoin-influenced decorative traditions. Wajima lacquerware (from Ishikawa Prefecture) is known for extreme durability and elaborate decoration. Each region has developed distinctive techniques and aesthetics over centuries.

### Can I watch lacquerware being made?

Some Naramachi workshops allow visitors to observe. Check with individual workshops for schedule and availability. The Nara Prefectural Crafts Centre occasionally hosts demonstrations.
