# Hydrangea Season in Nara: June's Forgotten Flower Festival

June in Japan is the month of rain. The tsuyu — the monsoon season — arrives in the Kansai region around the second week of June and persists until mid-July, bringing warm, persistent rain that saturates the landscape and discourages many visitors from travelling. Tourism statistics drop. Guidebooks suggest alternative plans. And yet June is when Nara produces one of its most beautiful and least celebrated floral displays: the blooming of the hydrangeas.

Hydrangeas (ajisai in Japanese) are the flowers of the rainy season. This is not coincidence but biology — they thrive in exactly the wet, humid conditions that June provides. Their heavy, globular flower heads, which range from deep blue through purple, pink, and white, seem designed for overcast skies and soft rain. The light during tsuyu is flat and diffuse, which eliminates harsh shadows and allows the hydrangeas' colours to glow with an intensity that bright sunshine would wash away. Rain beads on the flowers' surfaces, adding texture and reflectiveness. The relationship between hydrangeas and rain is a partnership, not an accident, and Nara's temple gardens have cultivated this partnership for centuries.

## The Biology of Colour

One of the most fascinating aspects of hydrangeas is the chemistry of their colour. The same variety of mophead hydrangea (Hydrangea macrophylla) can produce blue, purple, or pink flowers depending on soil pH. Acidic soils produce blue flowers — aluminium ions in the soil are absorbed by the plant and interact with pigments to create blue tones. Alkaline soils produce pink flowers, as the aluminium is locked in the soil and unavailable. Neutral soils produce purple — a mixture of both effects.

This means that hydrangea colour varies from garden to garden, and even from plant to plant within the same garden, depending on the local soil conditions. In Nara, the generally acidic soils of the forest and parkland favour blue and purple tones, giving the city's hydrangea displays a distinctive cool, blue-dominant palette. Temple gardens with limestone or concrete-adjacent plantings may show more pink.

## Where to See Hydrangeas in Nara

### Yagyu Ajisai Garden

**Location**: Yagyu village, approximately 30 minutes by car northeast of Nara
**Peak bloom**: Mid-June to early July
**Number of plants**: Approximately 2,000

The most spectacular hydrangea display in the Nara area is not in the city itself but in the rural village of Yagyu — famous for its samurai sword-fighting tradition and, in June, for the hydrangea garden that fills a hillside above the village. The garden is maintained by local volunteers and contains approximately 2,000 hydrangea bushes in every colour the species produces. The setting — rural, mountainous, with views over rice paddies and forested hills — is quintessentially Japanese, and the scale of the display is genuinely impressive.

**Getting there**: Bus from Nara to Yagyu (approximately 50 minutes, infrequent service — check schedules). Alternatively, taxi or rental car.

### Gangoji Temple

**Location**: Naramachi, central Nara
**Peak bloom**: Mid-June to early July
**Number of plants**: Several hundred

Gangoji, one of the oldest temples in Japan and a UNESCO World Heritage Site, maintains a beautiful hydrangea garden within its grounds. The flowers are planted along walls and around the ancient stone stupas (known as gokuraku-bo) in the temple's main courtyard. The combination of soft blue hydrangeas, moss-covered stone, and centuries-old tile roofs is one of Nara's most photogenic June compositions.

**Advantage**: Gangoji is in the heart of Naramachi, making it easily accessible and combinable with exploration of the old town.

### Shin-Yakushiji Temple Surroundings

**Location**: Near Kasuga Taisha, eastern Nara
**Peak bloom**: Early to mid-June
**Number of plants**: Moderate plantings along approach

The quiet residential lanes around Shin-Yakushiji temple harbour some of Nara's most charming hydrangea plantings. Local residents cultivate hydrangeas in their gardens and along the narrow streets, creating an informal flower walk that connects naturally with a visit to this underappreciated Nara Period temple.

### Hasedera Temple

**Location**: Sakurai, approximately 45 minutes by Kintetsu train
**Peak bloom**: Mid-June to early July
**Number of plants**: Approximately 30,000

Hasedera — the "flower temple" — claims the single most impressive hydrangea display in Nara Prefecture. Approximately 30,000 hydrangea plants line the mountainside around the temple, creating waves of colour that cascade down the slopes alongside the famous 399-step covered staircase. The sheer density of flowers, combined with the temple's dramatic hillside setting, makes this one of the premier hydrangea destinations in all of Japan.

During peak bloom, the temple sometimes extends opening hours and offers special viewing access. The combination of the hydrangeas, the ancient covered staircase, and the temple's elevated main hall (with panoramic views over the blooming hillside) is unforgettable.

### Yata-dera Temple

**Location**: Koriyama, approximately 20 minutes by Kintetsu train
**Peak bloom**: Mid-June to early July
**Number of plants**: Approximately 10,000

Known as the "hydrangea temple" (ajisai-dera), Yata-dera has cultivated hydrangeas as a signature feature. The temple grounds are terraced into the hillside, with hydrangea plantings along every level. The temple also maintains a collection of unusual hydrangea varieties, including lacecap types (gaku-ajisai) with their delicate, open flower structures.

### Nara Park Scattered Plantings

Throughout Nara Park, hydrangeas grow in scattered clusters — along stream edges, beside pathways, and around the margins of temple grounds. These are not formal gardens but naturalistic plantings that integrate with the park's broader landscape. Walking through the park in June with an eye for hydrangeas reveals pockets of colour in unexpected places — a cluster of blue beside a stone lantern, a line of purple along a stream bank, white flowers glowing in the shade of a camphor tree.

## Hydrangea Viewing in the Rain

Hydrangeas are best viewed in conditions that most visitors try to avoid. Here is how to embrace the rain:

**Clothing**: A quality rain jacket and waterproof shoes are more practical than an umbrella, which limits hand use and restricts vision. However, the traditional Japanese practice of carrying a beautiful umbrella (wagasa or bangasa) through a flower garden is itself an aesthetic experience.

**Photography**: Rain on hydrangeas creates extraordinary photographic opportunities. Water droplets on petals provide texture and reflection. Overcast skies eliminate harsh shadows and produce even, saturated colour. A macro lens or close-up mode reveals details invisible to the casual eye — the individual florets within the flower head, each one a miniature cup of colour.

**Timing**: Mornings are often drier than afternoons during tsuyu. If the forecast shows rain developing through the day, plan hydrangea viewing for the first hours of daylight.

**Comfort**: Japanese convenience stores sell inexpensive but effective rain gear. Carry a small towel for wiping rain from camera lenses, benches, and the self.

## The Cultural Context of Hydrangeas

Hydrangeas occupy an interesting position in Japanese aesthetics. They are not celebrated with the intensity of cherry blossoms (spring) or chrysanthemums (autumn), yet they have a deep presence in Japanese culture. The poet Matsuo Basho wrote of them. They appear in ukiyo-e woodblock prints. They are associated with patience, gratitude, and the beauty of imperfection — qualities that resonate with the rainy season's demand for acceptance and finding beauty in conditions that are less than ideal.

In the language of flowers (hanakotoba), blue hydrangeas can signify coldness or apology — a reflection of the flower's association with rain and melancholy. Pink hydrangeas carry warmer associations, linked to heartfelt emotion and sincerity. The multiplicity of meanings suits a flower that changes colour depending on its soil — a reminder that beauty is contingent, contextual, and never fixed.

## A Suggested Hydrangea Day

**Morning**: Start at Gangoji in Naramachi for an intimate temple garden experience. The temple opens early, and morning light (even on overcast days) is gentle and atmospheric.

**Mid-morning**: Walk through Naramachi's streets to Shin-Yakushiji, noting private garden hydrangeas along the route.

**Lunch**: Naramachi's restaurants offer seasonal menus that often feature early summer ingredients — ayu (sweetfish), young ginger, and fresh tofu.

**Afternoon**: Take the Kintetsu train to Hasedera for the grand-scale hydrangea display, or bus to Yagyu for the hillside garden. Either provides a dramatic contrast to the morning's intimate city viewing.

## Where to Stay

June is Nara's quietest month — a fact that makes it one of the best times to experience the city without crowds. Kanoya ryokan in June offers the particular pleasure of a traditional Japanese interior against the sound of rain outside — the shoji screens filtering soft light, the garden receiving the rain that feeds the season's growth. The transition from a morning spent among wet hydrangeas to an afternoon in a warm, dry tatami room, with tea and sweets, is one of the gentle rhythms that define rainy season travel in Japan.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When is peak hydrangea season in Nara?

Peak bloom typically occurs from mid-June to early July, coinciding with the tsuyu rainy season. Exact timing varies by location and year.

### What is the best hydrangea spot near Nara?

Hasedera temple (45 minutes by train) with approximately 30,000 plants is the largest display. Within the city, Gangoji temple offers the most atmospheric viewing. Yata-dera is known as Nara's dedicated "hydrangea temple."

### Are hydrangeas worth seeing in the rain?

Yes — hydrangeas are arguably at their most beautiful in rain. Water droplets enhance the flowers' texture, and overcast skies produce saturated, even colour. Embrace the rain as part of the experience.

### Why are hydrangeas different colours?

Soil pH determines hydrangea colour. Acidic soil produces blue flowers, alkaline soil produces pink, and neutral soil produces purple. The same plant can change colour if moved to different soil.

### Is June a good time to visit Nara?

June is excellent for visitors who value solitude and atmospheric beauty. Crowds are minimal, accommodation is easier to book, and the rainy season landscape has a lush, saturated quality unlike any other season.
