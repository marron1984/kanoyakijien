# Nara's Forgotten Temples: Ten Sites You've Never Heard Of

Nara has a problem that most cities would envy: its famous temples are so extraordinary that they eclipse everything else. Todai-ji's Great Buddha, Kofuku-ji's five-storey pagoda, Kasuga Taisha's lantern-lined paths — these sites deserve their fame and their crowds. But they create a gravitational field so strong that most visitors never escape it. They arrive at Kintetsu Nara Station, walk to Nara Park, visit the big three, feed the deer, and return to Kyoto or Osaka by dinner.

Meanwhile, scattered across Nara city and the surrounding countryside, dozens of temples sit in near-silence, receiving a handful of visitors per day — or per week. Some contain National Treasures. Some preserve architecture older than anything in central Nara. Some possess gardens, statues, or atmospheres that rival or surpass the famous sites. All of them offer what the famous temples increasingly cannot: solitude, contemplation, and the experience of encountering something genuinely old without the mediation of ticket booths, audio guides, and designated photo spots.

Here are ten temples that deserve your time.

## 1. Akishinodera

Tucked into a residential neighborhood in western Nara, Akishinodera is home to one of the most beautiful Buddhist statues in Japan: a standing figure of Gigeiten (the deity of the arts) whose delicate, almost melancholic expression has made it a quiet legend among Japanese art historians. The original head dates to the Nara period; the body was recreated during the Kamakura period after a fire. This composite origin gives the figure a subtle asymmetry — the ancient head sitting on the slightly newer body — that somehow enhances its beauty.

The temple's grounds are small and peaceful, planted with wildflowers in spring and colored by modest autumn foliage. Visits feel personal — you may be the only person in the hall, standing before a figure that has been admired for 1,200 years.

**Access:** 10-minute walk from Yamato-Saidaiji Station (Kintetsu line).

## 2. Hannya-ji

Known as the "cosmos temple" for the fields of cosmos flowers that bloom across its grounds in autumn, Hannya-ji sits north of central Nara Park in an area that most tourists never reach. The temple dates to 629 CE — predating the establishment of Nara as the capital — and contains a thirteen-storey stone pagoda from the Kamakura period that is designated an Important Cultural Property.

But the real attraction is the atmosphere. In October, when the cosmos are in full bloom, the temple becomes a landscape of pink, white, and purple flowers surrounding ancient stone structures. The combination of impermanence (the flowers last only weeks) and permanence (the stone pagoda has stood for 700 years) creates a visual meditation on time that is quintessentially Japanese.

**Access:** 20-minute walk north from Kintetsu Nara Station, or a short bus ride.

## 3. Futai-ji

This temple in eastern Nara was founded in 677 CE and is associated with a story that is remarkable even by the standards of Japanese temple legends. The temple's founding priest, En no Ozunu, was said to be a sorcerer of such power that the imperial court eventually exiled him. Futai-ji preserves something of that wild, pre-establishment character. Its grounds are forested and slightly overgrown, its buildings modest, its atmosphere one of age and mystery rather than institutional Buddhism.

The temple is particularly beautiful in early morning, when mist sometimes gathers in the surrounding trees and the sounds of the city have not yet reached this elevation. It offers a glimpse of what Buddhist practice might have felt like before it became associated with grand architecture and court patronage.

**Access:** 30-minute walk east from Nara Park, uphill through residential streets.

## 4. Byakugo-ji

Perched on a hillside overlooking the Nara Basin, Byakugo-ji is famous among Japanese flower enthusiasts for its camellia and hagihana (bush clover) but almost unknown to international visitors. The temple's sloped grounds ascend through layers of gardens, each offering wider views across the basin. On clear days, the panorama extends from the mountains east of Nara to the distant ridges beyond Osaka.

The temple's interior contains Kamakura-period statues of exceptional quality, displayed in a hall that retains its medieval atmosphere. The combination of art, gardens, and views makes Byakugo-ji one of Nara's most complete temple experiences — and one of its least crowded.

**Access:** 15-minute walk from Kintetsu Nara Station, uphill to the east.

## 5. Enjoji

Located in the Tomio area northwest of central Nara, Enjoji is a Shingon temple whose main hall contains a seated Amida Buddha from the late Heian period — a masterwork of wooden sculpture whose serene expression and gentle posture represent the peak of classical Japanese Buddhist art. The temple receives so few visitors that the hall is often opened specifically for you by a temple attendant who may offer a brief, personal explanation of the statue and its history.

The grounds include a garden designed in the Pure Land style — a physical representation of the Western Paradise of Amida Buddha, with a pond, bridges, and carefully placed stones. In November, the garden's maples turn brilliant red, and the reflection of color in the pond creates one of Nara's finest autumn scenes.

**Access:** 10-minute walk from Tomio Station (Kintetsu line).

## 6. Tokondo of Kairyuoji

In the village of Uda, south of Nara city, Kairyuoji is a mountain temple of extraordinary antiquity and atmosphere. Its Tokondo (East Golden Hall) dates to the early Kamakura period and houses Buddhist statues from the Heian era. The temple sits among cedar forests, and the approach — up a long stone staircase through towering trees — is as rewarding as the destination.

Kairyuoji requires more effort to reach than the central Nara temples, but it repays the journey with an experience of mountain Buddhism that the urban temples cannot provide. The silence, the forest, the age of the buildings, and the quality of the sculpture combine into something genuinely transcendent.

**Access:** Bus from Kintetsu Muroguchi-Ono Station (approximately 20 minutes), then a short walk.

## 7. Saidai-ji

Despite being one of the "Seven Great Temples of Nara" — the most important temples of the Nara period — Saidai-ji receives a fraction of the visitors that Todai-ji or Kofuku-ji attract. The disparity is partly due to location (it is west of the city center, near Yamato-Saidaiji Station) and partly because its original grand structures were destroyed by fire and rebuilt on a smaller scale.

What remains is nonetheless significant. The temple's Aizen Hall contains an important Kamakura-period statue of Aizen Myoo, and the temple hosts one of Nara's most unusual events: the Ochamori giant tea ceremony, held annually in the second week of January, in which participants drink matcha from comically enormous tea bowls. The ceremony has been held since the Kamakura period and draws Japanese visitors but few foreigners.

**Access:** Adjacent to Yamato-Saidaiji Station (Kintetsu line).

## 8. Shin-Yakushi-ji

Technically not unknown — it appears in some guidebooks — but dramatically undervisited relative to its quality. Shin-Yakushi-ji's main hall, one of the few original Nara-period structures in the city, houses a remarkable ensemble: a seated Yakushi Nyorai (Medicine Buddha) surrounded by twelve divine generals (Juni Shinsho), most dating to the Nara period and all carved from single blocks of clay in postures of dynamic fury that contrast dramatically with the calm Buddha at their center.

The hall is dim, lit by natural light filtering through wooden lattices, and the effect of encountering these fierce, life-sized guardian figures in the shadows is genuinely startling. This is Nara-period sculpture at its most powerful, experienced in something close to its original setting.

**Access:** 20-minute walk southeast from Kintetsu Nara Station.

## 9. Taimadera

Located in the town of Katsuragi, southwest of Nara city, Taimadera is associated with the legend of Princess Chujo, who is said to have woven a mandala of the Western Paradise in a single night with supernatural assistance. The Taima Mandala — a tapestry depicting Amida's Pure Land — is the temple's most famous treasure, though the original is no longer displayed and copies substitute.

The temple's East and West Pagodas, both dating to the Nara period, are the only pair of original Nara-period pagodas surviving anywhere in Japan. This fact alone would justify a visit, but the temple also possesses beautiful gardens, a serene approach through the countryside, and an atmosphere of deep, undisturbed antiquity.

**Access:** 5-minute walk from Taimadera Station (Kintetsu line), approximately 40 minutes from Nara.

## 10. Joruri-ji

In the Kizugawa Valley east of Nara, Joruri-ji possesses something unique in Japan: a row of nine seated Amida Buddha statues, dating to the late Heian period, arranged in a single hall overlooking a pond. The nine Buddhas correspond to the nine levels of rebirth in the Pure Land, and their serene, uniform presence — nine identical expressions of compassion, each slightly larger than life-size — creates an effect of overwhelming peace.

The temple's garden, designed as a representation of the Pure Land, uses the pond as a metaphorical divide between the earthly world and paradise. Standing on the eastern shore, looking across the water at the nine Buddhas in their hall, you see what a twelfth-century believer saw: the paradise that awaits the faithful, gleaming across the water.

**Access:** Bus from JR Kamo Station (approximately 20 minutes). Allow a half day for the visit.

## Planning Your Hidden Temple Visits

These ten temples reward different approaches. Akishinodera, Byakugo-ji, Shin-Yakushi-ji, and Hannya-ji are close enough to central Nara to be woven into a day that also includes the famous sites. Saidai-ji and Enjoji require a short train ride. Taimadera, Kairyuoji, and Joruri-ji are half-day excursions that benefit from an early start and a willingness to explore beyond the city limits.

Staying in Nara — rather than day-tripping from Kyoto or Osaka — makes these visits practical. Guests at Kanoya ryokan can devote early mornings to the quiet temples before the day-trip crowds arrive at the famous ones, or dedicate entire afternoons to the outlying temples, returning to Naramachi for evening rest.

None of these temples charges more than a modest admission fee, and several are free. None has an English-language audio guide. None sells elaborate souvenirs. What they offer instead is the thing that brought you to Nara in the first place, before you knew what you were looking for: the direct encounter with something old, beautiful, and unmediated by the apparatus of modern tourism.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are these temples open every day?

Most are open daily, though some close during holidays or may have restricted hours in winter. Kairyuoji and Joruri-ji, being more remote, sometimes have limited visiting hours. Check locally before making the journey.

### Do I need to speak Japanese to visit?

No, though English signage is limited or absent at most of these temples. A basic guidebook or translation app will provide sufficient context. The art and architecture speak across language barriers.

### Are these temples wheelchair accessible?

Most are not fully accessible, as they are historic structures with stone steps, uneven paths, and raised floors. Saidai-ji and Shin-Yakushi-ji offer the most accessible terrain among the ten.

### Which hidden temple should I visit if I only have time for one?

*If you have time for only one lesser-known temple in Nara, visit Shin-Yakushi-ji. Its Nara-period main hall, with a seated Medicine Buddha surrounded by twelve fierce guardian generals — most dating to the eighth century — offers the most powerful encounter with Nara-period art available outside the major museums, in an intimate setting that the famous temples cannot match.*
