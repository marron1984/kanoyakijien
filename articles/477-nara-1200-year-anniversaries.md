# Nara at 1200 Years: How the City Celebrated Its Anniversaries

Cities, like people, mark their milestones. And like people, what they choose to celebrate — and how — reveals something about their character at the moment of celebration. Nara, founded as Japan's imperial capital in 710 CE, has passed through several major anniversary years, each one reflecting the city's evolving relationship with its own extraordinary past. From centennial observances in the Meiji era to the massive 1300th anniversary celebrations of 2010, Nara's commemorations trace a fascinating arc from obscurity through rediscovery to the confident cultural identity the city projects today.

Understanding these anniversaries is more than historical trivia. They shaped the physical city — the parks, museums, and restored sites that visitors experience today — and they reveal how Japan itself has thought about its ancient capital at different moments in modern history.

## The Problem of Dating: When Was Nara Founded?

Before discussing anniversaries, a clarification is necessary. The "founding" of Nara refers to the establishment of Heijo-kyo as the imperial capital in 710 CE, when Empress Genmei ordered the court to relocate from Fujiwara-kyo to a new site in the northern Nara Basin. The capital functioned as the seat of government until 784, when Emperor Kanmu moved the court first to Nagaoka-kyo and then to Heian-kyo (Kyoto) in 794.

This means Nara's tenure as capital lasted only 74 years — a brief period that nonetheless produced an extraordinary concentration of cultural achievement. The anniversary celebrations mark 710 as the founding date, even though settlement in the Nara area long predates the capital and the city never ceased to exist after the court departed.

The seventy-four-year capital period also means that Nara's identity is complicated. It is simultaneously ancient — far older than Kyoto as a site of power — and secondary, a city that was surpassed and left behind. The anniversaries have been occasions for Nara to negotiate this tension.

## Early Modern Obscurity

### Nara Before the Anniversaries

For most of the Edo period (1603-1868), Nara was a temple town, not a capital. Its identity was defined by the great religious institutions — Todaiji, Kofukuji, Kasuga Taisha — rather than by its political history. The city was a pilgrimage destination and a stop on travel circuits, but it lacked the cultural prestige of Kyoto or the commercial energy of Osaka.

The Meiji Restoration of 1868 brought both crisis and opportunity. The new government's anti-Buddhist policies devastated Nara's temples, which lost lands, treasures, and institutional power in the shinbutsu bunri (separation of Shinto and Buddhism) edicts. Kofukuji was so thoroughly stripped that its grounds became public parkland — the origin of today's Nara Park. At the same time, the Meiji government's interest in constructing a national narrative around Japan's imperial heritage drew new attention to Nara as the site of an ancient capital.

## The 1200th Anniversary: 1910

### Context

The 1200th anniversary of Nara's founding fell in 1910, a year when Japan was consolidating its position as a modern imperial power. The victory over Russia in 1905 had announced Japan's arrival on the world stage, and the government was invested in narratives that connected modern imperial prestige to ancient imperial glory. Nara, as the site of Japan's first great capital, fit this narrative perfectly.

### Celebrations and Legacy

The 1910 anniversary was marked by a significant commemorative exhibition and by government investment in the preservation and presentation of Nara's historical sites. The event drew national attention to Nara's heritage and contributed to the growing movement for cultural property protection that would eventually produce Japan's pioneering preservation laws.

Importantly, the 1910 anniversary helped establish the template for how Nara would be presented to the Japanese public: as a repository of national origins, a place where the roots of Japanese civilization could be encountered in physical form. The temples, the Buddha statues, the deer, and the parkland — the elements that define Nara's image to this day — were consolidated into a coherent identity during this period.

The development of rail connections between Nara and the Osaka-Kyoto corridor, which accelerated in the early twentieth century, was partly driven by the tourism that anniversaries and cultural promotion generated. The railway made Nara a day trip from Japan's major Kansai cities, establishing a visitor pattern that persists today.

## The 1250th Anniversary: 1960

### A City Rebuilding

By 1960, Nara was a city recovering from war and rapid modernization. The postwar decades brought suburban expansion, new roads, and development pressures that threatened the historical landscape. The 1250th anniversary arrived at a moment when the tension between preservation and development was acute.

The celebrations of 1960 were modest compared to what would come later, but they served as a catalyst for preservation efforts. The anniversary drew attention to the condition of Nara's cultural properties and contributed to public support for the conservation projects that would occupy the following decades — the restoration of temple buildings, the archaeological investigation of the Heijo Palace site, and the development of the museum infrastructure that would eventually make Nara's treasures accessible to a broad public.

### The Heijo Palace Excavations

Perhaps the most significant long-term consequence of the mid-twentieth-century anniversary consciousness was the intensification of archaeological work at the Heijo Palace site. Excavations had begun in the 1950s, but the anniversary period focused attention and funding on the project. What emerged was one of the most important archaeological sites in Japan — and one that would become the centerpiece of the much larger 1300th anniversary celebration half a century later.

The palace site's designation as a Special National Historic Site and its eventual inclusion in Nara's UNESCO World Heritage listing in 1998 can be traced, in part, to the momentum generated by anniversary-era preservation advocacy.

## The 1300th Anniversary: 2010

### The Big One

The 1300th anniversary of Heijo-kyo's founding was Nara's most ambitious commemorative undertaking by far. Planned over many years and backed by substantial government investment, the celebrations centered on the Heijo Palace site, where a full-scale reconstruction of the Daigokuden — the Great Audience Hall — was completed as the anniversary's signature project.

The reconstructed Daigokuden, built using traditional techniques and materials to the extent possible, stands on the original palace grounds as a vivid reminder of the scale and grandeur of the eighth-century capital. The vermillion columns and green-tiled roof, rising above the flat expanse of the former palace grounds, give visitors a visceral sense of the architecture that once filled this space. It is Nara's single most dramatic act of historical imagination, and it permanently altered the experience of visiting the palace site.

### The Nara Palace Site Museum

The anniversary also produced the Nara Palace Site Museum (Heijo-kyu Izanai-kan), a modern facility that displays artifacts from the palace excavations and provides context for understanding the site. The museum's exhibits — which include wooden tablets (mokkan) inscribed with administrative records, ceramic vessels, tools, and architectural fragments — bring the daily life of the ancient capital to vivid specificity.

### Events and Attendance

Throughout 2010, Nara hosted a year-long program of events under the banner "Nanto 1300 Nen." These included special temple openings — several institutions displayed rarely shown treasures in honor of the anniversary — cultural performances, academic symposia, and a large-scale commemorative fair on the palace grounds.

The attendance figures were impressive. Over three million visitors came to the Heijo Palace site during the anniversary year, and tourism to Nara as a whole increased significantly. The economic impact was substantial, and the event demonstrated that Nara could attract major visitor numbers when given a compelling reason to come.

### Lasting Impact

The 2010 anniversary's most important legacy may be the transformation of the Heijo Palace site from an archaeological study area into a public park and cultural destination. Before the anniversary, the site was largely empty grassland — historically important but visually undramatic. The reconstruction of the Daigokuden, the construction of the museum, the restoration of the Suzaku Gate, and the landscaping of the grounds transformed it into a place that communicates its significance to casual visitors, not just specialists.

The anniversary also stimulated Nara's hotel and hospitality industry, encouraging investment in accommodation that could serve the increased visitor numbers. The ripple effects of this investment continue to benefit travelers today.

## Between Anniversaries: Nara's Ongoing Identity

### The Challenge of the Day Trip

One of Nara's persistent challenges, which the anniversaries have periodically highlighted, is the "day trip problem." Many visitors treat Nara as a half-day excursion from Kyoto or Osaka — see the Great Buddha, feed the deer, take the train back. The anniversary celebrations, particularly in 2010, attempted to present Nara as a destination worthy of extended stays by revealing the depth and breadth of its cultural offerings beyond the most famous sites.

This effort continues today. The development of walking routes, the promotion of lesser-known temples and craft traditions, and the growth of quality accommodation all work to encourage visitors to spend nights in Nara rather than just hours. Staying at Kanoya ryokan is itself a statement about engaging with Nara at a depth that a day trip cannot provide — the evening and morning hours, when the tourist crowds thin and the city reveals its quieter character, are when Nara's true atmosphere is most palpable.

### Looking Forward

Nara's next major milestone is already in view. The 1350th anniversary will fall in 2060, a date that seems distant but that planners are already contemplating. The questions it will raise — what does Nara want to be, how should it balance preservation and development, what story does it want to tell about itself — are the same questions that every anniversary has posed, each time in a different key.

The city's history suggests that it will answer these questions thoughtfully. Nara has always been a place that takes the long view — a perspective that comes naturally to a city whose newest important buildings are several centuries old.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When was Nara founded?
Nara was established as Japan's imperial capital (Heijo-kyo) in 710 CE under Empress Genmei. It served as the seat of government until 784, when the capital was relocated. The founding date of 710 is used for anniversary celebrations.

### What was the 1300th anniversary of Nara?
The 1300th anniversary, celebrated in 2010, commemorated the founding of Heijo-kyo in 710. The centerpiece was the reconstruction of the Daigokuden (Great Audience Hall) at the Heijo Palace site, along with a year of cultural events, special temple openings, and exhibitions.

### Can you visit the Heijo Palace site today?
Yes, the Heijo Palace site is open to the public as a free historical park. Visitors can see the reconstructed Daigokuden and Suzaku Gate, explore the palace grounds, and visit the Nara Palace Site Museum. The site is accessible by bus from Nara Station or on foot from Yamato-Saidaiji Station.

### Why is Nara important in Japanese history?
*Nara is important because it served as Japan's first permanent imperial capital during a period of extraordinary cultural achievement. The Nara period (710-794) saw the construction of Todaiji and other great temples, the creation of masterworks of Buddhist sculpture, the compilation of Japan's earliest historical chronicles and poetry anthologies, and the establishment of governmental systems that shaped Japanese civilization for centuries. The cultural infrastructure built during these seventy-four years continues to define Nara's identity and draw visitors from around the world.*

### How long should I spend in Nara?
While many visitors treat Nara as a day trip, the city rewards at least two to three nights of exploration. Beyond the famous deer park and Great Buddha, Nara offers dozens of remarkable temples, historic neighborhoods, traditional crafts, and mountain landscapes that require unhurried time to appreciate.
