# The Move from Nara: Why Japan's Capital Shifted to Kyoto

In 784 CE, Emperor Kanmu made one of the most consequential decisions in Japanese history: he moved the capital away from Nara. The move was first to Nagaoka-kyō (a site that was abandoned after just ten years) and then to Heian-kyō — present-day Kyoto — in 794 CE, where the capital would remain for over a thousand years.

The question of why is more complex than it appears. The standard answer — that the Buddhist temples had become too powerful — is true but incomplete. The move was driven by a convergence of political, religious, personal, and even practical factors that together made the magnificent city of Nara untenable as a capital. Understanding these factors illuminates both what Nara was and what it became after the court departed.

## The Power of the Temples

The most commonly cited reason for the move is the political power of Nara's Buddhist establishment. Emperor Shōmu's ambitious temple-building programme of the 740s had transformed Buddhism from a religion of personal devotion into a state institution with enormous wealth, land, and influence.

By the late 8th century, the great temples — Tōdai-ji, Kōfuku-ji, and others — controlled vast estates, maintained private armies of warrior monks, and intervened regularly in court politics. The temples were not merely spiritual institutions but political powers that rivalled and sometimes exceeded the authority of the imperial court.

The crisis reached its peak during the reign of Empress Shōtoku (Kōken), who elevated the Buddhist monk Dōkyō to the position of Daijō-daijin (Grand Minister) and, according to some accounts, considered making him emperor. The Dōkyō affair scandalized the court and demonstrated, in the starkest terms, the danger of allowing religious authority to merge with political power.

After Shōtoku's death in 770 CE, the court moved decisively to reassert imperial authority. The selection of Kanmu — a strong-willed emperor with no patience for monastic politics — was itself a reaction to the perceived excess of temple influence. His decision to move the capital was, at its core, an escape from institutions that his predecessor Shōmu had inadvertently made too powerful to control.

## The Fujiwara Factor

Temple politics was not the only political consideration. The Fujiwara clan, which had dominated court politics for much of the Nara period, was deeply entrenched in the city's power structures. Their clan temple, Kōfuku-ji, was one of the most powerful institutions in Nara, and their network of alliances with other temples and court factions made the city a Fujiwara stronghold.

Kanmu, whose own mother was of non-Fujiwara descent (she was of Korean immigrant lineage), had reason to want distance from a city where the Fujiwara dominated the physical and political landscape. A new capital offered the possibility of reshaping the court's power dynamics — literally rebuilding the stage on which politics was performed.

## Ritual Pollution and the Nagaoka Disaster

Japanese beliefs about ritual impurity (kegare) played a role that modern visitors sometimes overlook. In the Shinto and folk belief systems of the period, death, illness, and violence created pollution that could affect a place permanently. Nara, after seventy years as a capital, had accumulated considerable ritual pollution from deaths, political intrigues, and the Dōkyō scandal.

This concern became acute at Nagaoka-kyō, the first replacement capital. In 785 CE, just a year after the move, Fujiwara no Tanetsugu — the official overseeing the new capital's construction — was assassinated. Kanmu's brother, Prince Sawara, was accused of involvement and exiled, dying during the journey (possibly by suicide or starvation).

Sawara's vengeful spirit was subsequently blamed for a series of disasters: floods, epidemics, the death of Kanmu's wife and mother, and crop failures. The court concluded that Nagaoka-kyō was cursed by Sawara's anger, and in 794 CE, Kanmu abandoned it for a new site — Heian-kyō, meaning "Capital of Peace and Tranquility."

The story of Nagaoka illustrates how deeply spiritual concerns shaped political decisions. The move from Nara was not purely rational statecraft — it was also driven by the conviction that the spiritual health of a place mattered as much as its strategic advantages.

## Practical Considerations

Beyond the political and spiritual factors, practical concerns contributed to the decision:

**Water supply**: Nara's location, while scenic, presented challenges for water management as the city grew. The Heian-kyō site, situated between the Kamo and Katsura rivers, offered better natural drainage and water supply.

**Transportation**: Kyoto's position along river routes connecting to the Inland Sea made it more accessible for the maritime trade and communication that were becoming increasingly important. Nara, located inland in a basin, was less well-connected to Japan's expanding commercial networks.

**Space**: The Heijō-kyō site was constrained by hills to the east and limited room for expansion. The Kyoto basin offered more space for a capital that could grow over time — a foresight that proved justified, as Kyoto remained the capital for over a millennium.

**Geomancy (fūsui)**: Chinese geomantic principles, which the Japanese court took seriously, held that an ideal capital should have specific geographic features — mountains to the north, water to the south, hills to the east and west. The Kyoto basin fit these requirements more closely than Nara.

## The Impact on Nara

The departure of the capital was, in one sense, devastating. The city lost its political significance, its population declined, and the grand palace complex fell into ruin. Nara went from being the centre of the Japanese world to a provincial city in a matter of years.

But in another sense, the move preserved Nara. Because the city was no longer the seat of political power, it was not subject to the cycles of destruction and rebuilding that affected subsequent capitals. The great temples, freed from the political entanglements that had caused the move, continued to function as religious and cultural institutions. Their role shifted from political power to cultural preservation — a role they have maintained ever since.

The absence of political importance also meant that Nara was spared the worst of the civil wars that periodically devastated Kyoto. While Nara's temples suffered damage in the 12th and 16th centuries, the city's overall heritage survived more intact than it might have if it had remained a capital that rival factions wanted to control.

This is one of the paradoxes of Nara's history: the event that ended the city's golden age is also what preserved its golden-age heritage. The temples survived because the court left. The art survived because the temples survived. The continuity that makes Nara extraordinary today is, in part, a consequence of the political crisis that drove the capital away.

## What Kanmu Built

The capital that Kanmu created at Heian-kyō would prove even more enduring than Nara. Kyoto served as the imperial capital until 1868 — over a thousand years of continuous function. The Heian period (794–1185) that began with the move produced the aesthetic and literary culture — The Tale of Genji, The Pillow Book, the refined court arts — that many people think of as quintessentially Japanese.

But Kanmu did not simply replicate the Nara model in a new location. He deliberately limited the power of Buddhist institutions in the new capital. The great Nara temples were not invited to move. The new capital had fewer major temples, and those that were established — like Tō-ji and Saiji — were kept under tighter imperial control.

New forms of Buddhism, particularly the Tendai and Shingon schools established by Saichō and Kūkai on mountain retreats outside the city, replaced the Nara schools as the dominant religious forces. The relationship between religion and politics was restructured, though it was never fully resolved — temple power would resurface repeatedly throughout Japanese history.

## Visiting the Evidence

Several sites in Nara illuminate the story of the capital's departure:

**Heijō Palace Site (Heijō-kyū)**: The excavated and partially reconstructed palace grounds show the scale of what was abandoned. Walking the vast open site — where the Former Imperial Audience Hall and Suzaku Gate have been rebuilt — conveys the magnitude of the decision to leave.

**Kōfuku-ji**: The Fujiwara clan temple whose political power was one reason for the move. Its five-storey pagoda and National Treasure Hall are reminders of the institutional wealth and influence that made the court uncomfortable.

**Tōdai-ji**: The temple whose establishment under Emperor Shōmu set in motion the accumulation of religious power that ultimately drove the capital away. The irony is rich: the Great Buddha was built to protect the nation, and its success was so great that the court felt it had to flee.

For travellers interested in this pivotal moment in Japanese history, an overnight stay in Nara provides the time to visit both the palace site and the temples, understanding them not as isolated monuments but as parts of a single story about power, religion, and the difficult relationship between them. Kanoya ryokan makes a comfortable base for this kind of historically engaged exploration.

## The Legacy of Departure

The move from Nara to Kyoto was not just a change of address. It was a reorientation of Japanese civilisation — away from the state Buddhism of the Nara period and toward the courtly aesthetics of the Heian period. The literary culture, the refined sensibility, the emphasis on beauty and impermanence that characterise classical Japanese culture are, in a sense, consequences of the decision to leave Nara and start again.

Yet Nara did not disappear. It remained, and remains, the place where the foundations were laid — the place where Buddhism became a national institution, where Japanese art first achieved greatness, where the writing system took shape, and where the idea of Japan as a civilised state was first given physical form. The move to Kyoto did not erase this legacy. It simply allowed it to be preserved in a form that the passage of time has rendered, if anything, more valuable.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When did the capital move from Nara?

The capital moved from Nara (Heijō-kyō) in 784 CE. Emperor Kanmu first relocated to Nagaoka-kyō, then to Heian-kyō (Kyoto) in 794 CE, where it remained for over a thousand years.

### Why did the capital leave Nara?

The primary reason was the excessive political power of Nara's Buddhist temples, which had begun to interfere directly in imperial politics. Additional factors included Fujiwara clan entrenchment, practical concerns about water supply and transport, and beliefs about ritual pollution.

### What happened to Nara after the capital moved?

Nara lost its political importance and much of its population, but its great temples continued to function. The absence of political significance paradoxically helped preserve the city's heritage, as it was spared the cycles of destruction that affected later capitals.

### Can I visit the original Nara palace?

The Heijō Palace Site has been excavated and partially reconstructed. The Former Imperial Audience Hall and Suzaku Gate have been rebuilt to their original scale, and the site is open to visitors free of charge.
