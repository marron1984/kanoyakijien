# Nara's Ancient Postal Roads: Tracing the Gokaidō Connections

Before railways compressed Japan into a country of hours, it was a country of weeks. The distance between Edo and Kyoto — now a two-hour shinkansen ride — required roughly two weeks on foot along the Tokaido highway, passing through fifty-three post stations where travelers slept, ate, changed horses, and exchanged the news that held the nation together. These highways, collectively known as the Gokaidō (Five Routes), were the arterial system of Tokugawa Japan, and Nara occupied a peculiar position within their network: not on any of the five main routes, yet connected to all of them through a web of subsidiary roads that reflected the ancient capital's enduring religious and cultural importance.

Walking these old roads today — through cedar forests, past crumbling stone markers, along ridgelines that offer the same views Edo-period travelers saw — is one of the most underappreciated ways to experience Nara Prefecture. The infrastructure of modern tourism has largely ignored these routes, which means the walker encounters them much as they have existed for centuries: quiet, rural, and marked by the slow accumulation of history that main highways bulldozed long ago.

## The Gokaidō System and Nara's Place in It

### The Five Routes

The Tokugawa shogunate formalized the Gokaidō system in the early seventeenth century, though most of the routes followed paths that had been in use for centuries. The five roads — Tokaido, Nakasendo, Koshu Kaido, Oshu Kaido, and Nikko Kaido — radiated from Nihonbashi in Edo (modern Tokyo) and connected the shogun's capital to the country's major regions. Each road was maintained by the domains it passed through, and the post stations (shukuba) along them were regulated to provide standardized services to travelers.

Nara sat south of the main Tokaido and Nakasendo routes, which passed through Kyoto before continuing west or looping through the mountains. But the ancient capital's status as Japan's foremost pilgrimage destination meant that branch roads connecting Nara to these major highways carried constant traffic. Pilgrims bound for Kasuga Taisha, Todai-ji, Hase-dera, or the Yoshino mountains all needed routes from the main highways into the Yamato basin, and these routes developed their own post stations, tea houses, and traveler infrastructure.

### The Yamato Kaido

The most important of these connecting roads was the Yamato Kaido, which linked Osaka (and by extension the western terminus of the Tokaido) to Nara. This road crossed the Ikoma mountains through the Kuragari Pass and descended into the Yamato Plain, arriving in Nara after passing through a series of post towns that still retain fragments of their Edo-period character.

The Yamato Kaido was not merely a pilgrim route. It was the primary commercial artery connecting Nara's craft producers — sumi ink makers, brush artisans, sake brewers — to the markets of Osaka. The road carried finished goods westward and raw materials eastward in a steady circulation that sustained Nara's economy for centuries.

### The Ise Kaido Connection

To the east, the Ise Kaido connected Nara to Ise Grand Shrine, Japan's holiest Shinto site. This route passed through Sakurai, Hase, and over the mountains into Mie Prefecture. During the Edo period, when mass pilgrimages to Ise (okage mairi) periodically swept the country in waves of religious fervor, this road through Nara became one of the most heavily traveled pilgrimage routes in Japan. Travelers would visit Nara's temples on the way to or from Ise, making the ancient capital a natural waypoint on the pilgrimage circuit.

## Walking the Old Roads Today

### The Kuragari Pass Route (Yamato Kaido)

The most rewarding single-day walk along Nara's old postal roads follows the Kuragari Pass route over the Ikoma mountains. Starting from Tatsuta in Nara Prefecture, the path climbs through dense forest to the pass at approximately 455 meters elevation, then descends toward the Osaka side. The Kuragari Pass itself retains a remarkably atmospheric section of the original road — paved with fitted stones, shaded by ancient trees, and lined with stone Jizo statues that have watched over travelers for centuries.

**Practical details:** The walk from Tatsuta (reachable by Kintetsu Railway) to Kuragari Pass takes approximately two to three hours one way. The path is well marked but involves genuine mountain terrain — proper footwear is essential. The pass area includes a small shrine and rest area. Most walkers continue to Hiraoka on the Osaka side and return by train, making a full traverse of roughly five to six hours.

Along the route, stone markers (michishirube) still stand at intersections, their carved characters indicating distances to Nara, Osaka, and intermediate post towns. These markers, typically dating from the Edo or early Meiji period, are the road's most tangible surviving artifacts — functional objects that have outlasted the system they were designed to serve.

### The Yamanobe no Michi

Although it predates the Gokaidō system by many centuries — this path along the eastern edge of the Yamato Plain may be Japan's oldest road — the Yamanobe no Michi incorporated into the network of routes that connected Nara's southern temples to the main city. The walk from Tenri to Sakurai (about sixteen kilometers) passes ancient shrines, kofun burial mounds, and farmland that has been under continuous cultivation since the prehistoric period.

**Practical details:** The full Tenri to Sakurai walk takes five to six hours at a comfortable pace with stops. Both endpoints are served by JR and Kintetsu railways. The route passes Isonokami Shrine, Chogaku-ji temple, and Omiwa Shrine — any of which merits a significant stop. Spring (cherry blossoms along the path) and autumn (persimmon harvest in the orchards) are the ideal seasons.

### The Hase-dera Pilgrimage Road

The old road from Sakurai to Hase-dera temple follows the Hase River valley through a landscape that has changed remarkably little since the Heian period, when this was one of the most popular pilgrimage routes for Kyoto's aristocracy. The Tale of Genji mentions Hase-dera pilgrimages multiple times, and Sei Shonagon's Pillow Book describes the journey in detail.

The road passes through the old post town of Hase, where several traditional buildings survive along the narrow main street. The approach to Hase-dera itself — a long covered stairway climbing the hillside — has been a fixture of Japanese pilgrimage art for a millennium.

**Practical details:** The walk from Sakurai Station to Hase-dera takes approximately ninety minutes. The route follows minor roads alongside the river and is mostly flat. Hasedera Station on the Kintetsu line provides an alternative starting point for those who want only the final approach.

## Post Towns and Their Surviving Architecture

### Takada

The old castle town of Takada, southwest of Nara city, preserves one of the most complete streetscapes of traditional merchant architecture in the prefecture. The Honmachi district retains continuous rows of machiya townhouses with their characteristic narrow facades and deep interiors. During the Edo period, Takada prospered as a junction point where the Ise Kaido met roads heading south toward Yoshino and west toward Osaka.

Walking Takada's old streets reveals the architectural vocabulary of a post town: the latticed windows (koshi-mado) that allowed merchants to observe the road without being seen, the earthen storehouses (kura) set behind the main buildings to protect goods from fire, and the subtle variations in facade design that indicated each household's trade.

### Gojo

Further south, the town of Gojo retains its Edo-period layout along the old road to Kumano. The Shinmachi district is a designated preservation area where traditional buildings line both sides of the narrow street much as they did when pilgrims bound for the Kumano shrines passed through in steady streams. The town's location at the junction of mountain and plain made it a natural gathering point, and the old inns — some still operating — speak of centuries of hospitality toward weary travelers.

## The Postal System Itself

### Couriers and Speed

The Gokaidō was not merely a travel network — it was a communications system. Official couriers (hikyaku) carried government dispatches along the highways at remarkable speeds, changing horses at each post station. Express messages could travel from Edo to Osaka in roughly three days, a speed that would not be significantly exceeded until the telegraph arrived in the Meiji era.

Private courier services (machi-hikyaku) operated alongside the official system, carrying commercial correspondence and money transfers between cities. These private networks connected Nara's merchants to suppliers and customers throughout the country, and the reliability of the courier system was essential to the functioning of Nara's craft economy.

### The Post Station Experience

A typical post station offered a range of accommodations calibrated to the traveler's status and budget. Honjin were official lodgings reserved for daimyo lords and government officials — essentially requisitioned private homes whose owners were obligated to provide hospitality. Waki-honjin served samurai and wealthy merchants. Common travelers stayed at hatagoya, simple inns that offered a meal, a futon, and a bath.

This graduated hospitality system has its echoes in the modern Japanese accommodation landscape, where the distinction between luxury ryokan, business hotels, and budget hostels follows a similar logic. The tradition of the ryokan — offering guests not merely a room but an experience of curated hospitality — descends directly from the post station culture that the Gokaidō created. Establishments like Kanoya ryokan in Nara carry forward a philosophy of hosting that the old road system made central to Japanese culture.

## Planning Your Postal Road Walk

The best introduction to Nara's old road network is the Yamanobe no Michi, which combines accessibility, historical density, and manageable distance. For travelers seeking more solitude and a stronger sense of discovery, the Kuragari Pass route rewards the effort with genuine mountain atmosphere and the satisfaction of walking one of the most historically important passes in the Kansai region.

Spring and autumn are the ideal seasons for walking. Summer heat in the Yamato basin is oppressive, and winter, while crisp, shortens available daylight significantly. Carry water — vending machines are scarce on rural sections of these routes, and the convenience store culture of Japanese cities thins rapidly once you leave the train stations behind.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the Gokaidō?
*The Gokaidō were five major highways radiating from Edo (Tokyo) that formed the backbone of Japan's Tokugawa-era transportation and communication system, connecting the shogun's capital to key regions across the country.*

### Can you walk Nara's old postal roads today?
Yes, several historic routes remain walkable, including the Kuragari Pass route over the Ikoma mountains, the Yamanobe no Michi along the Yamato Plain, and the pilgrimage road to Hase-dera. All are accessible by public transportation at both ends.

### How long does the Yamanobe no Michi walk take?
The full walk from Tenri to Sakurai covers approximately sixteen kilometers and takes five to six hours at a comfortable pace, including stops at shrines and temples along the route.

### Was Nara on one of the five main Gokaidō routes?
Nara was not directly on any of the five main routes but was connected to them through important branch roads, particularly the Yamato Kaido from Osaka and the Ise Kaido leading to Ise Grand Shrine.

### What should I bring for a postal road walk?
Sturdy walking shoes, water, sun protection, and a basic trail map. Vending machines and convenience stores are scarce on rural sections. A rail pass or IC card for returning by train from the far end of the route is essential.
