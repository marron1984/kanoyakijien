# Nara Period Art: A Guide to Tenpyō-Era Masterpieces

The art produced in Nara during the 8th century — known as Tenpyō art after the era name used from 729 to 749 CE — represents the first great flowering of Japanese artistic achievement. The sculptures, paintings, textiles, and metalwork created during this period set standards that subsequent generations of Japanese artists would revere, emulate, and study for the next thirteen centuries.

Tenpyō art is remarkable for its technical sophistication, its emotional depth, and its cosmopolitan character. Drawing on traditions from China, Korea, India, and Central Asia, Nara's artists synthesised these influences into works that are recognisably international in their roots yet distinctly Japanese in their execution. For travellers who take the time to look carefully, Nara's museums and temples contain one of the world's great concentrations of pre-modern art.

## The Techniques

Understanding the techniques used by Tenpyō artists helps visitors appreciate what they are seeing.

### Bronze Casting

The most monumental technique, exemplified by the Great Buddha at Tōdai-ji. Bronze casting ranged from colossal statuary to small devotional figures and ritual implements. The process involved creating a clay core, building a wax model over it, encasing the wax in a clay mould, melting out the wax, and pouring in molten bronze — the lost-wax (cire perdue) method, used across the ancient world.

Nara-period bronze work reached its peak in the Yakushi Trinity at Yakushi-ji, cast in the early 8th century. The central Yakushi Buddha and flanking Nikkō and Gakkō bodhisattvas are among the finest bronze sculptures in East Asia — technically accomplished, emotionally serene, and possessed of a physical presence that photographs cannot convey.

### Dry Lacquer (Kanshitsu)

The most distinctive Nara-period technique was dry lacquer, which came in two forms:

**Hollow dry lacquer (dakkatsu kanshitsu)**: A clay core was sculpted into the desired form, then covered with layers of hemp cloth soaked in lacquer. After the lacquer hardened, the clay core was removed, leaving a lightweight hollow shell. Details were built up with a paste of lacquer mixed with wood powder or sawdust.

**Wood-core dry lacquer (mokushin kanshitsu)**: A rough wooden sculpture was carved, then covered with lacquer-soaked cloth and lacquer paste to create the finished surface.

The hollow technique produced sculptures of extraordinary lightness and expressiveness. The seated figure of the priest Ganjin at Tōshōdai-ji — eyes closed, face worn but peaceful — is the supreme example. Made after Ganjin's death in 763 CE, it captures individual character with a sensitivity that anticipates portrait sculpture by centuries.

The Ashura figure at Kōfuku-ji's National Treasure Hall is perhaps the most famous dry-lacquer sculpture in Japan. One of eight guardians (Hachibushū), Ashura stands with six arms and three faces, each wearing a subtly different expression. The figure is slender, youthful, and — most remarkably — vulnerable. This is not a fearsome guardian deity but a being caught in a moment of inner conflict, rendered with an emotional subtlety that astonishes modern viewers.

### Clay Sculpture (Sozō)

Clay modelling over wooden armatures produced works of extraordinary naturalism. The technique allowed sculptors to build up forms gradually, refining details with a freedom that casting did not permit.

The finest surviving examples are the guardian figures and attendants in the Hokke-dō (Sangatsu-dō) at Tōdai-ji. The main image — an eight-armed Fukūkensaku Kannon — is surrounded by a group of attendant deities made in various techniques. The clay figures among them, including the Shūkongō-jin (a wrathful protector deity kept behind closed doors and shown only once a year on December 16th), display a muscularity and dynamism that represent the peak of Nara-period sculptural achievement.

### Painting

Very little Nara-period painting survives, making what does exist exceptionally precious. The Kakogenzai Inga-kyō (Illustrated Sutra of Cause and Effect), a narrative handscroll illustrating the life of the Buddha, is the most important surviving example. Its style — with continuous narrative scenes above the sutra text — derives from Chinese painting traditions but shows distinctly Japanese sensibilities in its colour palette and compositional rhythm.

The painted decoration on objects in the Shōsō-in — leather screens, musical instruments, textiles — provides additional evidence of the painting traditions of the period, even though large-scale wall paintings and scrolls have been largely lost.

## Where to See Tenpyō Masterpieces

Nara's art is dispersed across multiple sites. Here is a guide to the essential locations:

### Kōfuku-ji National Treasure Hall

This modern museum on the Kōfuku-ji temple grounds houses what may be the single most important collection of Nara-period sculpture anywhere. The Ashura and the other Hachibushū figures are here, along with the head of a massive Yakushi Buddha and other works spanning the temple's long history.

The Ashura alone justifies the visit. Standing approximately 153 centimetres tall, the figure is slightly smaller than life-size, which gives it an intimacy that larger sculptures lack. Look at the three faces: each registers a different emotional state, and the overall effect is of a being caught between anger and compassion. The dry-lacquer technique allows for a subtlety of expression that would be impossible in stone or bronze.

### Tōdai-ji Hokke-dō (Sangatsu-dō)

The oldest building in the Tōdai-ji complex, the Hokke-dō houses a dense arrangement of Nara-period sculptures around the central Fukūkensaku Kannon. The experience of entering this hall — with its clustered deities in various materials and styles, the accumulated weight of incense and age — is one of the most powerful artistic encounters in Japan.

The sculptures here include works in dry lacquer, clay, and lacquered wood. The Nikkō and Gakkō bodhisattvas flanking the central image are masterpieces of clay sculpture, their slightly tilted heads and gentle expressions embodying the Tenpyō ideal of compassionate grace.

### Yakushi-ji

The Yakushi Trinity in the main hall is one of the supreme achievements of bronze casting in world art. The central Yakushi Nyorai sits in a posture of calm authority, flanked by the standing bodhisattvas Nikkō and Gakkō. The technical quality of the casting — the flowing drapery, the precisely modelled features, the sense of contained energy — is extraordinary.

The pedestal of the Yakushi Buddha is itself a work of art, decorated with motifs that include grape vines (a Silk Road motif) and the Four Directional Spirits, evidence of the cosmopolitan influences flowing through Nara.

### Tōshōdai-ji

The portrait of Ganjin, displayed briefly each year, is reason enough to time a visit to Nara around its showing (typically early June). The temple's main hall (kondō) also houses a group of important Nara-period sculptures, including a seated Vairocana Buddha and a standing Thousand-Armed Kannon made from a single block of wood.

### Nara National Museum

The museum's permanent Buddhist art collection provides essential context for what you see in the temples. Sculptures, paintings, and decorative arts are displayed with scholarly interpretation that helps visitors understand the techniques, iconography, and cultural significance of the works.

The annual Shōsō-in Exhibition (late October to mid-November) adds another dimension, displaying the decorative arts — textiles, metalwork, lacquerware, musical instruments — that complement the sculptural traditions.

## How to Look at Tenpyō Art

For visitors unfamiliar with Buddhist art, a few suggestions:

**Take time**: These sculptures were made to be contemplated, not glanced at. Spend at least ten minutes with a single work — the Ashura, the Ganjin portrait, the Yakushi Trinity — and allow the details to emerge.

**Look at the hands**: Mudra (hand gestures) carry specific meaning in Buddhist iconography, but they are also some of the most technically accomplished parts of the sculptures. The hands of the Yakushi Trinity are astonishingly lifelike.

**Notice the materials**: The difference between bronze, dry lacquer, and clay is visible and tactile even from a respectful distance. Each material gives the sculptor different expressive possibilities.

**Consider the context**: These works were not made for museums. They were made for temple halls, lit by oil lamps and candles, viewed through incense smoke by worshippers. The dim, atmospheric conditions of the Hokke-dō come closest to the original viewing experience.

An overnight stay in Nara is essential for seeing these works properly. Rushing between temples to see sculptures you spend thirty seconds with is a disservice to art that took months or years to create. Kanoya ryokan offers a calm base from which to visit the temples and museum at a reflective pace, with time between visits to absorb what you have seen.

## The Legacy of Tenpyō Art

The influence of Tenpyō art on subsequent Japanese art history is immeasurable. The Kamakura-period sculptors Unkei and Kaikei explicitly studied Nara-period models. The aesthetic principles established in the 8th century — the balance of naturalism and idealisation, the integration of technical mastery with spiritual expression — became permanent reference points for Japanese art.

For modern visitors, Tenpyō art offers something rarer: evidence that technical skill and emotional depth are not opposed but complementary. The artists who made the Ashura, the Ganjin portrait, and the Yakushi Trinity were master technicians. They were also, in the deepest sense, masters of human expression.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is Tenpyō art?

Tenpyō art refers to the visual arts produced during the Nara period (710–784 CE), named after the Tenpyō era (729–749 CE). It encompasses sculpture in bronze, dry lacquer, and clay, as well as painting, metalwork, and textiles.

### Where can I see the best Nara-period art?

The essential locations are: Kōfuku-ji National Treasure Hall (Ashura and Hachibushū), Tōdai-ji Hokke-dō (clay and dry-lacquer sculptures), Yakushi-ji (bronze Yakushi Trinity), Tōshōdai-ji (Ganjin portrait), and the Nara National Museum.

### What is dry lacquer sculpture?

Dry lacquer (kanshitsu) is a technique in which layers of hemp cloth soaked in lacquer are applied over a core. In the hollow technique (dakkatsu), the clay core is removed, creating a lightweight shell. This technique allowed exceptional expressive detail.

### When can I see the Ganjin portrait at Tōshōdai-ji?

The portrait of Ganjin is displayed for a limited period each year, typically around June 5–7, coinciding with the anniversary of Ganjin's death. Check Tōshōdai-ji's schedule for exact dates.
