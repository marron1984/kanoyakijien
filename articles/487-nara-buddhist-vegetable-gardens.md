# Nara's Buddhist Vegetable Gardens: Temple Farming Traditions

Behind the great halls and golden Buddhas of Nara's temples lies a quieter tradition that has sustained monastic life for over a thousand years: the temple vegetable garden. While tourists photograph pagodas and deer, monks tend rows of daikon radish, eggplant, shiso, and seasonal greens in walled plots that have been under continuous cultivation since the Nara period. These gardens are not ornamental. They are working farms, integral to the Buddhist practice of self-sufficiency and the culinary tradition of shojin ryori — the vegetarian temple cuisine that is among Japan's most refined food cultures.

For visitors who look beyond the famous sites, Nara's temple gardens offer a perspective on Japanese Buddhism that is grounded — quite literally — in the soil.

## The Buddhist Roots of Temple Farming

### Self-Sufficiency as Practice

The Vinaya — the Buddhist monastic code that governs monastic life — emphasizes self-reliance as both practical necessity and spiritual discipline. Early Buddhist monasteries in India, China, and Japan maintained gardens not merely for food production but as extensions of meditation practice. The rhythmic, repetitive work of weeding, watering, and harvesting was understood as a form of mindfulness: attention fixed on the present task, the body engaged in purposeful labor, the mind free from abstraction.

In Chinese Chan Buddhism — the precursor to Japanese Zen — the farmer-monk Baizhang Huaihai (720-814 CE) established the principle that became a famous saying: "A day without work is a day without eating." This ethic migrated to Japan with Zen Buddhism and blended with existing agricultural practices at Nara's older temples, which had been cultivating land since the eighth century.

The result was a monastic culture in which farming was not menial labor delegated to servants but a core practice undertaken by monks of all ranks. At many Nara temples, the garden remains the place where senior monks begin each day — hoeing and watering before the morning's first sutra chanting.

### The Precept Against Killing

Buddhist vegetarianism in Japan emerges from the first precept: the prohibition against taking life. While the application of this precept has varied across Buddhist traditions and historical periods — some Japanese sects have permitted fish or meat at various times — the ideal of a plant-based diet has remained the aspirational standard, particularly within monastic contexts.

This precept shaped not only what temples grew but how they grew it. Temple gardening in the Buddhist tradition avoids killing insects whenever possible, preferring manual pest removal over chemical or biological control. Companion planting — growing certain species together to naturally deter pests — is a traditional technique that temple gardeners practiced long before organic farming adopted similar principles.

The relationship between the Buddhist precept and the vegetable garden creates a distinctive agricultural ethic: food production as an act of compassion rather than domination.

## What Grows in Nara's Temple Gardens

### Traditional Temple Vegetables

Nara's temple gardens reflect both Buddhist dietary requirements and the Yamato Province's agricultural heritage. Common crops include:

**Daikon radish** — Perhaps the most important vegetable in temple cuisine, daikon appears in dozens of shojin ryori preparations: simmered, pickled, grated, dried, and fermented. Temple varieties tend to be heirloom cultivars, shorter and more flavorful than the commercial daikon found in supermarkets.

**Eggplant (nasu)** — Nara's summer gardens produce eggplant varieties specific to the Yamato region, including the small, round Yamato-maru-nasu. This heirloom variety, with dense flesh that holds its shape during long simmering, is prized for nitsuke (simmered dishes) in temple kitchens.

**Shiso (perilla)** — Both green and red varieties grow abundantly in temple gardens. Green shiso provides a fresh, bright garnish; red shiso is essential for making umeboshi (pickled plums), a temple kitchen staple. Shiso self-seeds readily and has been present in Nara's gardens since the Nara period itself.

**Gobo (burdock root)** — A staple of temple cooking, gobo provides earthy depth to simmered dishes and is valued in Buddhist dietary traditions for its reputed health benefits. Temple-grown gobo, harvested young and fresh, is markedly superior to the woody specimens often found in commercial markets.

**Kabocha (winter squash)** — Introduced to Japan from Southeast Asia via Portuguese traders, kabocha became a temple garden standard for its keeping quality and nutritional density. Its sweet, dense flesh requires no animal-based enrichment, making it ideal for vegetarian cuisine.

**Leafy greens** — Seasonal greens rotate through temple gardens: komatsuna and mizuna in cooler months, shungiku (chrysanthemum greens) in autumn, and various traditional greens collectively known as nappamono throughout the growing season.

**Yamato traditional vegetables (Yamato yasai)** — Nara Prefecture has officially designated approximately 20 heirloom vegetable varieties as Yamato yasai — traditional vegetables unique to the region. Several of these are maintained in temple gardens where monks have grown them continuously for centuries, preserving genetic diversity that commercial agriculture has abandoned. These include Yamato-mana (a leafy brassica), Shimogouri-uri (a small cucumber), and Tsukinowa-kokabu (a turnip variety).

### Herbs and Aromatics

Temple gardens also maintain herb sections that supply the flavoring agents essential to shojin ryori:

**Myoga** — A ginger relative that produces fragrant, edible flower buds. These appear in temple cuisine as garnishes and pickles, providing a bright, slightly astringent note.

**Sansho (Japanese pepper)** — Both the leaves and the seed pods are used in temple cooking. Young sansho leaves (kinome) are the classic garnish for spring dishes.

**Mitsuba (Japanese parsley)** — A shade-tolerant herb that thrives in the dappled light beneath temple trees. Its clean, celery-like flavor appears in soups and salads.

### Beyond Vegetables: Temple Orchards and Wild Harvesting

Many Nara temples maintain fruit trees — persimmon, plum, citrus — and rely on foraged ingredients from surrounding forests. Mountain vegetables (sansai) such as warabi (bracken fern), zenmai (royal fern), and takenoko (bamboo shoots) supplement garden produce seasonally and are considered among the great delicacies of temple cuisine.

## Shojin Ryori: The Cuisine of the Garden

### Principles

Shojin ryori is structured around constraints that become, in practice, creative catalysts. The prohibition against meat, fish, and — in strict practice — pungent alliums (garlic, onion, green onion, chives, and leek, collectively the "five forbidden pungents" or gokun) forces the cook to extract maximum flavor and satisfaction from a limited palette.

The result is a cuisine of extraordinary refinement. Dashi stock made from kombu seaweed and dried shiitake mushrooms provides umami depth. Tofu and its derivatives — yuba (tofu skin), koya-dofu (freeze-dried tofu), abura-age (fried tofu) — supply protein and textural variety. Seasonal vegetables, prepared with attention to their individual characteristics, provide color, flavor, and nutritional balance.

A formal shojin ryori meal follows the principle of "five flavors, five colors, five methods": sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and umami; white, black, red, yellow, and green; raw, simmered, grilled, steamed, and fried. This framework ensures nutritional completeness and aesthetic harmony within the vegetarian constraint.

### Experiencing Shojin Ryori in Nara

Several Nara temples offer shojin ryori meals to visitors, though arrangements typically must be made in advance. The experience usually includes a quiet dining room overlooking a garden — often the very garden that produced the ingredients on the table.

Yakushi-ji and several temples in the Yoshino mountain area are known for their shojin ryori offerings. Secular restaurants in Naramachi also serve shojin ryori-inspired cuisine, making the tradition accessible even without temple arrangements.

The connection between garden and table is the essential point. In temple cuisine, the concept of "chisan chisho" (local production, local consumption) is not a modern sustainability slogan but a practice as old as the temples themselves.

## Visiting Temple Gardens

### What to Expect

Most temple vegetable gardens are not formal tourist attractions. They are working spaces, and access varies by temple. Some gardens are visible from public pathways and can be admired from a respectful distance. Others are within private temple compounds and require permission to visit.

The most accessible approach is to join a temple experience program (tera-taiken) that includes garden work or cooking instruction. Several Nara-area temples offer seasonal programs where participants help with planting, weeding, or harvesting, followed by a meal prepared from the garden's produce. These programs provide physical engagement with Buddhist agricultural practice that no museum exhibit can replicate.

### Seasonal Calendar

Temple gardens in Nara follow the natural agricultural calendar:

- **Spring (March-May)**: Planting season. Seedbeds are prepared, seeds sown, and young transplants set out. Wild mountain vegetables are harvested from surrounding forests.
- **Summer (June-August)**: Peak growth and harvest of warm-season crops. Gardens are at their most visually abundant.
- **Autumn (September-November)**: Harvest of root vegetables, squash, and late greens. Persimmon and citrus ripen in temple orchards. Preservation work — pickling, drying, fermenting — intensifies.
- **Winter (December-February)**: Quiet season. Cold-hardy greens continue, and preserved foods from autumn sustain the kitchen. Planning for the coming year begins.

## The Broader Significance

Temple vegetable gardens in Nara represent a food system that has operated sustainably for over a millennium — a striking achievement in an era of industrial agriculture and global supply chains. The gardens' continuity demonstrates that small-scale, local, organic food production is not a modern innovation but an ancient practice, maintained through centuries of political upheaval, war, natural disaster, and social transformation.

Guests at Kanoya ryokan who have visited temple gardens often find a deeper appreciation for the kaiseki dinner that follows. While kaiseki is not strictly shojin ryori — it includes fish and meat — the underlying philosophy of seasonal ingredients, careful preparation, and respect for raw materials shares roots with temple cuisine. The vegetables that appear in the ryokan's dishes come from the same Yamato region that has supplied temple kitchens for centuries.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is shojin ryori?

Shojin ryori is traditional Japanese Buddhist vegetarian cuisine, developed in temple kitchens over centuries. It excludes meat, fish, and typically pungent alliums (garlic, onion, etc.), relying instead on seasonal vegetables, tofu, seaweed, and mushrooms. It follows principles of five flavors, five colors, and five cooking methods.

### Can I visit a temple vegetable garden in Nara?

Most temple gardens are working spaces with limited public access. The best approach is to join a temple experience program (tera-taiken) that may include garden work or cooking instruction. Some gardens are visible from public temple pathways. Contact individual temples or Nara tourism offices for current program availability.

### What vegetables are unique to Nara?

Nara Prefecture recognizes approximately 20 heirloom varieties as Yamato yasai (Yamato traditional vegetables), including Yamato-maru-nasu (round eggplant), Yamato-mana (leafy green), Shimogouri-uri (cucumber), and Tsukinowa-kokabu (turnip). Many of these are preserved in temple gardens where they have been grown continuously for centuries.

### Where can I eat shojin ryori in Nara?

*Shojin ryori can be experienced at several Nara temples that offer meals to visitors with advance reservation, including temples in the Yoshino area. Secular restaurants in the Naramachi district also serve shojin ryori-inspired cuisine year-round without reservation requirements. The most meaningful experience combines a temple visit with a meal, allowing guests to see the connection between garden and table that defines this culinary tradition.*

### Is shojin ryori the same as vegan food?

Shojin ryori is plant-based and avoids all animal products, making it compatible with a vegan diet. However, it also traditionally excludes five pungent plants (garlic, onion, green onion, chives, and leek) that are common in Western vegan cooking. The cuisine is motivated by Buddhist ethical principles rather than modern dietary philosophy, and its preparation follows aesthetic and seasonal conventions that distinguish it from general vegan cuisine.
