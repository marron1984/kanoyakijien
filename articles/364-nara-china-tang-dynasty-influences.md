# Nara and China: Tang Dynasty Influences on Japanese Culture

The city of Nara exists because of China. Its grid-plan layout, its temples, its system of government, its writing, its artistic traditions, even the Buddhism that pervades its cultural life — all were imported from or profoundly shaped by Tang Dynasty China (618–907 CE). To walk through Nara is to see the most complete surviving record of Tang cultural influence anywhere in the world, including China itself, where the destructions of subsequent centuries erased much of what Japan preserved.

This is one of the great ironies of East Asian cultural history: the best place to understand Tang Dynasty material culture is not Xi'an (the former Chang'an) but Nara, where Japanese reverence for the past has maintained buildings, objects, and traditions that their Chinese originals lost long ago.

## The Scale of Borrowing

Japan's absorption of Chinese culture during the 7th and 8th centuries was one of the most deliberate and comprehensive acts of cultural borrowing in world history. It was not unconscious assimilation but a planned programme of import and adaptation, driven by the Japanese court's desire to build a civilised state modelled on the most sophisticated empire in the world.

The mechanisms of transfer included:

### Diplomatic Missions (Kentōshi)

Between 630 and 894 CE, Japan sent approximately twenty official embassies to Tang China. These missions — the kentōshi — were enormous undertakings involving hundreds of people: diplomats, monks, scholars, artisans, and students. The sea crossing was perilous (several missions were lost to storms), but the rewards were immense.

Returning envoys brought back books, artworks, religious texts, musical instruments, medicines, and — most importantly — knowledge. Young scholars sent to study in China spent years absorbing Chinese learning before returning to Japan to implement what they had learned. The monk Kūkai (who would later found the Shingon sect) and the scholar Kibi no Makibi are among the most famous returnees, but hundreds of lesser-known individuals contributed to the transfer.

### The Monk Ganjin

The most dramatic individual story of Sino-Japanese cultural exchange is that of Ganjin (Chinese: Jianzhen, 688–763 CE), a distinguished Chinese monk who was invited to Japan to establish proper ordination procedures for Buddhist clergy.

Ganjin attempted the crossing six times over twelve years. He was thwarted by storms, shipwrecks, official obstruction, and illness. By the time he finally reached Japan in 753 CE, he was blind. Despite his blindness, he established Tōshōdai-ji in Nara, which became the centre of the Ritsu (Vinaya) school of Buddhism and remains one of the most beautiful temples in the city.

The dry-lacquer portrait sculpture of Ganjin at Tōshōdai-ji — serene, eyes closed, his face showing the marks of age and suffering — is one of the masterpieces of Japanese art. It is displayed to the public only briefly each year, typically in June around the anniversary of Ganjin's death.

### Immigrant Communities

Chinese and Korean immigrants formed significant communities in Nara-period Japan. Many brought specialised skills — metalworking, weaving, ceramic production, medicine — that contributed directly to the cultural achievements of the period. The genealogical records of the time document numerous immigrant lineages, and their contributions to Japanese civilisation were openly acknowledged.

## What Was Borrowed

### Urban Planning

Heijō-kyō (Nara) was modelled directly on Chang'an, the Tang capital. Both cities used a grid plan with the imperial palace at the north, a central boulevard running south, and residential and commercial blocks (bō) arranged symmetrically. The resemblance was intentional — the Japanese court wanted their capital to look like a proper Chinese-style city.

The differences are telling, however. Nara was smaller than Chang'an. It lacked the massive walls of the Chinese capital. And certain Chinese features — such as the market districts — were adapted to Japanese conditions. The borrowing was not slavish imitation but selective adaptation.

Visitors can see the scale of this borrowed urban vision at the Heijō Palace Site (Heijō-kyū), where the reconstructed Former Imperial Audience Hall and Suzaku Gate give a sense of the Chinese-influenced grandeur the Japanese court aspired to.

### Government and Law

The Ritsuryō system — the legal and administrative framework of the Nara state — was based directly on Chinese models, specifically the Tang legal codes. The system divided the government into ministries, established ranks for officials, organised the population into tax-paying households, and created a centralised bureaucracy that replaced the older clan-based power structure.

Japan adapted the system significantly. The Chinese meritocratic examination system was adopted in theory but largely bypassed in practice — in Japan, birth remained more important than examination performance. The imperial institution, based on hereditary divine authority, was fundamentally different from the Chinese model, where the emperor ruled by the "Mandate of Heaven" and could theoretically be replaced.

### Buddhism

Buddhism reached Japan via Korea and China, and the specific forms of Buddhism practised in Nara were heavily influenced by Chinese doctrinal traditions. The six schools of Nara Buddhism — Sanron, Jōjitsu, Hossō, Kusha, Kegon, and Ritsu — were all Chinese imports, based on Chinese translations of Indian texts and Chinese commentarial traditions.

The physical form of Buddhism in Nara — its temple layouts, its sculptural styles, its ritual practices — was also predominantly Chinese. Tōdai-ji's Great Buddha was inspired by a tradition of monumental Buddhist sculpture that originated in India, passed through Central Asia and China, and reached Japan as the most ambitious expression of state-sponsored Buddhism.

### Art and Architecture

Nara-period art is essentially Tang art filtered through Japanese sensibility. The sculptural techniques — dry lacquer, hollow-core dry lacquer, clay modelling over wooden armatures — were Chinese methods. The painting traditions, though almost entirely lost from this period, were based on Chinese precedents.

Temple architecture followed Chinese models: the pagoda, the main hall (kondō), the lecture hall (kōdō), the gate structures — all derived from Chinese Buddhist architecture. The bracketing systems, roof forms, and spatial arrangements visible at Tōshōdai-ji and Yakushi-ji are direct transmissions of Tang architectural practice.

Here is the crucial point: because China's own wooden architecture from the Tang period has almost entirely been lost (only a handful of Tang-era buildings survive in China), the temples of Nara preserve Tang architectural traditions that China itself cannot show. Tōshōdai-ji's kondō is closer to a Tang original than almost anything surviving on the Chinese mainland.

### Writing and Literature

The Japanese writing system itself is a Chinese import. The Man'yōshū, Japan's first great poetry anthology, was written using Chinese characters (man'yōgana) adapted to represent Japanese sounds. Literary Chinese (kanbun) was the language of government, scholarship, and formal communication throughout the Nara period.

The Chinese literary tradition also shaped Japanese poetry. The five-and-seven-syllable patterns that define Japanese verse were influenced by Chinese poetic models, though the specific forms — tanka, chōka — are Japanese developments.

## What Was Transformed

The most important thing about Japan's borrowing from China is not what was taken but what was done with it. In virtually every domain, Japanese practitioners adapted Chinese models to local conditions, preferences, and needs.

Buddhist sculpture became more naturalistic and emotionally expressive in Japanese hands. Architecture was adapted to Japanese climate and materials. The writing system was eventually supplemented by kana syllabaries that allowed Japanese to be written in its own grammatical structure rather than forced into Chinese patterns. The government system was reshaped by Japanese social realities.

This process of selective adaptation is one of the defining characteristics of Japanese civilisation, and it began in the Nara period. What you see in Nara is not China copied but China transformed — Chinese forms and ideas reworked by Japanese sensibilities into something that is recognisably both Chinese-influenced and distinctly Japanese.

## Experiencing Tang Influence in Nara Today

For travellers interested in the China-Japan connection, several Nara sites are particularly relevant:

**Tōshōdai-ji**: Founded by the Chinese monk Ganjin, this temple preserves the most authentically Tang-influenced architecture in Japan. The kondō is often described as the finest surviving example of Tang-era architectural principles.

**Yakushi-ji**: The East Pagoda (currently the subject of major restoration) dates from the early 8th century and embodies Chinese architectural influence at its most refined.

**Nara National Museum**: The permanent collection and the annual Shōsō-in Exhibition display objects that illustrate the range and depth of continental influence on Nara-period culture.

**Heijō Palace Site**: The reconstructed buildings show the Chinese-inspired scale and formality of the Nara capital's political centre.

Understanding these connections deepens the experience of visiting Nara considerably. Staying overnight — at a traditional inn like Kanoya ryokan — allows the time necessary to visit both the major temples and the museum sites where the China connection is most clearly visible.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Was Nara modelled on a Chinese city?

Yes, Nara (Heijō-kyō) was modelled on Chang'an, the Tang Dynasty capital. Both cities used a grid plan with the palace at the north, though Nara was smaller and adapted the Chinese model to Japanese conditions.

### Who was Ganjin?

Ganjin (688–763 CE) was a Chinese Buddhist monk who made six attempts over twelve years to reach Japan. He finally arrived in 753 CE, blind from his hardships, and founded Tōshōdai-ji in Nara to establish proper Buddhist ordination practices.

### Is Nara's architecture really more Tang Chinese than anything in China?

In some respects, yes. Most Tang-era wooden buildings were destroyed in China over the centuries. Nara's 8th-century temples, particularly Tōshōdai-ji, preserve Tang architectural principles more completely than almost any surviving Chinese building.

### What were the kentōshi missions?

The kentōshi were official Japanese diplomatic embassies to Tang China, sent approximately twenty times between 630 and 894 CE. They brought back knowledge, texts, art, and technologies that shaped Japanese civilisation.
