# Nara's Deer Antler Cutting Ceremony: Shika no Tsunokiri

Every October, in a dusty enclosure within Nara Park, a tradition unfolds that has no equivalent anywhere else in Japan. Men in Edo-period costume chase, wrestle, and restrain wild deer — not to harm them, but to carefully saw off their antlers. The crowd watches from wooden bleachers, caught between amusement and awe, as an animal that moments ago seemed gentle enough to eat from a tourist's hand demonstrates the considerable strength and speed it has possessed all along.

This is Shika no Tsunokiri, the deer antler cutting ceremony, and it has been performed in Nara for over 350 years. It is not a spectacle designed for tourists, though tourists now fill the majority of seats. It is a practical measure with deep historical roots — a ritual that reveals the complex, centuries-long relationship between Nara's human population and the approximately 1,200 wild deer that share their city.

## The History Behind the Ceremony

### Origins in the Edo Period

The ceremony dates to 1671, during the early Edo period. The immediate cause was pragmatic: male deer in autumn rut become aggressive, and their antlers posed a genuine danger to the citizens of Nara. Deer had been considered sacred messengers of the gods since the founding of Kasuga Taisha in 768 CE — killing or injuring a deer was a capital offense well into the Edo period — but living alongside large animals bearing sharp antlers during their most volatile season required a solution.

The answer was a ceremonial cutting. Rather than a clandestine, purely practical operation, the authorities of Kasuga Taisha and the Nara magistrate created a formal event. By making the antler cutting a ceremony, they acknowledged the sacred status of the deer while addressing the safety of the townspeople. The ritual framing transformed a veterinary procedure into an act of reverence.

### TheRole of Kasuga Taisha

The ceremony has always been connected to Kasuga Taisha, the shrine whose deity is said to have arrived in Nara riding a white deer. The shrine's priests participate in the ceremony, offering prayers before the cutting begins. This religious dimension is not superficial decoration added to a practical task — it reflects the foundational belief that Nara's deer are divine messengers, and that any intervention upon their bodies must be conducted with appropriate solemnity.

During the feudal period, the ceremony was a formal affair attended by samurai and officials. The men who captured the deer were skilled in a form of restraint that avoided injury to the animal. After the Meiji Restoration, the ceremony continued under the management of what would eventually become the Nara Deer Preservation Foundation, which today oversees the health and welfare of all deer in Nara Park.

## What Happens During the Ceremony

### The Capture

The ceremony takes place in a temporary enclosure erected in a section of Nara Park, typically near the Tobihino area east of the park's central grasslands. Several male deer are guided into the enclosure before the event begins. When the ceremony starts, men called seko, dressed in the traditional garb of Edo-period deer catchers — happi coats and headbands — enter the enclosure and attempt to capture the deer using only ropes and their own strength.

This is the most dramatic portion of the ceremony. The deer, understandably, do not cooperate. A male sika deer in autumn weighs between 50 and 80 kilograms and can run at considerable speed within the confined space. The seko work in coordinated teams, gradually cornering and then roping the deer. The technique requires athleticism, timing, and genuine courage — the antlers they are attempting to remove are, after all, still attached and sharp.

The capture typically takes several minutes per deer. The crowd watches each attempt with the tension of a sporting event, gasping at near misses and applauding successful captures.

### The Cutting

Once a deer is restrained — held firmly on the ground by multiple seko — a Shinto priest performs a brief blessing. Then a specialist uses a small saw to carefully cut the antlers. This is the moment that concerns some first-time observers, but it is important to understand that antler cutting is painless. Deer antlers, once fully grown, are composed of dead bone covered by a thin layer of velvet that has already dried and peeled away by October. There are no nerves in the hardened antler. The deer may struggle against the restraint, but the cutting itself causes no pain — it is comparable to trimming a fingernail.

After the antlers are removed, the deer is released back into the enclosure and eventually into the park. It trots away, visibly lighter, and returns to its life of grazing and accepting rice crackers from visitors.

### The Ceremonial Framework

Between captures, priests offer prayers, and announcers explain the historical context to the audience. The entire event typically involves the cutting of antlers from several deer over the course of one to two hours. The pace is unhurried — this is a ceremony, not a production line. Each deer receives individual attention and ritual acknowledgment.

## When and Where to Attend

### Dates and Timing

Shika no Tsunokiri is held on designated days in October, typically over a long weekend or consecutive days spanning a Saturday, Sunday, and sometimes a Monday holiday. The exact dates vary by year and are announced by the Nara Deer Preservation Foundation in late summer. In recent years, sessions have been held at approximately 12:00 noon, with the enclosure opening to spectators about an hour before.

### Getting There

The ceremony is held in Nara Park, accessible on foot from Kintetsu Nara Station in about 15 minutes or from JR Nara Station in about 25 minutes. The specific location within the park varies slightly from year to year but is always well signposted during the event period. Follow the crowds and the signs reading 鹿の角きり.

### Seating and Viewing

Seating is on temporary wooden bleachers surrounding the enclosure. Tickets are typically required and can be purchased at the gate on the day of the event for a modest fee (around 1,000 yen in recent years). Seats fill quickly, especially on weekends, so arriving at least 45 minutes before the scheduled start is advisable. Standing room around the periphery offers a less optimal but still adequate view.

### Photography

The ceremony offers excellent photographic opportunities, though the action is fast and unpredictable. A telephoto lens in the 100-200mm range is ideal for capturing the drama of the chase from the bleachers. The light in October is typically warm and directional — afternoon sessions benefit from golden autumn light that enhances the already cinematic scene.

## The Deer of Nara: Context for the Ceremony

Understanding the ceremony requires understanding the deer themselves. Nara's approximately 1,200 sika deer are classified as a National Natural Treasure. They are not domesticated, not pets, and not livestock — they are wild animals that have, over the course of more than a millennium, developed an unusual tolerance for human proximity. This tolerance should not be mistaken for tameness. During the autumn rut, male deer can be unpredictable and occasionally dangerous, which is precisely why the antler cutting ceremony exists.

The deer's sacred status derives from the mythology of Kasuga Taisha. According to tradition, the deity Takemikazuchi arrived in Nara from Kashima in present-day Ibaraki Prefecture, riding a white deer. From that founding myth, all deer in the Nara area came to be regarded as divine messengers. For centuries, the penalty for killing a deer was death — a law that was enforced with documented severity.

This sacred status created a unique urban ecosystem. While deer in other Japanese cities were hunted or driven away, Nara's deer were protected and, over generations, lost much of their natural wariness of humans. The relationship is genuinely symbiotic: the deer benefit from the protection and the supplementary feeding that the Deer Preservation Foundation provides, while the city benefits from an attraction that draws millions of visitors annually.

## Cultural Significance Beyond the Practical

The ceremony endures not merely because antlers still need cutting — though they do — but because it enacts something important about Nara's identity. It is a public demonstration of the covenant between the city and its deer. By conducting the cutting as a ceremony rather than a veterinary operation, Nara affirms that the deer are not merely animals to be managed but sacred beings whose bodies deserve ritual respect.

This philosophy extends to all aspects of deer management in Nara. Injured deer are treated at a dedicated facility. Pregnant does are temporarily housed in a protected enclosure during fawning season. The annual deer census is conducted with meticulous care. The antler cutting ceremony is the most visible expression of a year-round commitment to coexistence.

## Combining the Ceremony with Your Nara Visit

October is an ideal month to visit Nara for reasons beyond the ceremony. The autumn foliage begins its transformation, the summer heat has broken, and the cultural calendar is rich with events. Visitors who time their trip for the antler cutting can combine it with visits to temples and shrines that are particularly beautiful in early autumn light.

Staying in the area allows you to attend morning sessions without rushing from Osaka or Kyoto. Kanoya ryokan offers a traditional base from which the walk to Nara Park is both short and scenic, passing through the historic streets of Naramachi — a particularly pleasant route in the cool October mornings.

## Practical Tips

- **Bring binoculars** if you want to observe the cutting procedure in detail from the bleachers
- **Wear comfortable shoes** — you will be walking through parkland to reach the venue and may stand for extended periods
- **Arrive early** to secure good seats, especially on weekends
- **Respect the ceremony** by refraining from flash photography and excessive noise during the Shinto blessings
- **Check current dates** with the Nara Deer Preservation Foundation website or at your accommodation, as schedules are confirmed annually

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does the antler cutting hurt the deer?

No. By October, deer antlers are fully hardened bone with no blood supply or nerve endings. The cutting is painless, comparable to trimming a human fingernail. The deer may resist the restraint, but the actual sawing causes no discomfort.

### How much do tickets cost?

Tickets are typically around 1,000 yen per person and can be purchased at the entrance on the day of the event. Prices may vary slightly from year to year.

### Is the ceremony suitable for children?

Yes, though very young children may find the chase and restraint of the deer distressing. Children old enough to understand the explanation — that the procedure is painless and beneficial to the deer — generally find it fascinating.

### Do the antlers grow back?

Yes. Deer shed their antlers naturally every spring and regrow them over the summer. The cycle repeats annually, which is why the ceremony is held every year.

### How long does the ceremony last?

Approximately one to two hours, depending on how many deer are processed and how quickly each capture proceeds.

### When is the best time to visit Nara for the ceremony?

*Shika no Tsunokiri is held over several days in October, typically on a weekend. The best strategy is to check the Nara Deer Preservation Foundation's annual schedule, arrive a day early to explore the park and temples, and attend a weekday session if possible for smaller crowds and better seating.*
