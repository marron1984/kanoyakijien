# Understanding Buddhist Statues: An Iconography Guide for Nara Temples

Nara contains the highest concentration of significant Buddhist statuary in Japan. The city's temples house works spanning thirteen centuries, from the austere bronze figures of the seventh century to the refined wooden sculptures of the Kamakura period. For many visitors, however, these statues remain beautiful but opaque — impressive in scale and craftsmanship, but mysterious in meaning. Without an iconographic vocabulary, one golden figure looks much like another.

This is a loss, because Buddhist statues are not arbitrary. Every element — the hand position, the facial expression, the objects held, the number of arms, the posture, the attendant figures — communicates specific information about the deity's identity, function, and relationship to the practitioner. The statues are, in effect, a visual language. Learning even the basics of this language transforms temple visits from passive admiration to active reading.

This guide provides the fundamental iconographic vocabulary needed to identify and understand the major categories of Buddhist statues you will encounter in Nara's temples.

## The Four Categories

Buddhist statuary in Japan falls into four hierarchical categories, each with distinctive visual characteristics.

### 1. Nyorai (Tathagata) — The Buddhas

Nyorai are fully enlightened beings who have completed the path to Buddhahood. They are depicted in the simplest manner: unadorned robes, no jewellery, no crowns, no elaborate accessories. Their simplicity reflects their transcendence of worldly attachment.

**Visual identification:**
- Simple monastic robes draped over both shoulders or one shoulder
- Tightly curled hair (representing the 108 snail-shell curls of the historical Buddha)
- A bump on the crown of the head (ushnisha), symbolising supreme wisdom
- An urna (small dot or jewel) between the eyebrows, representing spiritual vision
- Elongated earlobes (from the weight of princely earrings renounced when Siddhartha became an ascetic)

**Key Nyorai in Nara:**

**Rushana Butsu (Vairocana) at Todaiji:** The Great Buddha of Nara — a sixteen-metre seated bronze figure representing the Cosmic Buddha, the embodiment of the entire Buddhist universe. The right hand is raised in the abhaya mudra (fear-dispelling gesture); the left hand is open in the varada mudra (wish-granting gesture). This is the single most famous Buddhist statue in Japan.

**Yakushi Nyorai (Medicine Buddha) at Yakushiji and Shin-Yakushiji:** Identified by the medicine jar (yakko) held in the left hand. Yakushi is the Buddha of healing, and his temples were centres of physical and spiritual medicine. The Yakushi triad at Yakushiji — the central figure flanked by Nikko (Sunlight) and Gakko (Moonlight) bodhisattvas — is one of the finest bronze ensembles from the Nara period.

**Shaka Nyorai (Shakyamuni) at various temples:** The historical Buddha, Siddhartha Gautama. Often depicted in the moment of enlightenment, seated in meditation with both hands in the lap (dhyana mudra) or with the right hand touching the earth (bhumisparsha mudra, "earth-touching" — calling the earth to witness his enlightenment).

**Amida Nyorai (Amitabha) at various temples:** The Buddha of the Western Paradise. Commonly depicted with both hands forming the jo-in (meditation mudra) or with one or both hands in the raigo-in gesture — welcoming the faithful to the Pure Land. The exact finger configurations of Amida's hand gestures indicate nine grades of rebirth.

### 2. Bosatsu (Bodhisattva) — The Compassionate Beings

Bosatsu are beings who have attained enlightenment but delay their own entry into Nirvana to help all sentient beings. Unlike the austere Nyorai, Bosatsu are depicted with elaborate ornamentation — crowns, jewellery, scarves, flowing garments — reflecting their continued engagement with the worldly realm.

**Visual identification:**
- Ornate crowns and jewellery
- Flowing robes and scarves
- Youthful, idealized appearance
- Often hold symbolic objects (lotus, jewel, water vessel)
- May have multiple arms or heads (indicating their vast compassion and power)

**Key Bosatsu in Nara:**

**Kannon (Avalokiteshvara):** The Bodhisattva of Compassion, by far the most commonly depicted bosatsu. Kannon appears in many forms:
- **Sho Kannon (Sacred Kannon):** The simplest form, holding a lotus bud. The standing Kannon at Yakushiji's Toindo hall is a sublime example.
- **Juichimen Kannon (Eleven-headed Kannon):** Multiple small heads atop the main head, each facing a different direction to perceive suffering in all realms. The Juichimen Kannon at Shorinji temple is a designated National Treasure.
- **Senju Kannon (Thousand-armed Kannon):** Multiple arms radiating outward, each holding a different implement for saving beings. The standing Senju Kannon at Toshodaiji, carved from a single block of wood, is among the most powerful examples in Japan.

**Miroku (Maitreya):** The Future Buddha — the Bodhisattva who will become the next Buddha. Often depicted seated in contemplation, with one leg crossed over the other and one hand raised to the cheek in a thinking pose. The Miroku figures at Chuguji (near Horyuji) are among the most celebrated sculptures in Japanese art.

**Monju (Manjushri):** The Bodhisattva of Wisdom, typically depicted riding a lion and holding a sword (to cut through ignorance) and a sutra scroll.

**Jizo (Kshitigarbha):** The guardian of children and travellers, uniquely depicted as a shaven-headed monk rather than an adorned prince. Jizo holds a shakujo (monk's staff with rings) and a wish-fulfilling jewel. Small Jizo statues, often dressed in red bibs and caps, are found throughout Nara — along roadsides, at temple entrances, and in cemeteries.

### 3. Myoo (Vidyaraja) — The Wrathful Kings

Myoo are fierce, wrathful manifestations of the Buddhas' power, deployed to combat evil and protect the dharma. Their terrifying appearance is not malevolent but protective — they frighten demons and obstacles to enlightenment.

**Visual identification:**
- Fierce, scowling expressions with bared fangs
- Flaming aureoles (representing the destruction of passion)
- Weapons — swords, ropes, tridents
- Dynamic, aggressive postures
- Multiple arms and heads in some forms

**Key Myoo in Nara:**

**Fudo Myoo (Acala):** The most important Myoo, identifiable by the sword in the right hand (to cut attachment), the rope in the left (to bind evil), and the flames behind the figure. Fudo is typically depicted standing or seated on a rock, with a fierce grimace — one eye looking up, one down. Important Fudo statues are found at several Nara temples.

### 4. Ten (Deva) — The Guardian Figures

Ten are protective deities drawn from Hindu mythology and incorporated into Buddhist cosmology as guardians of the dharma, the temples, and the faithful. They are depicted in armour and aggressive postures, standing guard.

**Visual identification:**
- Armour and military dress
- Weapons and shields
- Vigorous, dynamic poses
- Often standing on defeated demons
- Fierce but less extreme expressions than Myoo

**Key Ten in Nara:**

**Shitenno (Four Heavenly Kings):** Four armoured guardians stationed at the cardinal points around a central Buddha. Each has distinctive attributes: Jikokuten (East) holds a sword; Zochoten (South) holds a spear or trident; Komokuten (West) holds a brush and scroll or a small pagoda; Tamonten/Bishamonten (North) holds a pagoda and spear. Outstanding Shitenno sets are found at Todaiji (Kaidan-in), Horyuji, and Kofukuji.

**Nio (Two Kings):** The muscular guardian figures flanking temple gates. One figure has an open mouth (pronouncing "a," the first sound), the other a closed mouth (pronouncing "un," the last sound) — together representing the beginning and end of all things. The Nio at Todaiji's Great South Gate, carved by Unkei and Kaikei in 1203, are Japan's most famous guardian figures.

**Juni Shinsho (Twelve Divine Generals):** Twelve warrior guardians of Yakushi Nyorai, each representing a two-hour period of the day. The set at Shin-Yakushiji, dating from the eighth century, is the finest in Japan — twelve dynamic clay figures arranged in a circle around the central Yakushi, each with a distinctive pose and expression.

## Reading Hand Gestures (Mudra / In)

Hand gestures are the single most useful tool for identifying Buddhist statues. Key mudras include:

- **Semui-in (Abhaya):** Right hand raised, palm outward — "fear not." A gesture of reassurance and protection.
- **Yogan-in (Varada):** Hand lowered, palm outward — wish-granting. Often paired with abhaya in the other hand.
- **Jo-in (Dhyana):** Both hands in the lap, palms up, thumbs touching — meditation.
- **Tenbori-in (Dharmachakra):** Both hands at chest level, fingers forming a wheel shape — "turning the wheel of dharma," representing the Buddha's first sermon.
- **Goma-in (Bhumisparsha):** Right hand reaching down to touch the earth — calling the earth to witness enlightenment.
- **Raigo-in:** Hands raised with thumb and finger touching — the welcoming gesture of Amida Buddha.

## A Practical Viewing Route

For visitors wishing to see the full range of Buddhist iconography in a concentrated area, the following route through central Nara covers the major categories:

1. **Todaiji Nandaimon:** Begin with the Nio guardians (Ten category)
2. **Todaiji Daibutsuden:** The Great Buddha (Nyorai category), flanked by bosatsu
3. **Todaiji Kaidan-in:** The Shitenno set (Ten category) — serene, controlled guardian figures
4. **Shin-Yakushiji:** Yakushi Nyorai with the Juni Shinsho (Nyorai and Ten categories)
5. **Kofukuji National Treasure Hall:** A comprehensive survey including the Ashura (a multi-armed guardian figure and arguably Nara's most popular statue)
6. **Yakushiji:** The Yakushi triad (Nyorai and Bosatsu categories)

This route can be completed in a full day. Visitors staying in the Naramachi area — at Kanoya ryokan, for instance — can split it over two days, which allows more time at each temple and avoids the fatigue that diminishes close observation.

## Materials and Techniques

Understanding what the statues are made of adds another dimension to viewing:

- **Bronze (kondou):** The earliest major Nara statues, including the Great Buddha, were cast in bronze and gilded. The technique came from Korea and China.
- **Dry lacquer (kanshitsu):** Layers of lacquer-soaked cloth built up over a clay core, which is later removed. Produces lightweight, detailed figures. The Ashura at Kofukuji is a supreme example.
- **Wood (mokuzou):** Became dominant from the Heian period onward. Japanese cypress (hinoki) is the preferred material. Single-block (ichiboku) and joined-block (yosegi) techniques each produce different aesthetic qualities.
- **Clay (sozou):** The Juni Shinsho at Shin-Yakushiji are unfired clay over wooden armatures — a fragile medium that makes their survival for thirteen centuries remarkable.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which Nara temple has the best Buddhist statues?

Kofukuji's National Treasure Hall offers the widest variety in a single location, including the beloved Ashura figure. For single iconic images, the Great Buddha at Todaiji is unmatched in scale, and the Yakushi triad at Yakushiji is unsurpassed in bronze casting quality.

### Why do some Buddhist statues have many arms?

Multiple arms represent the deity's vast capacity to help sentient beings simultaneously. Each arm typically holds a different implement — a tool for addressing a specific form of suffering. The Senju (thousand-armed) Kannon is the most common multi-armed figure.

### Are visitors allowed to photograph Buddhist statues in Nara?

Policies vary by temple. Todaiji generally permits photography inside the Daibutsuden. Kofukuji's National Treasure Hall and many other treasure halls prohibit photography to protect the works. Always check signage at each location.

### What is the oldest Buddhist statue in Nara?

The Shaka triad at Horyuji, created by the sculptor Tori Busshi in 623 CE, is among the oldest precisely dated Buddhist sculptures in Japan. The temple also houses the Kudara Kannon, whose exact date is debated but is of similar antiquity.
