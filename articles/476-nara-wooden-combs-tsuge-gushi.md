# Japanese Wooden Combs: Tsuge-gushi Craft in Nara

Among the quiet refinements of Japanese material culture, few objects embody the principle of functional beauty as perfectly as the tsuge-gushi — the boxwood comb. Carved from dense, fine-grained wood, finished to a silk-smooth surface, and designed to last a lifetime with proper care, the Japanese wooden comb represents a craft tradition in which utility and aesthetics are completely inseparable. Nara, with its long history as a center for woodworking and traditional manufacture, remains one of the most important production areas for these combs, and one of the best places in Japan to find and purchase them.

The tsuge-gushi is not a museum piece or a souvenir novelty. It is a working tool, used daily by women across Japan for its genuinely superior performance in caring for hair. That this functional excellence comes wrapped in extraordinary craftsmanship is what makes the object special — and what makes the tradition worth understanding.

## The Material: Satsuma Tsuge

### Why Boxwood

The Japanese boxwood tree — tsuge (Buxus microphylla var. japonica) — produces wood of remarkable density and uniformity. Its grain is so fine as to be almost invisible, and its texture, once polished, approaches the smoothness of ivory. These physical properties make it ideal for comb making: the teeth can be cut extremely thin without breaking, and the smooth surface glides through hair without snagging, pulling, or generating the static electricity that plagues plastic combs.

The finest tsuge wood comes from Kagoshima Prefecture in southern Kyushu, where the warm climate produces slow-growing trees with especially dense, even-grained timber. This Satsuma tsuge, named after the old provincial name, is considered the premium material for comb making and commands prices that reflect its scarcity. A single trunk may take decades to reach the size needed for comb blanks, and the best wood is air-dried for years before being worked.

### Preparation of the Wood

The preparation of tsuge wood for comb making is a process measured in years rather than days. After cutting, the timber is rough-shaped into blocks and then submerged in camellia oil — tsubaki-abura — for an extended period. This oil treatment serves multiple purposes: it stabilizes the wood against warping and cracking, deepens its honey-gold color, and imparts the natural luster that distinguishes a fine tsuge comb from an ordinary wooden one.

After the oil treatment, the blocks are air-dried slowly in a controlled environment. Some craftsmen maintain stocks of prepared wood that have been seasoned for a decade or more, believing that the longer the preparation, the finer the finished product. This patient approach to material preparation — investing years of time before any shaping begins — is characteristic of Japanese craft traditions and helps explain why authentic tsuge-gushi carry significant price tags.

## The Craft of Comb Making

### Hand-Cut Teeth

The defining skill of comb making is cutting the teeth. In a hand-crafted tsuge-gushi, each tooth is individually sawn using a fine-bladed saw, then shaped and polished by hand. The spacing between teeth must be perfectly uniform — any irregularity will catch and pull hair — and the taper from base to tip must be precise to allow the comb to slide smoothly through different hair textures.

A skilled comb maker can cut teeth with spacing as narrow as one millimeter, producing what is called a fine-tooth comb (megurigushi) used for removing oils and debris from hair — a traditional alternative to washing in an era before daily shampooing was common. Wider-toothed combs (sukigushi) are designed for detangling and styling, and their teeth are shaped with a subtle curve that follows the natural arc of the head.

The tooth-cutting process is unforgiving. A single slip of the saw can ruin a comb that represents hours of work on expensive material. This is why experienced comb makers are rare and why their products, even at premium prices, represent genuine value relative to the skill and time involved.

### Shaping and Finishing

Beyond the teeth, the body of the comb is shaped to fit comfortably in the hand, with a gentle curve along the spine and rounded edges that feel natural against the palm. Decorative combs may feature carved or painted designs — flowers, waves, seasonal motifs — but the highest-quality functional combs are often plain, allowing the beauty of the wood itself to serve as the only ornament.

The finishing process involves progressive polishing with increasingly fine materials, from sandpaper to polishing cloths infused with camellia oil. The goal is a surface so smooth that it feels almost liquid to the touch, with a deep, warm sheen that develops further with use over the years. A well-maintained tsuge comb darkens gradually from honey gold to a rich amber, acquiring a patina that reflects its owner's years of use — a quality the Japanese call tsuya, the luster of long care.

## Combs in Japanese Culture

### Hair Culture and the Comb

The comb occupies a central place in Japanese cultural history. In a civilization that elevated hair styling to an art form — the elaborate coiffures of Heian court ladies, the architectural updos of Edo-period courtesans, the precise shimada styles of geisha — the comb was an essential tool, and often a significant personal possession.

During the Edo period, comb collecting became a refined pursuit among wealthy townswomen, and combs were crafted from exotic materials including tortoiseshell, lacquered wood, ivory, and gold-inlaid metal. These ornamental combs, worn as hair accessories, were expressions of personal taste and social status. The tsuge-gushi, by contrast, was the working comb — the tool used in the daily toilet, kept in the sleeve or cosmetic box, valued for performance rather than display.

This functional tradition is the one that survives most vigorously today. While ornamental combs have become collectors' items and museum pieces, the everyday tsuge-gushi continues to be made, sold, and used by women who prefer its performance to any modern alternative.

### Superstitions and Symbolism

Combs carry symbolic weight in Japanese culture. The Japanese word for comb — kushi — is a homophone for the characters meaning "suffering" and "death" (ku and shi), making combs inauspicious as gifts in some traditional contexts. Picking up a dropped comb is considered unlucky, and combs are sometimes deliberately broken at funerals as a symbolic severance of worldly attachments.

Despite these superstitions — or perhaps because of them — combs that are kept and cared for over many years acquire a particular emotional significance. A mother's comb, passed to a daughter, carries the accumulated meaning of daily use, of the intimate ritual of combing hair, of the continuity between generations that the object embodies.

## Nara's Comb Making Heritage

### Historical Context

Nara's connection to comb making is rooted in the city's broader woodworking heritage. As an ancient capital and major temple town, Nara developed a concentrated community of skilled woodworkers who served both religious and secular markets. The same precision that produced Buddhist sculptures and temple fittings was applied to smaller objects — brushes, boxes, tools, and combs — that required fine-grained wood and meticulous craftsmanship.

The Naramachi merchant district, which grew up around the temples in the medieval period, became a center for the retail sale of these craft products, and it retains that character today. Several shops in Naramachi specialize in traditional combs and hair accessories, offering both locally made products and curated selections from craftsmen elsewhere in Japan.

### Where to Buy Tsuge-gushi in Nara

The most reliable sources for authentic tsuge-gushi in Nara are the long-established craft shops in Naramachi and along the approach roads to major temples. Look for shops that specialize in traditional personal accessories — combs, brushes, mirrors — rather than general souvenir shops, which may carry machine-made imitations.

When evaluating a tsuge comb, hold it up to the light and examine the teeth. Hand-cut teeth show subtle individual variations; machine-cut teeth are perfectly identical. Run your thumb along the spine — a well-finished comb will feel seamlessly smooth. Genuine Satsuma tsuge has a warm, golden tone and a fine, almost invisible grain pattern. The price is also a guide: a hand-crafted tsuge-gushi from quality material typically starts at several thousand yen for a small comb and can reach well over ten thousand yen for larger, finer pieces.

## Using and Caring for a Tsuge Comb

### Daily Use

A tsuge comb is used by drawing it through dry or slightly damp hair with slow, steady strokes from roots to tips. The camellia oil that saturates the wood transfers in minute quantities to the hair with each stroke, adding natural luster and moisture without chemicals. Over time, daily use of a tsuge comb can genuinely improve hair condition — an effect that users report with the consistency of a folk remedy that actually works.

The comb should not be used on wet, tangled hair, which places excessive stress on the teeth. A wide-toothed comb or fingers should detangle wet hair first; the tsuge comb is for finishing and daily maintenance.

### Maintenance

Care for a tsuge comb is simple but important. Clean it periodically by wiping with a soft cloth moistened with camellia oil, using a toothpick or fine brush to remove any debris between the teeth. Avoid water — prolonged moisture can swell and warp the wood. Store the comb in a cloth pouch or case, away from heat and direct sunlight.

With proper care, a tsuge comb will last decades and improve with age. The wood deepens in color, the surface develops a richer patina, and the teeth — if they have not been subjected to abuse — retain their precision indefinitely. A well-used tsuge comb is an object that becomes more beautiful, not less, as time passes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a tsuge-gushi?
A tsuge-gushi is a traditional Japanese comb made from boxwood (tsuge). These combs are hand-crafted from dense, fine-grained wood that has been treated with camellia oil, producing a smooth, static-free comb that is prized for its hair care benefits and lasting beauty.

### Why are tsuge combs so expensive?
Authentic tsuge combs are expensive because they require premium boxwood that takes decades to grow, years of preparation including oil treatment and seasoning, and skilled hand-craftsmanship for cutting and finishing. Each tooth is individually sawn and polished, making the production process labor-intensive.

### Are wooden combs better for hair?
Many users report that tsuge wooden combs improve hair condition over time. The camellia oil in the wood transfers naturally to hair during combing, adding moisture and shine. Unlike plastic combs, tsuge combs do not generate static electricity, reducing frizz and flyaway hair.

### Where can I buy a tsuge comb in Nara?
Naramachi, Nara's historic merchant district, has several shops specializing in traditional combs and personal accessories. Look for established craft shops rather than general souvenir stores, and examine the comb closely for signs of hand craftsmanship such as individually varied teeth and seamless finishing.
