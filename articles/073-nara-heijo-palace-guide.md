# Heijo Palace: Walking the Grounds of Nara's Ancient Imperial Capital

West of central Nara, a vast open field marks the site of Japan's first permanent imperial capital. Heijo-kyo was established in 710 CE as the seat of government, modelled on the Tang Dynasty capital of Chang'an, and served as the political centre of Japan until 784 CE. For 74 years, this was the heart of the most ambitious civilisation-building project in Japanese history — the place from which emperors governed, laws were written, temples were commissioned, and the foundations of Japanese culture were laid.

Today, the Heijo Palace site (Heijo-kyu) is a UNESCO World Heritage Site covering approximately 130 hectares — a remarkable expanse of open ground within a modern city, where reconstructed buildings rise from a landscape of excavated foundations and carefully maintained grassland. For visitors who have seen Nara's great temples and want to understand the political context in which they were created, the palace site provides the essential complement.

## What Was Heijo-kyo?

### The Capital

Heijo-kyo was a planned city of approximately 4.8km east-west by 4.8km north-south, laid out on a strict grid pattern modelled on Chang'an. At its peak, the city housed an estimated 100,000–200,000 people — a remarkable population for an 8th-century settlement.

The imperial palace compound occupied the northern centre of the grid, symbolically and politically positioned as the apex of the entire capital. Government offices, ceremonial halls, and the emperor's private quarters were contained within walled precincts. South of the palace, the city extended in a grid of residential blocks, markets, temples, and arterial roads.

### The Palace

The Heijo Palace compound itself covered approximately 120 hectares — far larger than most visitors expect. It contained:

- **Daigoku-den** (Great Audience Hall): The principal ceremonial building, where the emperor received officials and conducted state ceremonies
- **Chodo-in** (Imperial Audience Compound): A complex of 12 buildings surrounding a central courtyard, used for official government business
- **Suzaku-mon** (Vermillion Bird Gate): The main southern gate of the palace compound
- **Various government offices**: Including the ministries that administered the Japanese state
- **Gardens and residential quarters**: Including the emperor's private spaces

## What to See Today

### Reconstructed Buildings

**Daigoku-den (Great Audience Hall)**: Completed in 2010 on its original foundations, this is the site's centrepiece. The building is enormous — over 44 metres wide and 20 metres deep — with vermillion columns, a tiled roof, and an interior that conveys the ceremonial grandeur of the Nara court. Inside, a reproduction of the imperial throne sits beneath a painted ceiling, giving visitors a sense of the scale and formality of 8th-century state ritual.

**Suzaku-mon (Vermillion Bird Gate)**: Reconstructed in 1998, this imposing two-storey gate marks the formal southern entrance to the palace compound. Walking through it, as officials and visitors would have done 1,300 years ago, provides a physical sense of arrival at the seat of power.

**Toin Teien (East Palace Garden)**: A reconstructed garden based on archaeological excavation, featuring a winding stream, bridges, and plantings that evoke the pleasure gardens of the Nara court. These gardens were the settings for poetry-composing parties and banquets — the social rituals that accompanied governance.

### Archaeological Remains

Much of the palace site remains as excavated foundations — stone bases and earthen platforms that indicate where buildings once stood. These open-field remains require imagination to appreciate but are, in some ways, more evocative than the reconstructions. Walking across the vast grounds, seeing the scale of the original compound laid out in foundation stones, conveys the ambition of the Nara-period state in a way that no single building can.

Ongoing archaeological excavation continues at the site. Visitors may encounter active digs, usually visible from designated viewing areas.

### The Museum

The Heijo Palace Site Museum (Heijo-kyu Izanai-kan), located near the Suzaku Gate, provides context for the site through:

- Scale models showing the complete palace as it appeared in the 8th century
- Archaeological finds: pottery, tiles, wooden tablets (mokkan), and other objects recovered from the site
- Explanatory panels (English and Japanese) covering the site's history and significance
- A reconstruction of an official's workspace, showing the administrative environment of the Nara government

The museum is free and provides the background knowledge that makes a walk across the site meaningful rather than merely pleasant.

## Visiting Heijo Palace

### Getting There

The site is approximately 2km west of Kintetsu Nara Station. Options:

- **Bus**: Routes from Kintetsu Nara Station or JR Nara Station (approximately 15 minutes)
- **Walk**: 25–30 minutes from central Nara — a flat, straightforward route
- **Train**: Yamato-Saidaiji Station (Kintetsu Line) is adjacent to the site's western edge
- **Bicycle**: Rental bicycles from the station area make the trip easy and pleasant

### Hours and Admission

- **Grounds**: Open 24 hours (though buildings close in the evening)
- **Daigoku-den and Suzaku-mon**: 9:00am–4:30pm
- **Museum**: 10:00am–6:00pm (varies seasonally)
- **Admission**: Free for all areas and buildings
- **Closed**: Mondays for some facilities; grounds always accessible

### Time Required

Allow 90 minutes to 2 hours for a meaningful visit — museum, Suzaku Gate, walk to Daigoku-den, garden visit. The site is large, and the walk between buildings takes time.

### Best Conditions

The site is entirely outdoors (except the museum and building interiors), so weather affects the experience:
- **Spring and autumn**: The most comfortable temperatures for walking the grounds
- **Summer**: Very hot and exposed — visit in early morning or late afternoon, bring water and sun protection
- **Winter**: Cold but atmospheric — the stark, open landscape has a melancholy beauty
- **Rain**: The museum provides shelter, but the outdoor experience suffers

## Historical Significance

### Scale of Ambition

The Heijo Palace site forces a recalibration of expectations about 8th-century Japan. This was not a modest court in a provincial setting — it was a major imperial capital, deliberately designed to rival the greatest cities of Asia. The scale of the compound, the sophistication of the grid planning, and the quality of the archaeological finds all testify to a state that was ambitious, organised, and internationally connected.

### Democratic Governance

The wooden tablets (mokkan) recovered from the site — over 100,000 of them — provide a remarkable window into the daily operations of the Nara government: tax records, personnel assignments, supply orders, legal decisions. These mundane documents reveal a functioning bureaucracy managing a complex state — a far cry from the romantic image of Japanese history as a sequence of warriors and aesthetic refinements.

### Living Heritage Connection

The palace site connects directly to the temples that visitors see in central Nara. Todai-ji was commissioned by the same government that operated from this compound. Kasuga Taisha served the same aristocratic families. The artistic masterpieces in the temples were created by workshops funded and supervised from these offices. Understanding the palace provides the political and administrative context for the cultural achievements that make Nara famous.

For travellers staying in Naramachi at accommodation like Kanoya, the palace site makes an excellent half-day excursion — a morning visit followed by lunch in central Nara provides both historical depth and the satisfying contrast between the capital's vanished political grandeur and the temples' enduring spiritual presence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Heijo Palace worth visiting?

For historically curious travellers, yes. The site provides essential context for understanding Nara's temples and the civilisation that created them. The Daigoku-den reconstruction is impressive, and the museum is informative.

### How does it compare to temples like Todai-ji?

It is a completely different experience — archaeological and historical rather than artistic and spiritual. The two complement each other perfectly.

### Is the site accessible?

The grounds are flat and mostly accessible. The museum and reconstructed buildings have accessible entrances.

### Can I combine it with other sites?

The palace site pairs well with a visit to Yakushi-ji and Toshodai-ji in western Nara, creating a full day of Nara-period heritage.
