# Nara's Ancient Roads: Walking the Yamanobe no Michi Trail

There are roads in Nara Prefecture that were already old when the Great Buddha was new. The Yamanobe no Michi — the "trail along the mountain's edge" — runs along the eastern foothills of the Nara Basin, following a route that was first described in the Kojiki, Japan's oldest surviving chronicle, compiled in 712 CE. By that reckoning, this is the oldest recorded road in Japan. But the path itself is older still. It was walked by the emperors who ruled from the Yamato region centuries before Nara became the capital, and by the farmers and priests and merchants who preceded even them.

Today, the Yamanobe no Michi offers something increasingly rare in Japan: a walk through an ancient, inhabited landscape that has not been significantly altered by modern development. The trail passes through mandarin orange groves, alongside rice paddies, past Shinto shrines that predate Buddhism's arrival in Japan, and through villages where persimmons dry on wooden racks in autumn. There are no ticket gates, no crowds, and no gift shops. There is only the path, the mountains rising to the east, the basin spreading to the west, and a silence broken by birdsong and the occasional passing farmer on a small truck.

## Understanding the Route

### Geography and Overview

The Yamanobe no Michi runs roughly north-south along the eastern edge of the Nara Basin, following the base of the mountains that form the basin's eastern wall. The most commonly walked section stretches approximately 16 kilometers from Tenri in the north to Sakurai in the south, though the trail extends further in both directions. Most walkers complete the Tenri-to-Sakurai section in five to seven hours, including stops at shrines and rest points.

The terrain is gentle. The trail follows the contour of the foothills at a relatively constant elevation, rising and falling modestly as it crosses small valleys and ridges. There are no steep climbs or technical sections. The difficulty is in distance, not elevation — this is a long walk on mostly flat or gently undulating ground.

### Why It Follows the Mountain Edge

The trail's placement along the foothills was not arbitrary. In ancient Japan, the floor of the Nara Basin was marshy and frequently flooded, making direct north-south travel across the lowlands impractical. The foothills offered firm, dry ground above the wetlands while remaining below the steep, forested mountains. The Yamanobe no Michi was, in effect, the only practical north-south route through the region — a natural highway dictated by topography.

This geography also explains the concentration of ancient shrines along the route. The foothills were the interface between the cultivated lowlands and the wild mountains, between the human world and the realm of the gods who were believed to dwell on the peaks. Shrines were placed along this boundary to mediate between the two realms — a function they continue to serve, in a diminished but still perceptible way, today.

## Key Sites Along the Trail

### Isonokami Jingu

Near the northern terminus at Tenri, Isonokami Jingu is one of the oldest shrines in Japan. Its origins predate the establishment of the Japanese state, and it served as the imperial armory during the Yamato period. The shrine's treasure, the legendary sword Futsu-no-Mitama, is mentioned in Japan's oldest mythological texts. The grounds are home to a flock of free-ranging chickens — descendants of birds that were traditionally kept at the shrine as sacred animals.

The shrine's atmosphere is markedly different from the polished tourism of central Nara. There is a rawness to Isonokami Jingu — the dense forest, the ancient stones, the sense of a place that was sacred long before anyone built structures on it. Walking the Yamanobe no Michi from here southward, you carry that atmosphere with you.

### Chogaku-ji

A small Buddhist temple set among citrus groves, Chogaku-ji is known for its beautiful garden and its association with the Kamakura-period priest Kenshin. The temple is often quiet, its grounds shaded by old trees, and its modest buildings blend into the landscape in a way that suggests centuries of peaceful coexistence with the surrounding farmland.

### Sasai Shrine

Perched on a hillside with views across the Nara Basin, Sasai Shrine is a small, rarely visited Shinto sanctuary that embodies the character of the Yamanobe no Michi. There are no parking lots, no vending machines, no explanatory signs in multiple languages. There is a steep stone staircase, a pair of guardian foxes, a small wooden hall, and a view that has barely changed in a thousand years.

### Ohmiwa Jinja

The spiritual climax of the southbound walk, Ohmiwa Jinja is one of the most important shrines in Japan. It is unusual in that it has no main hall — the object of worship is Mount Miwa itself, the conical peak that rises directly behind the shrine. This is among the oldest forms of Shinto worship, predating the construction of shrine buildings entirely. The mountain is the deity.

Ohmiwa Jinja's approach through a massive torii gate and along a tree-lined avenue is one of the great sacred walks in Japan. The air changes as you enter the shrine's precincts — cooler, more fragrant, charged with the particular quality that the Japanese call kekkai, the boundary of a sacred zone. The mountain looms behind the shrine, densely forested and off-limits to casual visitors, though designated hiking routes permit partial ascent with appropriate purification.

### Sai Shrine and the Trail's Southern Reaches

South of Ohmiwa Jinja, the trail continues through increasingly rural landscape to Sakurai, passing several smaller shrines and burial mounds. The Sai Shrine, dedicated to the deity of sake brewing, reflects the agricultural character of the region — the rice paddies visible from the trail produce the grain that becomes the region's celebrated sake.

## The Experience of Walking

### What Makes This Walk Different

The Yamanobe no Michi is not a wilderness hike. You will walk through villages, past houses, along farm roads. You will share the path with local residents walking their dogs or cycling to a neighbor's house. You will cross modern roads and pass convenience stores. This is not a manicured heritage trail sealed off from contemporary life.

And that is precisely its value. The Yamanobe no Michi demonstrates that Japan's deep past is not confined to museums and designated cultural properties. It continues to exist, layered beneath and intertwined with the present, in landscapes that have been continuously inhabited for millennia. The rice paddies you pass are planted in fields that have been cultivated since the Yayoi period. The shrines you visit occupy sites that have been sacred since before recorded history. The path beneath your feet has been walked for at least 1,300 years and probably much longer.

### Seasonal Considerations

**Spring (March-May):** Cherry blossoms appear along the trail in early April, and the rice paddies are flooded and planted, creating mirror-like surfaces that reflect the sky. The weather is mild, and the foothills are covered in fresh green.

**Summer (June-August):** Hot and humid. The rice paddies are lush green, and the trail is shaded in many sections by dense vegetation. Start early to avoid the worst heat. Bring adequate water — there are gaps between vending machines and rest stops.

**Autumn (October-November):** The finest season. Persimmons ripen on trees throughout the trail. The rice has been harvested, leaving golden stubble in the paddies. The light is warm and low, ideal for photography. Temperatures are comfortable for sustained walking.

**Winter (December-February):** Cold but clear. The leafless trees open views that are hidden in other seasons. The trail is quiet — you may walk for long stretches without encountering another person. The mountains to the east are sometimes dusted with snow.

## Practical Information

### Getting to the Trailhead

Most walkers start at Tenri and walk south to Sakurai, though the reverse direction is equally viable. Tenri is accessible by JR or Kintetsu from Nara city in approximately 20-30 minutes. Sakurai is similarly connected. Both stations are small but functional, with convenience stores nearby for last-minute supplies.

### Trail Navigation

The Yamanobe no Michi is generally well marked with stone markers and directional signs, many in Japanese only. A map is strongly recommended. The Tenri or Sakurai tourist information offices provide English-language trail maps. GPS navigation apps work reliably along the trail, as cellular coverage is consistent.

Despite good marking, the trail occasionally passes through residential areas where the route can be ambiguous. When in doubt, trend toward the mountains (east) and look for the small stone markers with the characters 山の辺の道.

### What to Bring

Pack as you would for a full day of comfortable walking: sturdy shoes, rain protection, sun protection, and at least one liter of water. The trail passes through several small villages with vending machines and occasional cafes, but distances between services can be significant. A packed lunch from your accommodation is wise — there is no better place to eat an onigiri than on a stone bench beside a two-thousand-year-old shrine.

### Accommodation and the Trail

Day-tripping the Yamanobe no Michi from Nara city is straightforward, as the train connections are quick and frequent. Staying in central Nara at a ryokan such as Kanoya allows you to depart early, walk the trail at a contemplative pace, and return in time for a kaiseki dinner — the ideal conclusion to a day spent in Japan's most ancient landscape.

For those who want more time, it is possible to split the walk over two days, though accommodation options along the trail itself are limited. Most walkers complete the full route in a single day.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does it take to walk the Yamanobe no Michi?

The main section from Tenri to Sakurai is approximately 16 kilometers and takes five to seven hours, depending on pace and the number of stops at shrines and rest points.

### Is the trail difficult?

No. The terrain is gentle, with only modest elevation changes. The challenge is distance rather than difficulty. Anyone in reasonable physical condition who is comfortable walking for a full day can complete the trail.

### Can I walk the trail in reverse (Sakurai to Tenri)?

Yes. Both directions are equally well marked and enjoyable. Walking north from Sakurai places Ohmiwa Jinja near the beginning of your walk rather than the end, which some walkers prefer.

### Is the trail open year-round?

Yes. The Yamanobe no Michi is an open path through inhabited countryside and is accessible in all seasons. Extreme weather may make sections muddy, but the trail is never officially closed.

### Do I need to speak Japanese to walk the trail?

*While Japanese language skills enhance the experience — particularly for reading shrine explanations and interacting with local residents — the Yamanobe no Michi can be walked comfortably with English-language maps available from the Tenri or Sakurai tourist offices, a GPS-equipped smartphone, and the universal language of pointing at items in the small cafes and shops along the route.*
