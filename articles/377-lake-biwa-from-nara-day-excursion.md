# Lake Biwa from Nara: Japan's Largest Lake Day Excursion

Lake Biwa is a body of water that defies easy comparison. Covering 670 square kilometres of Shiga Prefecture, it is Japan's largest lake, its oldest (approximately four million years old, making it one of the oldest lakes on earth), and a presence so vast that it shapes the climate, culture, and economy of the entire region. For travellers based in Nara, a day trip to Lake Biwa's southern or eastern shores offers a dramatic change of scenery — from the landlocked temple city of the ancient capital to the open, light-filled expanse of a great inland sea.

The journey is longer than some day trips from Nara — roughly ninety minutes to two hours by train, depending on your destination — but the rewards are substantial. Lake Biwa's shores are lined with historic castle towns, venerable temples, waterfront villages, and landscapes that have inspired Japanese painters and poets for centuries.

## Understanding Lake Biwa

Lake Biwa is not merely large; it is ecologically and culturally unique. Formed by tectonic activity millions of years ago, it supports over sixty endemic species found nowhere else on earth, including the biwa salmon (biwa masu) and several species of freshwater pearl mussel. The lake has functioned as a vital transportation corridor since antiquity, connecting the capital region to the Japan Sea coast and the provinces beyond. Omi Province, the historic name for the region surrounding the lake, was considered one of the wealthiest and most strategically important in Japan.

The lake also occupies a central place in Japanese aesthetics. The "Eight Views of Omi" (Omi Hakkei), a set of scenic views inspired by Chinese landscape painting traditions, have been celebrated in art and literature since the fifteenth century. These views — evening snow on Mount Hira, autumn moon at Ishiyama, returning sails at Yabase, and others — established Lake Biwa as a canonical landscape in the Japanese artistic imagination.

## Where to Go: Southern and Eastern Shores

The southern and eastern shores of Lake Biwa are the most accessible from Nara and offer the greatest concentration of historical and cultural sites. Three destinations are particularly recommended.

### Otsu

The prefectural capital of Shiga, located at the southwestern corner of the lake, is the closest Lake Biwa destination from Nara. Otsu has served as a port city and administrative centre since the seventh century — it briefly served as Japan's capital under Emperor Tenji in 667 — and it retains a number of significant temples and shrines.

**Mii-dera (Onjo-ji)**: One of the great temples of the Tendai Buddhist school, founded in the seventh century and expanded over the following millennium. The temple complex is vast, sprawling across a hillside above the lake, and its buildings span many architectural periods. The Kondo (Main Hall), a National Treasure, dates to 1599. The temple's bell, one of the "Three Great Bells of Japan," features in numerous legends and literary works. In spring, the temple's cherry blossoms are spectacular, drawing crowds from across the Kansai region.

**Ishiyama-dera**: This lakeside temple, perched on dramatic rock formations, is famous as the place where Murasaki Shikibu is said to have begun writing The Tale of Genji. The temple's main hall is a National Treasure, and the grounds offer views across the lake and the Seta River. The connection to Genji makes Ishiyama-dera a natural pairing with a visit to Uji for literary-minded travellers.

### Omi-Hachiman

Perhaps the most atmospheric town on Lake Biwa's shores, Omi-Hachiman lies on the eastern shore about forty minutes north of Otsu by train. This former castle town and merchant city preserves an extraordinary canal district (Hachiman-bori) where whitewashed storehouses and traditional merchant houses line a waterway bordered by willows and irises. Boat rides along the canal are available and offer a serene perspective on the town's Edo-period architecture.

Omi-Hachiman's merchant heritage is central to its identity. The Omi merchants (Omi shonin) were among the most successful traders in Edo-period Japan, establishing commercial networks that extended across the country. The Omi Merchant Museum and several preserved merchant houses offer insight into the business practices, aesthetic values, and daily life of this remarkable mercantile culture.

Above the town, the ruins of Hachiman-yama Castle occupy a hilltop reached by cable car or a pleasant hiking trail. The summit offers panoramic views of Lake Biwa, the surrounding rice paddies, and the mountains beyond.

### Hikone

Further north along the eastern shore, Hikone is home to one of Japan's only twelve original castle keeps — and one of only five designated as National Treasures. Hikone Castle, completed in 1622 by the Ii clan, lords of the domain throughout the Edo period, is a compact but elegantly proportioned structure whose white walls and curved gables are reflected in the castle moat. The interior retains its original wooden construction, and the top floor offers commanding views of Lake Biwa.

The castle grounds include the Genkyu-en Garden, a beautifully designed Edo-period strolling garden that incorporates views of the castle and, beyond it, Lake Biwa and the distant mountains. The garden is particularly striking in autumn, when the maples surrounding the central pond ignite in colour.

Hikone's castle town streets, while partially modernised, retain sections of Edo-period merchant and samurai architecture. The town also hosts Hikone's beloved mascot, Hikonyan, a samurai cat whose popularity has made him one of Japan's most recognised regional characters.

## Lake Biwa Cuisine

The lake's culinary traditions are distinctive. Funazushi, a fermented crucian carp dish, is one of the oldest forms of sushi in Japan — a direct ancestor of modern sushi that involves preserving the fish in salt and rice for months or even years. The result is pungent and complex, an acquired taste but a genuinely unique culinary experience. Omi beef, raised in the pastures surrounding the lake, is one of Japan's three great wagyu brands (alongside Kobe and Matsuzaka), prized for its delicate marbling and sweet, rich flavour. Freshwater fish from the lake, including ayu (sweetfish) and moroko (a small local species), appear on menus throughout the region, grilled, simmered, or served as tempura.

## Getting There from Nara

**To Otsu**: From JR Nara Station, take the JR Nara Line to Kyoto Station (approximately forty-five minutes), then transfer to the JR Tokaido Line or Kosei Line to Otsu (approximately ten minutes). Total journey: roughly one hour.

**To Omi-Hachiman**: From JR Nara Station, travel via Kyoto Station and then the JR Biwako Line (Tokaido Main Line) to Omi-Hachiman Station. Total journey: approximately ninety minutes.

**To Hikone**: Continue on the JR Biwako Line past Omi-Hachiman to Hikone Station. Total journey from Nara: approximately two hours.

All routes involve a transfer at Kyoto Station, which is straightforward and well signposted.

## Practical Information

**Time needed**: A focused visit to one destination (Otsu, Omi-Hachiman, or Hikone) fills a comfortable day. Combining two is possible but rushed.

**Best time to visit**: Spring brings cherry blossoms to the lakeside temples and castle grounds. Autumn foliage is spectacular at Mii-dera and Genkyu-en Garden. Summer offers lake breezes that temper the heat. Winter brings clear skies and snow-capped mountains reflected in the lake.

**Suggested itinerary**: For a first visit, Omi-Hachiman offers the most complete single-day experience — the canal district, merchant museums, castle ruins, and lakeside scenery combine into a richly layered day.

**Lake access**: Several points along the southern and eastern shores offer lakeside parks, beaches, and walking paths. The Michigan cruise boat operates from Otsu Port and offers sightseeing tours of the southern lake.

## Returning to Nara

The journey back from Lake Biwa passes through Kyoto Station, offering the option of a brief stop in the city — though after a full day on the lake, most travellers prefer the direct return to their accommodation. Guests at Kanoya ryokan, arriving back in the evening after a day on Lake Biwa's expansive shores, find that the ryokan's intimate scale — the enclosed garden, the low-ceilinged tatami rooms, the carefully plated kaiseki dinner — provides a welcome return to the human-sized proportions that make traditional Japanese hospitality so satisfying.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I get from Nara to Lake Biwa?

Take the JR Nara Line to Kyoto Station, then transfer to the JR Biwako Line (Tokaido Main Line) heading toward Shiga Prefecture. Journey times range from one hour (to Otsu) to two hours (to Hikone), depending on your destination.

### Which Lake Biwa destination is best for a day trip from Nara?

Omi-Hachiman offers the best combination of historical atmosphere, cultural sites, and scenic beauty for a single-day visit. Its canal district, merchant heritage, and hilltop castle views make it a compelling destination.

### What is Omi beef?

Omi beef is one of Japan's three premier wagyu brands, raised in the pastures around Lake Biwa in Shiga Prefecture. It is renowned for its delicate marbling, tender texture, and sweet, rich flavour, and has been produced in the region for over four hundred years.

### Is Lake Biwa worth visiting from Nara?

Yes. Lake Biwa's combination of Japan's oldest castle keep (Hikone), atmospheric canal town (Omi-Hachiman), historic lakeside temples (Mii-dera, Ishiyama-dera), unique cuisine, and sheer natural scale makes it one of the most varied day-trip destinations from Nara.
