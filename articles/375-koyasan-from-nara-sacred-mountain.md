# Koya-san from Nara: Planning the Sacred Mountain Pilgrimage

High on a forested plateau in the mountains of northern Wakayama Prefecture, Koya-san (Mount Koya) has served as the headquarters of Shingon Buddhism for over 1,200 years. Founded in 816 by Kukai — the monk, scholar, and calligrapher known posthumously as Kobo Daishi — Koya-san is one of Japan's most sacred sites: a mountain monastery complex of more than one hundred temples, a vast cemetery stretching through ancient cedar forests, and a living centre of Buddhist study and practice that has operated without interruption since the ninth century.

For visitors based in Nara, Koya-san presents an excursion of unusual depth. The two sites share a historical relationship — Kukai studied and worked in Nara before establishing his mountain retreat — and the journey from the ancient capital to the sacred mountain traces a path from court Buddhism to mountain asceticism that mirrors one of the great movements in Japanese religious history. The trip requires early planning and a full day (or, ideally, an overnight stay), but the experience is among the most powerful available anywhere in Japan.

## Kukai and the Founding of Koya-san

Kukai (774–835) is one of the most extraordinary figures in Japanese history. Born on the island of Shikoku, he studied at the imperial university in Nara before travelling to China in 804 as part of a diplomatic mission. In Chang'an (modern Xi'an), he received initiation into esoteric Buddhism from the Chinese master Huiguo, who recognised Kukai as his spiritual successor. Kukai returned to Japan in 806 with a vast collection of texts, ritual implements, and artistic works, and over the following decades he established Shingon Buddhism as one of the dominant religious traditions in Japan.

In 816, Emperor Saga granted Kukai permission to establish a monastic retreat on Mount Koya. The site — a remote, bowl-shaped plateau at approximately 800 metres elevation, surrounded by eight peaks said to resemble a lotus flower — was chosen for its seclusion and its symbolic geography. Kukai envisioned it as a mandala made physical: a sacred space where the structures of esoteric Buddhist cosmology would be embedded in the landscape itself.

The monastery grew steadily over the following centuries, surviving fires, political upheaval, and periods of decline to become one of the most important religious centres in Japan. By the Edo period, Koya-san supported thousands of monks and maintained connections to virtually every powerful family in the country. Today, some fifty-two of the mountain's temples offer shukubo (temple lodging) to visitors, and the complex has been designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site as part of the Sacred Sites and Pilgrimage Routes in the Kii Mountain Range.

## What to See on Koya-san

### Okunoin

The approach to Okunoin — the inner sanctuary where Kukai is believed to rest in eternal meditation — is one of the most atmospheric walks in Japan. A two-kilometre path leads through a vast cemetery of over 200,000 tombstones, memorial towers, and cenotaphs, set among towering cedar trees whose canopy filters the light into a dim, green-gold glow. The memorials span every era of Japanese history: feudal warlords, merchant families, modern corporations, and anonymous pilgrims are all represented here, their stones softened by moss and time.

The path culminates at the Torodo (Lantern Hall), where thousands of lanterns burn perpetually before the mausoleum of Kobo Daishi. According to Shingon belief, Kukai did not die in 835 but entered a state of eternal meditation (nyujo), and he continues to offer salvation to all beings from within this sanctuary. The sense of accumulated devotion at Okunoin is profound — centuries of prayer have charged the atmosphere in ways that even sceptical visitors tend to acknowledge.

The walk is best done twice: once in daylight, to appreciate the cemetery's scale and detail, and once at night, when the lanterns glow and the forest becomes almost otherworldly. Several temples offer guided night walks of Okunoin.

### Danjo Garan

The Danjo Garan (Sacred Precinct) is the ceremonial heart of Koya-san, the complex that Kukai originally established as the centre of Shingon practice. The precinct contains several important structures:

- **Konpon Daito (Great Stupa)**: The massive vermillion pagoda that dominates the precinct, standing forty-eight metres tall. Its interior houses a three-dimensional mandala with five Buddha images and painted pillars — one of the most significant esoteric Buddhist spaces in Japan.
- **Kondo (Golden Hall)**: The main hall for religious ceremonies, rebuilt numerous times after fires.
- **Saito (Western Stupa)**: A smaller pagoda paired with the Konpon Daito, together representing the twin mandalas of Shingon Buddhism.
- **Miedo (Portrait Hall)**: Said to contain a self-portrait by Kukai, this hall is opened only on special occasions.

### Kongobu-ji

The administrative headquarters of Shingon Buddhism, Kongobu-ji is a large temple complex that includes Japan's largest rock garden — the Banryutei, a vast expanse of white gravel representing a pair of dragons emerging from clouds. The temple's interior rooms feature beautiful fusuma (sliding door) paintings from the Kano school and the Momoyama period.

### Reihokan Museum

This museum houses a remarkable collection of Shingon Buddhist art, including sculptures, paintings, ritual implements, and mandala scrolls spanning over a thousand years. Some of the pieces are designated National Treasures, and the collection rivals any in the Kansai region for the quality and depth of its Buddhist art.

## Getting to Koya-san from Nara

The journey requires multiple transfers but is well established and clearly signposted.

**By train**: From Kintetsu Nara Station, take the Kintetsu Line to Osaka-Abenobashi (Kintetsu Minami-Osaka Line via Yamato-Saidaiji and Osaka-Uehommachi, approximately ninety minutes). From the adjacent Nankai Osaka-Abenobashi Station (connected to Tennoji), transfer to the Nankai Koya Line to Gokurakubashi Station (approximately ninety minutes on the express). From Gokurakubashi, a cable car ascends to Koya-san Station (five minutes), where buses connect to the mountain-top temple complex. Total travel time is approximately three and a half to four hours each way.

**By car**: Driving from Nara takes approximately two to two and a half hours via the Keinawa Expressway and the Hanwa Expressway to Hashimoto, then Route 370/480 up the mountain. Parking is available on the mountain top.

The length of the journey from Nara makes an overnight stay on Koya-san highly advisable if your schedule permits. A day trip is technically possible with a very early start, but it leaves limited time on the mountain.

## Shukubo: Temple Lodging

One of the defining experiences of Koya-san is staying overnight in a temple. Approximately fifty-two temples offer shukubo, ranging from simple to quite luxurious. Guests sleep in tatami rooms, eat shojin ryori (Buddhist vegetarian cuisine), and can participate in morning prayers and fire rituals (goma). The meals alone are worth the stay — multi-course vegetarian feasts that demonstrate the extraordinary refinement Buddhist monks have brought to plant-based cooking over centuries.

Reservations can be made through the Koya-san Shukubo Association website or through major booking platforms. Advance booking is strongly recommended, particularly during autumn foliage season and Golden Week.

## Practical Information

**Best time to visit**: Autumn (late October to mid-November) is stunning, with the mountain's maples turning vivid red against the dark cedar forests. Spring is pleasant, and summer offers cool relief from lowland heat. Winter brings snow and a stark, meditative beauty, but some facilities may be limited.

**Time needed**: A minimum of six hours on the mountain covers the essential sites (Okunoin, Danjo Garan, Kongobu-ji). An overnight stay allows the night walk through Okunoin and morning prayers — experiences that transform the visit.

**What to bring**: Comfortable walking shoes, layers (the mountain is noticeably cooler than the plains), and rain gear. Okunoin's path is gravel and can be slippery when wet.

**Accessibility**: The main temple areas and bus routes are largely accessible, but Okunoin's cemetery path has uneven surfaces and some steps.

## The Nara Connection

Kukai's relationship with Nara was formative. He studied at the imperial university and at several Nara temples before his journey to China, and after his return, he was appointed abbot of Todai-ji before eventually establishing his mountain retreat. The journey from Nara to Koya-san thus retraces, in compressed form, the trajectory of Kukai's own spiritual career — from the established Buddhist institutions of the capital to the radical solitude of the mountain.

Guests at Kanoya ryokan who plan a Koya-san excursion may wish to bookend the experience: a night in Nara's refined hospitality before the journey, and a return to the ryokan's comforts afterward, allowing the mountain's spiritual intensity to settle in the quiet of a traditional Japanese inn.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I visit Koya-san as a day trip from Nara?

It is possible but challenging. The journey takes approximately three and a half to four hours each way, leaving limited time on the mountain. An overnight stay in temple lodging (shukubo) is highly recommended for the full experience.

### What is Okunoin?

Okunoin is the inner sanctuary of Koya-san, where Kobo Daishi (Kukai) is believed to rest in eternal meditation. A two-kilometre path through a vast, atmospheric cedar-forest cemetery leads to the Torodo (Lantern Hall) and the mausoleum.

### What is shukubo?

Shukubo is temple lodging offered by Buddhist temples. On Koya-san, approximately fifty-two temples accept overnight guests, providing tatami rooms, Buddhist vegetarian cuisine (shojin ryori), and the opportunity to participate in morning prayers and rituals.

### How do I get to Koya-san from Nara?

Travel via Osaka, transferring to the Nankai Koya Line at Tennoji/Osaka-Abenobashi. From Gokurakubashi, a cable car ascends to the mountaintop. Total travel time from Nara is approximately three and a half to four hours by public transport, or two to two and a half hours by car.
