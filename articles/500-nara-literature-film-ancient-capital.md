# Nara in Literature and Film: The Ancient Capital on Screen and Page

Nara has been written about for longer than almost any other city in Japan. The eighth-century poetry anthologies compiled in the city — the Man'yoshu and the Kaifuso — are among the oldest surviving works of Japanese and Sino-Japanese literature, and they describe a place that, in certain lights, remains recognizable today. The deer are still there. The great temples still anchor the eastern hills. The sense of a place where time accumulates rather than passes still pervades the landscape.

What is remarkable about Nara's literary and cinematic presence is not its volume — Kyoto generates far more artistic attention — but its character. Writers and filmmakers who choose Nara over Kyoto or Tokyo are usually reaching for something specific: the weight of deep time, the quiet that settles over a place whose greatest dramas happened so long ago that they have become geology rather than history, the particular melancholy of a former capital that has made peace with its own diminishment. Nara in art is almost never a backdrop for contemporary action. It is a place people go to when they need to think about what lasts.

## Classical Literature

### The Man'yoshu (c. 759)

The Man'yoshu — the "Collection of Ten Thousand Leaves" — is the foundational text of Japanese poetry and the most important literary work associated with Nara. Compiled in the latter decades of the Nara period, it contains 4,516 poems spanning roughly four centuries of Japanese verse. Unlike later imperial anthologies, which were curated by literary elites, the Man'yoshu includes poems by emperors and beggars, soldiers and women, creating a panoramic portrait of ancient Japanese emotional life.

Nara itself appears throughout the collection. Poems describe the view from Mount Mikasa, the deer on the fields below Kasuga, the sound of temple bells across the basin, and the experience of departing the capital — a theme that runs through the collection with particular poignancy because many of its poets were officials transferred to distant provinces. The anguish of leaving Nara was a recognized literary mode, and the city appears in these farewell poems as a place of such beauty and culture that distance from it constitutes genuine suffering.

For visitors, the Manyou Botanical Garden in Nara Park creates a physical connection to the Man'yoshu by cultivating the plants mentioned in its poems, with relevant verses posted beside each species. Walking the garden is a form of embodied literary criticism — experiencing the actual flowers and grasses that prompted the words.

### The Pillow Book of Sei Shonagon (c. 1002)

Sei Shonagon's Makura no Soshi, composed about two centuries after Nara lost its capital status, treats the city as a pilgrimage destination rather than a political center. Her famous list-making style captures Nara in vivid fragments: the journey to Hase-dera temple through the Yamato countryside, the impression of ancient temple buildings against forested hills, and the slightly condescending affection that a sophisticated Kyoto courtier felt toward the old capital.

Shonagon's Nara is already a place defined by its past rather than its present — a characterization that has proven remarkably durable. Her descriptions of the pilgrimage road to Hase-dera remain accurate enough to serve as a rough guide for today's walker, and her acute observations about the landscape's effect on the traveler's mood anticipate by a thousand years the language of modern travel writing.

### The Tale of Genji (c. 1008)

Murasaki Shikibu's masterwork is primarily set in Kyoto, but Nara and the Yamato region appear at crucial moments. The pilgrimage to Hase-dera — a major plot device that brings characters together through apparently chance encounters at the temple — reflects the real importance of Nara-area temples in Heian aristocratic life. The novel treats these pilgrimages as simultaneous acts of devotion and social navigation, and the journey through the Yamato countryside is described with the careful attention to landscape that characterizes the novel's best passages.

For readers who visit Hase-dera after reading The Tale of Genji, the long covered stairway ascending the hillside takes on the quality of a literary set — you are walking where fictional characters walked, in a landscape the author knew well enough to describe with authority.

## Edo Period Writing

### Matsuo Basho and the Poetry of Pilgrimage

Basho, Japan's most revered haiku poet, visited Nara multiple times during his pilgrimage journeys, and his poems about the city are among the most frequently cited descriptions in Japanese literature.

His most famous Nara haiku:

*Kiku no ka ya / Nara ni wa furuki / hotoke-tachi*

(Chrysanthemum fragrance — / in Nara, the ancient / Buddhas)

This poem, composed during an autumn visit, captures in seventeen syllables the experience that thousands of visitors have had but could not articulate: the way Nara's temples and their contents seem to belong to a time scale different from the visitor's own, and the way seasonal beauty (the chrysanthemums) provides the sensory bridge between the human present and the Buddha's eternity.

Basho's Nara poems appear in several of his travel journals, and his approach to the city — as a pilgrim-poet seeking spiritual resonance through landscape — established a mode of experiencing Nara that persists in contemporary travel writing.

### Ihara Saikaku

The Osaka-based novelist Ihara Saikaku, writing in the late seventeenth century, included Nara settings in several of his works. His Nara is a more worldly place than Basho's — a destination for merchants on pleasure trips, a market town where commerce and religion coexist pragmatically, a place where the sacred and the profane share the same streets. Saikaku's eye for economic detail and social comedy gives his Nara passages a flavor entirely different from the poetic tradition, and they provide one of the few literary windows into the city's commercial life during the Edo period.

## Modern Literature

### Yasunari Kawabata — "The Ancient Capital" (Koto, 1962)

Kawabata's Nobel Prize-winning career included several works set in or near the Kansai region, but it is his novel Koto (The Ancient Capital) that most directly engages with the theme of Japan's old capitals. Although set primarily in Kyoto, the novel's meditation on tradition, beauty, and the passage of time applies to Nara with equal force, and Kawabata's sensibility — his attention to seasonal change, his awareness of what persists and what vanishes, his treatment of landscape as emotional terrain — has shaped how subsequent writers approach both cities.

### Osamu Dazai — References and Reflections

Dazai Osamu, best known for his confessional novels of postwar despair, visited Nara and wrote about the experience in several essays. His Nara is characteristically Dazai-esque: beautiful but imbued with an awareness of personal failure and historical loss that transforms sightseeing into existential reckoning. His essay on visiting the Great Buddha is notable for its honesty about the gap between what a famous sight is supposed to make you feel and what it actually does make you feel — a theme that resonates with any traveler who has stood before a masterpiece and felt, instead of awe, only the pressure to perform awe.

### Natsume Soseki

Soseki, Japan's most important modern novelist, passed through Nara during his travels and referenced the city in his essays and letters. His observations tend toward the philosophical, treating Nara's antiquity as a stimulus for thinking about the relationship between Japan's past and its rapidly modernizing present. Writing in the early twentieth century, when Japan's identity crisis was at its most acute, Soseki found in Nara a concentration of everything the country was simultaneously trying to preserve and leave behind.

### Contemporary Fiction

Nara appears in contemporary Japanese fiction less frequently than Kyoto or Tokyo but with a consistency that reflects its unique atmospheric qualities. Banana Yoshimoto's works occasionally reference Nara as a place of spiritual retreat. Kaori Ekuni's novels use the Kansai region, including Nara, as a setting for stories of quiet emotional reckoning. And the growing genre of Japanese literary tourism — novels written specifically about the experience of traveling to historic places — has produced several works that use Nara as their primary setting.

## Nara on Screen

### Documentary Film

Nara's most powerful cinematic representations have been in documentary rather than fiction. The NHK television network has produced numerous documentaries about Nara's temples, crafts, and cultural traditions, several of which have been released internationally. The patient, observational style of Japanese documentary filmmaking — long takes, minimal narration, attention to seasonal change — suits Nara's character better than dramatic narrative.

The BBC's various Japan-focused documentary series have included Nara segments that have introduced the city to international audiences. These programs typically focus on the temples and deer, establishing the visual vocabulary through which non-Japanese viewers recognize Nara.

### Naomi Kawase

The filmmaker most closely associated with Nara is Naomi Kawase, who was born and raised in the city and has set much of her work there. Kawase's films — including Shara (2003), The Mourning Forest (Mogari no Mori, 2007), and An (Sweet Bean, 2015) — use Nara's landscapes and light with an intimacy that reflects a lifetime of local knowledge. Her camera finds beauty not in the famous sites but in the spaces between them: the residential streets, the forests, the quality of afternoon light filtering through trees.

The Mourning Forest, which won the Grand Prix at Cannes, is set in the wooded hills of Nara Prefecture and uses the landscape as a character in its own right — the forest's density, silence, and indifference to human emotion becoming the dominant force of the narrative. For viewers who watch the film before visiting Nara, the forested hills behind Kasuga Shrine carry a cinematic resonance that enriches the physical experience.

### Anime and Animation

Nara appears in several anime series and films, typically as a setting for episodes involving school trips or historical themes. The deer of Nara Park are particularly popular in anime, appearing as both realistic background elements and anthropomorphized characters. The Studio Ghibli aesthetic — detailed natural landscapes rendered with loving precision — draws on the kind of old-growth forest and temple grounds that Nara preserves, and while no major Ghibli film is set specifically in Nara, the visual influence is acknowledged by the studio's artists.

## Literary Pilgrimage in Practice

### Walking Nara with a Book

The most rewarding way to combine literature and travel in Nara is simply to carry a relevant book and read passages at the locations they describe. The Man'yoshu — available in several English translations, including Ian Hideo Levy's acclaimed rendering — is the essential literary companion. Reading a poem about the view from Mount Mikasa while standing on Mount Mikasa collapses thirteen centuries into a single moment of shared perception.

Basho's travel journals (The Narrow Road to the Deep North and related works) provide a more portable literary companion, with Nara passages short enough to read standing in front of the scenes they describe.

### The Literary Ryokan Tradition

Traditional Japanese inns have a long association with literary production. Many of Japan's greatest writers composed significant works while staying at ryokan, and the quiet, contemplative atmosphere of a well-run ryokan is deliberately conducive to reading, writing, and reflection. At Kanoya ryokan, the unhurried evening hours — after the kaiseki dinner and the bath, when the city outside has fallen silent — create the conditions that Japanese literary culture has always recognized as ideal for engaging deeply with a text.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most important literary work associated with Nara?
*The Man'yoshu, compiled in Nara around 759 CE, is Japan's oldest and most important poetry anthology. Its 4,516 poems include numerous descriptions of Nara's landscape, temples, and seasonal beauty, making it the foundational literary text of the ancient capital.*

### Which filmmaker is most associated with Nara?
Naomi Kawase, born and raised in Nara, has set much of her award-winning filmography in the city and its surrounding landscapes. Her film The Mourning Forest won the Grand Prix at Cannes in 2007.

### Are there English translations of literature about Nara?
Yes, major works including the Man'yoshu, The Pillow Book, The Tale of Genji, and Basho's travel journals are available in multiple English translations. Ian Hideo Levy's translation of the Man'yoshu and Meredith McKinney's Pillow Book translation are particularly recommended.

### Does Nara appear in anime or manga?
Nara appears in various anime series, typically in school trip episodes or historical settings. The deer of Nara Park are especially popular in anime. While no single major anime is exclusively set in Nara, the city's visual identity — temples, deer, ancient forests — appears regularly across the medium.

### What is the best book to read before visiting Nara?
For first-time visitors, a Man'yoshu selection provides the deepest historical connection. For those interested in the atmosphere of the old capital, Sei Shonagon's Pillow Book captures the experience of encountering Nara's landscape with fresh eyes. Basho's haiku offer the most distilled literary encounters with specific Nara sites.
