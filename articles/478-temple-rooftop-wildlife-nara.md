# Temple Rooftop Wildlife: Birds and Creatures of Nara's Sacred Buildings

Look up. That is perhaps the most useful advice for any visitor to Nara's temples, and not only for the architectural details of bracket systems and roof ridges. The great rooftops of Nara's Buddhist temples and Shinto shrines are living ecosystems — habitats where birds, bats, insects, and small mammals have established themselves over centuries in a relationship with sacred architecture that is as old as the buildings themselves.

The massive tiled roofs of Todaiji, the layered eaves of Kofukuji's pagoda, the weathered cedar bark roofing of smaller halls — each offers a different microhabitat that attracts different species. For visitors with an interest in natural history, Nara's temple grounds provide some of the finest urban wildlife observation in Japan, and the rooftops themselves are where some of the most interesting species make their homes.

## Why Temples Attract Wildlife

### Architecture as Habitat

Japanese temple architecture creates ecological niches that few other building types can match. The deep, overhanging eaves that are a hallmark of traditional Japanese construction provide sheltered spaces beneath them — protected from rain and wind but open to the air — that are ideal nesting and roosting sites for birds. The complex bracket systems that support these eaves offer ledges, crevices, and enclosed spaces of varying sizes, accommodating everything from small songbirds to raptors.

The roofs themselves, with their layered tiles, create further habitat. The gaps between tiles provide shelter for insects, which in turn attract insectivorous birds and bats. The ridge ornaments — the elaborate ceramic finials called shibi and onigawara that crown the roof ridges — create perching points that birds use for territorial display and hunting surveillance.

Beneath the floors, the raised timber construction typical of Japanese temples creates a shaded, dry space that shelters ground-dwelling creatures. And the surrounding temple grounds — with their ancient trees, ornamental ponds, and undisturbed undergrowth — provide the food sources and cover that sustain the rooftop populations above.

### The Sacred Protection Factor

Temple wildlife benefits from a protection that is both practical and philosophical. Buddhist doctrine respects the lives of all sentient beings, and this principle has historically extended to tolerance of the animals that share temple space. Birds nesting in eaves are generally left undisturbed. Bats roosting under roofs are accepted rather than exterminated. Even the wasps that build nests in bracket crevices are often managed rather than destroyed.

This benign neglect — or, more generously, this spiritual coexistence — has created conditions for wildlife populations to establish themselves over centuries. The oldest temples in Nara have been hosting birds and bats for longer than most forests have existed in their current form. The continuity of habitat, uninterrupted by the demolition and rebuilding cycles that affect secular buildings, gives temple wildlife an ecological stability that is increasingly rare.

## Birds of Nara's Temple Rooftops

### Raptors

The most dramatic rooftop residents are the raptors. The northern goshawk (otaka) and the Eurasian sparrowhawk (haitaka) both hunt in Nara's temple precincts, using the elevated perches of pagoda finials and hall ridges as surveillance points from which to launch attacks on smaller birds in the grounds below. The goshawk, in particular, has adapted well to semi-urban environments in Japan, and Nara's mixture of old-growth trees and open parkland provides ideal hunting territory.

During spring nesting season, keep watch on the tallest trees near major temple buildings. Goshawks favor the dense canopy of old camphor and cedar trees adjacent to temple halls, where the proximity of the building may provide additional shelter from wind and predators.

The common kestrel (chogenbo) is another raptor that frequents temple rooftops, hovering over the open grounds of Nara Park before returning to perch on roof ridges. Kestrels are easiest to spot in the cooler months, when their hovering silhouettes stand out against pale skies.

### Owls and Nocturnal Birds

Several owl species inhabit Nara's temple grounds, though their nocturnal habits make them difficult to observe. The brown hawk-owl (aobazuku) is a summer visitor that nests in tree cavities near temples, its distinctive two-note call — ho-ho, ho-ho — audible on warm evenings from May through August. Guests staying overnight near temples may hear it after dark, a sound that has been part of the Nara soundscape for centuries.

The Ural owl (fukuro) is a year-round resident of the forested hillsides east of Nara Park, and individuals sometimes roost in the larger trees within temple compounds during the day. Early morning visitors to the quieter temples along the Kasuga Primeval Forest edge have the best chance of spotting one.

The collared scops owl (otus lettia), Japan's smallest owl species, has also been recorded in temple grounds, though sightings are infrequent.

### Pigeons, Doves, and Corvids

The most visible temple birds are the most common ones. Rock doves (doban, or common pigeons) and Oriental turtle doves (kijibato) are constant presences on Nara's temple rooftops, and their cooing provides the ambient soundtrack of any temple visit. While these species are too familiar to excite most birdwatchers, their behavior on temple roofs is worth observing. Pigeons use the warm, south-facing tile surfaces for sunning, spreading their wings to absorb heat in a behavior called sunbathing that helps control feather parasites.

The large-billed crow (hashibutogarasu) and the carrion crow (hashibosogarasu) are equally ubiquitous. Crows are intelligent opportunists that exploit temple grounds for food — dropped offerings, insect larvae in wood, scraps from visitor lunches — and their nests, built from sticks and wire in the forks of temple-ground trees, are conspicuous from late winter onward. Though often dismissed as pests, Nara's crows are impressive birds deserving of observation: their problem-solving behavior, social communication, and aerial agility are remarkable.

### Small Songbirds

The eaves and bracket systems of temple buildings host a variety of smaller species. The Japanese white-eye (mejiro), with its distinctive white eye-ring, feeds on insects in and around the wooden structures. The varied tit (yamagara) and Japanese tit (shijukara) nest in tree cavities near temple buildings and forage among the roof timbers for insects.

The barn swallow (tsubame) is a particularly beloved temple resident. Swallows build their mud nests under the eaves of Japanese buildings, and temples — with their wide, sheltered eaves and tolerant inhabitants — are prime nesting sites. The arrival of swallows in spring is a celebrated seasonal marker in Japan, and their nests under temple eaves are traditionally protected and welcomed. At many Nara temples, you can spot active swallow nests under the eaves of secondary buildings, the parent birds swooping in and out with insects for their chicks.

The blue-and-white flycatcher (oruri) and the narcissus flycatcher (kibidaki) are spring and summer visitors that favor the forested temple grounds of Nara's hillside temples. Their songs — the oruri's cascading melody and the kibidaki's staccato phrases — are among the finest bird sounds you will hear in Nara.

## Beyond Birds: Other Rooftop Creatures

### Bats

Japanese pipistrelle bats (aburakumouri) roost under the tiles and in the eaves of many Nara temples, emerging at dusk to hunt insects over the grounds. On warm summer evenings, the silhouettes of bats flitting above temple rooftops are a common sight, particularly around buildings near water — ponds and fountains attract the insects that bats feed on.

### Squirrels and Small Mammals

The Japanese squirrel (nihon risu) and the Siberian flying squirrel (momonga, actually the Japanese dwarf flying squirrel in Nara) inhabit the old-growth trees of temple compounds. While they are not rooftop dwellers per se, they frequently traverse the roof structures when moving between trees. The flying squirrel is nocturnal and seldom seen, but its presence in Nara's temple forests has been documented by wildlife researchers.

### Insects

The insect life of temple rooftops is an ecosystem in itself. Wood-boring beetles gradually work their way through ancient timbers — a concern for conservators but a food source for woodpeckers and other insectivorous birds. Paper wasps build their nests in the sheltered spaces of bracket systems. Spiders weave webs between roof tiles, catching the insects attracted to the warm surfaces.

## Observing Temple Wildlife

### Practical Tips

Binoculars are essential for observing rooftop wildlife, as most activity occurs at heights difficult to see with the naked eye. A compact pair with 8x or 10x magnification is sufficient. Early morning, when birds are most active and temples are least crowded, is the best time for observation.

The quieter temples — Shin-Yakushiji, Toshodaiji, Akishinodera, and the smaller halls within the Kasuga Taisha complex — offer better wildlife observation than the heavily visited sites. The edge zones where temple grounds meet the Kasuga Primeval Forest are particularly productive, as forest species venture into the temple compound to feed.

Spring (March through May) and early summer (June) are the peak seasons for bird activity, coinciding with nesting. Autumn migration (September through November) brings passage migrants through the area. Winter is quieter but offers clearer visibility through bare branches.

### The Integration of Culture and Nature

What makes wildlife observation at Nara's temples special is the integration of the natural and the cultural. The birds on the rooftops, the bats in the eaves, the squirrels in the ancient trees — they are not peripheral to the temple experience but part of it. Buddhist architecture was designed to sit within nature, not to exclude it, and the wildlife that inhabits these buildings is a living expression of that principle.

At Kanoya ryokan, the awareness of nature that temple wildlife observation cultivates carries into the broader experience of traditional hospitality. The seasonal garden, the sound of birds through open windows, the attention to natural materials and natural rhythms — all of these are enriched when you have spent a morning watching swallows build beneath a temple's thousand-year-old eaves.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What birds can you see at Nara's temples?
Nara's temple grounds host a wide variety of birds including raptors (goshawks, kestrels), owls (brown hawk-owl, Ural owl), swallows, woodpeckers, songbirds like the Japanese white-eye and blue-and-white flycatcher, as well as doves and crows. Species vary by season.

### Is Nara good for birdwatching?
Yes, Nara is excellent for birdwatching due to its combination of ancient temple grounds, old-growth trees, and the Kasuga Primeval Forest — one of the few untouched forests near a major Japanese city. Over 100 bird species have been recorded in the Nara Park area.

### When is the best time for birdwatching in Nara?
Spring (March-May) is the peak season, with nesting activity and the arrival of summer migrants like flycatchers and swallows. Early morning visits to quieter temples offer the best observation conditions with minimal disturbance from crowds.

### Do animals live inside Nara's temples?
Yes, various creatures inhabit the structures themselves. Bats roost under tiles and in eaves, swallows build nests beneath overhanging roofs, and birds nest in the complex bracket systems. Temples have traditionally tolerated these inhabitants in keeping with Buddhist respect for all living beings.

### Why do swallows nest at Japanese temples?
*Swallows nest at Japanese temples because the buildings' wide, deeply projecting eaves provide ideal sheltered sites for their mud nests, protecting them from rain and direct sun. Additionally, Buddhist and Shinto tradition regards swallows as auspicious visitors, and their nests are traditionally welcomed rather than removed. The tolerance of temple communities toward nesting wildlife, combined with the architectural features of traditional Japanese construction, has made temples reliable nesting sites for swallows across centuries — a relationship between human architecture and wild nature that exemplifies the coexistence at the heart of Japanese sacred spaces.*
