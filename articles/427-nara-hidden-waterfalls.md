# Nara's Hidden Waterfalls: Day Hikes to Cascades

Nara is known for temples, deer, and ancient gardens — not waterfalls. But the mountains that surround the Nara Basin on three sides are drained by dozens of streams, and where these streams cross rock ledges and geological fault lines, they produce waterfalls ranging from modest curtains to thundering cascades. Most of these falls are unknown to the average tourist, visited primarily by local hikers and, in some cases, by practitioners of Shugendo mountain asceticism who have used waterfalls as places of spiritual purification for over a thousand years.

For visitors who want to combine physical activity with natural beauty — and who are willing to venture beyond the city's well-trodden temple circuit — Nara's waterfalls offer a rewarding counterpoint to the cultural attractions of the basin floor.

## Why Nara Has Waterfalls

The mountains of Nara Prefecture rise to over 1,700 metres in the south (the Omine range) and 500-800 metres in the east and west. Heavy rainfall — the southern mountains receive over 3,000 millimetres annually, among the highest in Japan — feeds streams that descend steeply through narrow valleys. Where these streams encounter resistant rock formations — granite ledges, sandstone benches, or fault-line scarps — waterfalls form.

The geological variety of Nara Prefecture (see our geology guide) means that these waterfalls occur in different rock types, producing different characters: smooth curtain falls over granite, stepped cascades over layered sedimentary rock, and narrow chute falls through fault-line crevices.

## The Waterfalls

### Fudo-Nanae no Taki (Fudo Seven-Stage Falls)

**Location**: Kawakami Village, southern Nara Prefecture
**Height**: Approximately 100 metres total (seven distinct drops)
**Hike**: 30-minute walk from the trailhead
**Difficulty**: Easy to moderate

One of Nara's most impressive waterfall complexes, the Seven-Stage Falls descend through a forested ravine in seven distinct cascades, the largest dropping approximately 20 metres. The falls are sacred to Shugendo practitioners — a statue of Fudo Myoo (the fierce Buddhist deity associated with waterfall purification) stands near the base.

The trail follows a stream through old-growth forest, crossing wooden bridges and passing moss-covered boulders. The approach itself is beautiful — the sound of water builds gradually as you ascend. In autumn, the surrounding deciduous forest produces brilliant colour that frames the white water against red and gold.

**Access**: Drive or take a bus from Yoshino to Kawakami Village. The trailhead is signed.

### Futainotaki (Twin Waterfall)

**Location**: Totsukawa Village, deep southern Nara Prefecture
**Height**: Approximately 50 metres (two parallel streams)
**Hike**: 15-minute walk from the viewpoint car park
**Difficulty**: Easy

Two parallel streams of water fall side by side down a cliff face — a natural formation that has inspired legends of twin deities and paired lovers. The falls are most impressive after heavy rain, when both streams run at full volume and the spray fills the gorge.

Totsukawa Village is remote — Japan's largest village by area and one of its least populated. The journey from Nara city takes approximately three hours by car, making this a full-day excursion. But the dramatic mountain scenery along the route and the wildness of the location make the journey worthwhile.

**Access**: Car recommended. The falls are on Route 425 in Totsukawa Village.

### Mitarai Valley Waterfalls

**Location**: Tenkawa Village, southern Nara Prefecture
**Height**: Various (multiple falls along the valley)
**Hike**: 7-kilometre trail through the valley (2.5–3.5 hours)
**Difficulty**: Moderate

Mitarai Valley is perhaps the finest waterfall hiking in Nara Prefecture. The trail follows the Yamakaminishi River through a series of gorges, crossing suspension bridges and passing multiple waterfalls, cascades, and deep emerald pools. The water is exceptionally clear — the river runs through granite, which filters rather than muddies the flow.

Key waterfalls along the route include:

**Mitarai Falls**: The main cascade, a powerful curtain of water dropping into a deep blue-green pool. The viewing platform provides a dramatic perspective.

**Konotaki**: A smaller but photogenic fall upstream, accessible via a short side trail.

**Numerous unnamed cascades**: The valley contains dozens of smaller falls, chutes, and rapids that reward exploration off the main trail.

The full trail runs from Mitarai Valley to Dorogawa Onsen, where you can soak in hot springs after the hike — an ideal combination of effort and reward.

**Access**: Bus from Kintetsu Shimoichiguchi Station to Tenkawa Village (approximately 90 minutes). The trail is signed from the bus stop.

### Momijigari Falls (Maple Viewing Falls)

**Location**: Akame area, near the Nara-Mie border
**Height**: Various (48 waterfalls along the gorge)
**Hike**: 4-kilometre trail (1.5–2 hours)
**Difficulty**: Easy to moderate

Technically just across the border in Mie Prefecture but easily accessed from Nara, the Akame 48 Waterfalls (Akame Shijuhachi Taki) is a renowned waterfall gorge containing — as the name suggests — 48 named waterfalls along a four-kilometre trail. The falls range from gentle curtains to significant cascades, and the gorge's narrow walls create a dramatic, enclosed atmosphere.

The gorge is famous for autumn colour (the name "momijigari" means "maple hunting"), and the combination of red maples, black rock, and white water in November is extraordinary. But the gorge is beautiful in every season — lush green in summer, snow-dusted in winter, and ringed with fresh growth in spring.

The trail is well-maintained with steps, bridges, and handrails. It is accessible to most fitness levels, though the stone steps can be slippery when wet.

**Access**: Train to Akameguchi Station (Kintetsu line, approximately 90 minutes from Nara), then bus or taxi to the gorge entrance.

### Sasanotaki (Bamboo Grass Falls)

**Location**: Kamikitayama Village, eastern Nara Prefecture
**Height**: Approximately 32 metres
**Hike**: 10-minute walk from the road
**Difficulty**: Easy

A single powerful fall dropping over a granite cliff into a pool surrounded by dense forest. The falls are named for the sasa bamboo grass that grows along the approach trail. Less visited than the major waterfall sites, Sasanotaki offers a quieter experience — you may have the falls entirely to yourself on weekdays.

**Access**: Car required. The falls are in the mountains east of Yoshino, approximately two hours from Nara city.

### Nachi-no-Taki (Nachi Falls) — A Day Trip Extension

**Location**: Nachikatsuura, Wakayama Prefecture
**Height**: 133 metres (single drop — the tallest in Japan)
**Hike**: Short walk from the bus stop or a longer pilgrimage trail
**Difficulty**: Easy (direct access) to moderate (pilgrimage trail)

While technically in Wakayama Prefecture rather than Nara, Nachi Falls is accessible as a long day trip from Nara and represents the ultimate expression of the sacred waterfall tradition that begins in Nara's mountains. At 133 metres, it is the tallest single-drop waterfall in Japan, and its setting — framed by the vermillion pagoda of Seiganto-ji temple — is one of the most iconic landscape views in the country.

The falls have been a Shinto sacred site for millennia. Waterfall worship (taki shugyo) at Nachi predates the arrival of Buddhism in Japan.

**Access**: JR train to Kii-Katsuura Station (approximately 4 hours from Nara), then bus to Nachi Falls. A very long day trip — consider an overnight stay.

## Sacred Waterfalls and Shugendo

Many of Nara's waterfalls are not merely scenic features but active religious sites. Shugendo — the syncretic mountain ascetic tradition that blends Shinto, Buddhist, and Taoist elements — uses waterfalls as places of misogi (purification). Practitioners stand beneath the falling water, enduring the cold and the force of the cascade as a form of spiritual discipline.

The Omine mountain range in southern Nara Prefecture is one of the heartlands of Shugendo, and many waterfalls along the pilgrimage routes bear evidence of this practice — carved Fudo Myoo figures, shimenawa (sacred ropes), offering altars, and paths worn smooth by centuries of approaching feet.

Visitors should not attempt waterfall purification without guidance — the water is cold, the force can be dangerous, and the practice carries spiritual protocols that should be respected. However, understanding the religious dimension adds meaning to a visit. These waterfalls are not just beautiful; they are places where the boundary between the physical and spiritual has been considered thin for over a millennium.

## Best Seasons

**Early summer (June)**: The rainy season fills the streams, and waterfalls run at maximum volume. The surrounding forests are at their greenest. Trails can be muddy.

**Autumn (October–November)**: The combination of flowing water and autumn foliage is the most photogenic season. Akame 48 Waterfalls and Mitarai Valley are particularly stunning.

**Summer (July–August)**: The cool spray of waterfalls provides welcome relief from the heat. Swimming is possible in some pools (check locally for permitted areas). Be aware of afternoon thunderstorms.

**Winter**: Reduced water flow but dramatic atmosphere — ice formations on rock faces, frost on moss, and absolute solitude. Some trails may be inaccessible due to snow in higher elevations.

**Spring (April–May)**: Snowmelt from the mountains boosts water flow. Cherry blossoms along lower approaches add colour.

## Practical Information

**Footwear**: Waterproof hiking boots with good grip are essential. Wet rock is slippery, and trail surfaces vary from maintained paths to natural stone.

**Water and food**: Carry sufficient water and snacks. Most waterfall trails have no facilities. Mitarai Valley trail ends at Dorogawa Onsen, where restaurants are available.

**Photography**: A tripod enables slow-shutter waterfall photography (silky water effect). A polarising filter reduces glare on wet rock and deepens water colour. Waterproof camera protection is advisable — spray is unavoidable at close range.

**Time planning**: Allow more time than the hiking estimates suggest. Waterfall viewing rewards patience — sit, watch, listen. The sound of water against rock is itself a reason to linger.

**Insect protection**: Forest trails in summer harbour mosquitoes and, in southern Nara, leeches (yamabiru). Tuck trousers into socks and apply repellent.

Visitors based in Nara city — at accommodation such as Kanoya ryokan in Naramachi — can reach the nearest waterfall hikes (Mitarai Valley, Fudo Seven-Stage Falls) within 90 minutes to two hours by car or public transport, making these feasible as day trips with an early start.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I reach any waterfalls by public transport?

Yes. Mitarai Valley is accessible by bus from Kintetsu Shimoichiguchi Station. Akame 48 Waterfalls is reachable by train and bus. The more remote falls in Totsukawa and Kamikitayama require a car.

### Are the trails suitable for children?

Akame 48 Waterfalls and the Mitarai Valley main trail are manageable for children aged 8 and above with reasonable fitness. Younger children may struggle with distance and uneven terrain.

### Is swimming allowed in the waterfall pools?

In some locations, yes — particularly in summer. Check locally for regulations and safety conditions. River currents near waterfalls can be stronger than they appear.

### What is the closest waterfall to Nara city?

The small cascades in the Kasugayama primeval forest (behind Kasuga Taisha) are the closest to the city centre — a 30-minute walk from the park. For significant waterfalls, Mitarai Valley is the nearest major site.
