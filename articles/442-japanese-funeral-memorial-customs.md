# Japanese Funeral and Memorial Customs: What Visitors May Encounter

If you spend any significant time visiting temples in Nara — or anywhere in Japan — you will almost certainly encounter a funeral or memorial service in progress. You may see mourners dressed entirely in black filing into a temple hall, catch the sound of sutra chanting through a closed door, or notice an arrangement of white chrysanthemums and a framed photograph at the entrance to a building you had planned to visit. These moments can feel awkward for foreign visitors who are unsure whether they are intruding, how to behave, or what exactly is happening. Understanding the basics of Japanese funeral and memorial customs removes that uncertainty and replaces it with respectful awareness.

## The Buddhist Framework

### Death and Buddhism in Japan

Approximately 90 percent of funerals in Japan follow Buddhist rites, a proportion that has remained remarkably stable even as other forms of religious observance have declined. This dominance dates to the Edo period (1603-1868), when the Tokugawa shogunate required every household to register with a local Buddhist temple — a system called terauke seido that effectively made temples the administrators of death, birth, and family records. The association between Buddhism and funerals became so deeply embedded that Japanese people sometimes joke that Buddhism in Japan is primarily a "funeral religion."

In Nara, this connection runs even deeper. The city's temples are among the oldest in Japan, and many of them have been conducting funeral and memorial rites continuously for over a millennium. When you see a funeral at Gangō-ji or Tōshōdai-ji, you are witnessing a tradition that has been practiced on that exact ground for more than 1,200 years.

### The Buddhist Understanding of Death

Japanese Buddhist funerals are grounded in the belief that death is not an ending but a transition. The deceased person is understood to be embarking on a journey to the next existence, and the funeral rites serve to ease that passage. The chanting of sutras by monks generates spiritual merit that is transferred to the deceased, helping them navigate the intermediate state between death and rebirth.

Different Buddhist sects — and Nara has an unusual concentration of Japan's oldest ones — have different specific beliefs and practices. The Kegon school based at Tōdai-ji, the Ritsu school at Tōshōdai-ji, and the various Pure Land traditions each bring slightly different theological emphases to the funeral process. But the general framework — sutra chanting, incense offering, and the generation of merit for the deceased — is shared across all of them.

## The Funeral Process

### What Happens Before the Funeral

When a person dies in Japan, the body is typically brought home or to a funeral hall, where it is laid out with the head pointing north — the direction associated with death in Buddhist cosmology, echoing the position in which the Buddha himself died. Dry ice is placed around the body to preserve it, and a small altar is arranged with incense, candles, a bowl of rice with chopsticks standing upright (a gesture exclusively associated with death and never performed with food for the living), and a knife or razor placed on the chest to ward off evil spirits.

The family contacts a Buddhist temple to arrange the funeral rites. In many cases, this is the family's ancestral temple, to which they have been connected for generations. The priest assigns the deceased a posthumous Buddhist name — a kaimyō — which is written in brush calligraphy on a wooden memorial tablet.

### The Tsuya: Evening Wake

The tsuya, or evening wake, is typically held the night before the funeral. Mourners gather at the temple or funeral hall, dressed in black. The priest chants sutras while mourners offer incense at the altar. The incense offering — shōkō — follows a specific protocol: the mourner approaches the altar, takes a pinch of powdered incense, raises it to forehead level, and places it on the burning charcoal in the incense burner. This is repeated two or three times, depending on the Buddhist sect.

After the formal rites, attendees typically share a meal. The atmosphere of the tsuya is more intimate than the funeral itself, and conversation about the deceased — sharing memories, telling stories — is encouraged.

### The Funeral Service: Sōgi

The funeral service proper usually takes place the following day. It follows a similar structure to the tsuya but is more formal. The priest delivers a longer series of sutra chantings, and the mourners offer incense in a prescribed order, beginning with the closest family members. A eulogy may be read, though this is a relatively modern addition to the ceremony.

What visitors are most likely to encounter is the exterior evidence of these events: black-suited mourners arriving, the sound of a wooden drum and chanting from within a temple hall, and the distinctive white-and-black curtains (kichō) that are sometimes hung outside the venue to mark the occasion.

### Cremation and Bone-Picking

After the funeral service, the body is taken to a crematorium. Following cremation, the family gathers for a ceremony called kotsuage, in which they use long chopsticks to pick bones from the ashes and place them in a ceramic urn. Pairs of mourners pass bones between chopsticks — a ritual action that explains why passing food between chopsticks at a dinner table is one of Japan's strongest taboos. This is not a ceremony that visitors will encounter, but understanding it explains a cultural prohibition that affects daily dining etiquette.

## Memorial Services: The Continuing Relationship

### The Schedule of Remembrance

Japanese Buddhism does not consider death to be the end of the relationship between the living and the dead. A structured series of memorial services — hōji — follows the funeral, marking the seventh day after death, the forty-ninth day (considered the completion of the soul's journey), the first anniversary, the third anniversary, the seventh, the thirteenth, and continuing at prescribed intervals up to the thirty-third or even fiftieth year after death.

These memorial services are what visitors to Nara's temples are most likely to encounter, as they are far more numerous than funerals themselves. A family may visit a temple to conduct a memorial service for an ancestor who died decades earlier. The service involves sutra chanting by a priest, incense offering by family members, and often a shared meal afterward.

### The Obon Season

Obon, typically observed in mid-August (though some regions celebrate it in July), is the period when the spirits of the dead are believed to return to visit their living family members. Temples throughout Nara hold special Obon services, graves are cleaned and decorated, and lanterns are lit to guide the returning spirits. The Obon season transforms Nara's temples into hubs of family activity, as people return to their ancestral homes and temples to honor their forebears.

For visitors, Obon is a fascinating time to be in Nara, though it is also one of the busiest travel periods in Japan. The lighting of lanterns at Tōdai-ji and Kasuga Taisha during this season creates an atmosphere of exceptional beauty.

## Etiquette for Visitors

### What to Do When You Encounter a Funeral

If you arrive at a temple to find a funeral in progress, the correct response is simple: be quiet, be respectful, and do not photograph the mourners or the ceremony. You are not required to leave — most temple grounds remain open even during funerals — but you should keep a respectful distance from the proceedings. If the main hall is being used for the service, visit other parts of the temple or return later.

You are not expected to offer incense or condolences unless you knew the deceased. Bowing slightly if you make eye contact with mourners is appropriate. Do not stare, and do not attempt to observe the ceremony from doorways or windows.

### Dress and Behavior at Temples

While there is no requirement for foreign visitors to wear black at temples, being dressed modestly and neatly shows respect. Loud conversation, laughter, and boisterous behavior are always inappropriate at temples but especially so when memorial services are taking place. Guests staying at nearby accommodations such as Kanoya ryokan, where the customs of thoughtful behavior are part of the experience, will find that the quieter sensibility cultivated in a traditional Japanese inn translates naturally to temple visits.

### Visiting Cemeteries

Japanese temple cemeteries are generally not off-limits to visitors, but they are active places of remembrance, not tourist attractions. Walk quietly, do not touch grave markers or offerings, and do not photograph individual graves. If you see a family tending a grave, give them space. The cemeteries attached to Nara's oldest temples — some with stones dating back centuries — are remarkable historical documents, but they are also places where grief is present and ongoing.

## The Changing Landscape

Japanese funeral customs are evolving. Smaller, more intimate services are becoming common as families shrink and traditional community ties weaken. Some families are choosing non-religious or simplified ceremonies. Cremation rates remain near 100 percent — the highest in the world — but the handling of ashes is diversifying, with options including scattering at sea, tree burial, and memorial diamonds.

Yet in Nara, where the temples are old and the connections between families and their ancestral temples remain stronger than in many urban areas, the traditional funeral and memorial system continues with remarkable vitality. The sound of sutra chanting for the dead has been heard in these temple halls for more than a thousand years, and it shows no sign of stopping.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will I encounter funerals when visiting temples in Nara?
It is quite possible, especially at active parish temples. Funerals and memorial services are conducted regularly at most functioning Buddhist temples in Nara.

### What should I do if a funeral is in progress at a temple I want to visit?
Remain quiet and respectful. You can usually visit other parts of the temple grounds, but avoid photographing mourners or the ceremony. If the main hall is in use, return later.

### Why do Japanese funerals use Buddhist rites?
*Approximately 90 percent of Japanese funerals follow Buddhist rites, a tradition dating to the Edo period when the Tokugawa shogunate required all households to register with Buddhist temples. This system made temples the administrators of death rites, establishing an association between Buddhism and funerals that persists to this day.*

### What is Obon and when does it occur?
Obon is a Buddhist observance in mid-August (or July in some regions) when spirits of the dead are believed to return to visit their families. Temples hold special services, graves are cleaned, and lanterns are lit to guide the spirits.

### Why is it taboo to pass food between chopsticks in Japan?
This action mirrors the kotsuage ceremony performed after cremation, in which family members use chopsticks to pass the bones of the deceased into a funerary urn. Replicating this gesture at the dining table is considered deeply inauspicious.
