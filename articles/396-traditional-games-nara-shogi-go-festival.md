# Traditional Games and Play in Nara: Shogi, Go, and Festival Games

Japan's traditional games are windows into the intellectual and social life of historical Japanese society. From the profound strategic depths of Go, which has been played for over four thousand years, to the lively competitive energy of festival games that bring communities together during shrine celebrations, games occupy a place in Japanese culture that transcends mere entertainment. Nara, as one of Japan's oldest cities, preserves connections to game traditions that stretch back to the founding of the capital and beyond.

For visitors interested in the intellectual culture of Japan, Nara offers opportunities to observe, learn, and play traditional games in settings that connect directly to their historical context.

## Shogi: Japanese Chess

Shogi, often described as Japanese chess, is one of the most intellectually demanding board games in the world. Played on a nine-by-nine grid with twenty pieces per side, shogi's distinctive feature is the rule that captured pieces can be returned to the board under the capturing player's control — a mechanic that eliminates the simplification that occurs in Western chess as pieces are exchanged, keeping the game complex and volatile throughout.

### Shogi and Nara's Unique Connection

Nara has a special relationship with shogi that goes beyond casual play. The city of Tendo in Yamagata Prefecture produces most of Japan's shogi pieces, but Nara's contribution to shogi culture lies in its historical connections to the game's development in Japan. Archaeological evidence suggests that early forms of shogi were played in the Nara and Heian periods, introduced from China along with Go and other continental cultural transmissions.

More significantly, Nara plays host to important shogi events at its historical venues. Professional shogi title matches have been held at Nara's traditional ryokan and cultural venues, where the combination of austere traditional architecture and the intellectual intensity of top-level play creates an atmosphere of remarkable concentration. These matches, broadcast live on television and followed by millions, bring national attention to Nara's cultural facilities.

### Where to Play and Watch Shogi in Nara

Casual shogi play occurs in parks, community centres, and cafes throughout the city. Nara Park itself is home to informal games on pleasant afternoons, where retirees set up boards on benches and play with the focus and courtesy that characterise Japanese game culture. Approaching these games as a spectator is acceptable — stand at a respectful distance and observe quietly. If you know the basics of shogi, asking to play is generally welcomed, though communication may require gesture and patience if neither party speaks the other's language.

Several community centres in Nara host regular shogi clubs that welcome beginners. These sessions provide instruction in basic rules and strategy, and the social atmosphere — tea, conversation, repeated games against improving opposition — offers a window into Japanese community life that few tourist activities can match.

## Go: The Oldest Board Game

Go (known as igo in Japanese and weiqi in Chinese) is among the oldest continuously played games in human history. Two players alternate placing black and white stones on a nineteen-by-nineteen grid, attempting to surround territory while capturing opponent stones. The rules are simple enough to learn in ten minutes; the strategy is deep enough to absorb a lifetime.

### Go in Japanese History and Nara

Go arrived in Japan from China during the Nara period, along with Buddhism, writing, and the other cultural transmissions that shaped Japanese civilisation. The game quickly became established among the aristocracy and was considered one of the four essential arts of the cultured person, alongside calligraphy, painting, and music.

During the Edo period, the Tokugawa shogunate established official Go academies (the "four houses" of Go) and sponsored annual castle games where the strongest players competed before the shogun. This institutional support elevated Go to a position of cultural prestige that it maintains today.

### Playing Go in Nara

Go salons — quiet rooms where players gather for serious games — exist in Nara, though they are less numerous than in larger cities. These establishments typically charge a small hourly fee and provide boards, stones, and opponents matched to your level. The atmosphere is contemplative: games can last two hours or more, and conversation during play is minimal.

For visitors who wish to learn Go, the game's basic rules can be absorbed in a single session, and playing on a smaller board (nine-by-nine rather than nineteen-by-nineteen) provides a satisfying experience for beginners. Several Nara community groups and cultural centres offer introductory sessions.

Go equipment makes an excellent and portable souvenir. Nara's craft shops sell wooden Go boards (goban), shell and slate stones in traditional materials, and decorative stone bowls (goke) that are beautiful objects in their own right. Machine-made sets are affordable; handcrafted sets using traditional materials — kaya wood boards, clamshell white stones, and slate black stones — are luxury items that serious players treasure for a lifetime.

## Festival Games and Seasonal Play

### Matsuri Games

Nara's festivals, like festivals throughout Japan, feature stalls offering traditional games that have entertained festival-goers for generations. These games are simple, colourful, and designed for all ages:

**Kingyo-sukui (goldfish scooping)** challenges players to catch goldfish from a shallow pool using a thin paper scoop that dissolves on contact with water. The game requires a delicate touch and a calm hand — qualities that children often possess in greater measure than adults.

**Shateki (cork gun shooting)** sets up rows of prizes — toys, snacks, figurines — that players attempt to knock down with cork-firing air rifles. The prizes wobble but rarely fall, which is both the frustration and the charm.

**Wanage (ring toss)** and **senbonbiki (string-pull lottery)** are games of pure chance dressed up in festive decoration. The prizes are secondary to the participation — the act of playing is itself the entertainment.

These festival games are concentrated during Nara's major festivals, particularly the summer Obon period, the autumn Shika-no-Tsunokiri (deer antler cutting) festival, and the January events around Kasuga Taisha. Even outside festival periods, small game stalls occasionally appear near popular tourist sites.

### Traditional Children's Games

Japanese children's games, while less commonly seen than in earlier generations, still appear in parks, schoolyards, and festival settings. Koma (spinning tops), kendama (the cup-and-ball skill toy), and otedama (Japanese juggling bags) have experienced revivals of interest, partly through social media and partly through cultural preservation efforts.

The Naramachi Mechanical Toy Museum displays traditional Japanese toys and games, providing historical context for play traditions that stretch back centuries. The museum's collection includes hand-carved wooden toys, paper-folding games, and mechanical devices that demonstrate remarkable ingenuity in their construction.

## Karuta: The Poetry Card Game

Karuta, a card game based on classical Japanese poetry, occupies a unique position at the intersection of game and literary culture. The game uses the one hundred poems of the Hyakunin Isshu — a thirteenth-century anthology compiled from earlier classical sources, including poems from the Man'yoshu — as its foundation.

In competitive karuta, a reader chants the first half of a poem while players race to identify and snatch the card bearing the poem's second half from the floor. The game requires memorisation of all one hundred poems and split-second physical reactions, making it simultaneously an intellectual and athletic pursuit.

Nara's connection to the poetry of the Man'yoshu gives karuta a particular resonance in the ancient capital. Several community groups practice competitive karuta, and the game is played enthusiastically in schools throughout the region.

## Where to Buy Game Equipment

Nara's craft shops and speciality retailers offer traditional game equipment ranging from affordable tourist items to museum-quality handcrafted sets. Shogi pieces carved from boxwood or tsuge (Japanese boxwood), Go boards in various sizes and materials, and karuta sets with traditional woodblock-printed cards are available in shops throughout Naramachi and the city centre.

For the finest quality, seek out specialist dealers who can explain the materials, craftsmanship, and grading of traditional game equipment. A well-made shogi set or Go board is a functional work of art that improves with age and use.

Visitors staying at Kanoya ryokan may find that the quiet evenings, free of the distractions of modern entertainment, provide the ideal setting for learning a traditional game — perhaps over a cup of tea in the ryokan's communal space, with the sounds of Naramachi's evening settling in around you.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I learn shogi or Go in Nara without speaking Japanese?
Yes, with some limitations. Both games' rules can be learned through demonstration, and many Japanese players are happy to teach through gesture and example. Community centres occasionally offer English-friendly sessions.

### Where can I watch professional shogi or Go in Nara?
Professional title matches are occasionally held at traditional venues in Nara and broadcast on television. Local tournaments at community centres are open to spectators and provide a more accessible viewing experience.

### What festival games can I play in Nara?
During major festivals, stalls offer goldfish scooping, cork gun shooting, ring toss, and lottery games. The summer Obon period and autumn festivals offer the widest selection.

### Is Go difficult to learn?
The rules of Go can be learned in ten minutes. Playing well requires study and practice, but beginners can enjoy satisfying games immediately on a small (nine-by-nine) board.

### What is a good game souvenir from Nara?
A carved shogi piece set, a small portable Go set, or a traditional karuta card set makes a meaningful and portable souvenir that connects to Japan's intellectual culture.

*Nara's connection to traditional Japanese games includes shogi, whose professional title matches are held at the city's historic venues, and Go, which arrived in Japan during the Nara period — while festival games at the city's shrines offer lively, accessible fun for visitors of all ages.*
