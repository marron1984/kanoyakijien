# Nara Festivals Calendar: Monthly Events and Celebrations Guide

Nara's festival calendar is unusual among Japanese cities in one important respect: its events are not primarily commercial or secular in origin. While most Japanese cities celebrate matsuri that mix religious tradition with community entertainment, Nara's major festivals are rooted in practices that have continued at the same temples and shrines, in some cases without interruption, for well over a thousand years. Attending one of these events is not like watching a cultural performance. It is participation — however peripheral — in a living tradition that connects the present to the Nara period itself.

The city's relatively compact size means that festivals have an intimate quality often lost in larger cities. Crowds gather, but they remain manageable. The settings — ancient temple grounds, lantern-lined shrine paths, hillsides overlooking the city — provide a backdrop that no purpose-built venue could replicate.

What follows is a month-by-month guide to Nara's most significant festivals and events. Dates are provided where fixed; some events follow the lunar calendar and shift slightly each year.

## January

### Yamayaki (Grass Burning of Mount Wakakusa) — Fourth Saturday of January

This is perhaps Nara's most visually spectacular event. On a winter evening, the entire hillside of Mount Wakakusa is set alight, creating a wall of flame visible from across the city. The ceremony begins at 17:15 with a procession of monks from Todai-ji and Kofuku-ji, followed by fireworks, and then the lighting of the grass at approximately 18:00.

The origins of Yamayaki are disputed — some accounts link it to a boundary dispute between the two temples, others to the clearing of ground after unsuccessful attempts to drive away wild boar. Regardless of its origins, the visual impact is extraordinary. The fire burns for roughly 30 minutes, and the combination of flames, fireworks, and the silhouette of ancient temples creates an image that remains long after the evening ends.

**Viewing tip**: The best public views are from the grounds of Nara Park, particularly near the Ukimido floating pavilion on Sagi-ike Pond, where the fire reflects on the water.

### Hatsu-mode (New Year Temple Visits) — January 1-3

Like the rest of Japan, Nara marks the New Year with visits to temples and shrines. Kasuga Taisha and Todai-ji both receive large numbers of visitors during the first three days of January. The atmosphere is festive but reverent — queues form, amazake (sweet sake) is served, and the temples are decorated with seasonal arrangements.

## February

### Mantoro (Lantern Festival at Kasuga Taisha) — February 3

Twice each year — in February and August — all 3,000 stone and bronze lanterns at Kasuga Taisha are lit simultaneously. The February event, coinciding with Setsubun (the seasonal turning point), transforms the shrine approach into a corridor of flickering light. The effect in the winter darkness is genuinely moving, particularly along the inner corridors where bronze hanging lanterns create patterns of shadow and warmth.

The February Mantoro begins at 18:00, and visitors can walk through the illuminated shrine grounds until approximately 20:30. Arriving early is advisable — the approach path fills quickly.

### Setsubun at Kofuku-ji and Todai-ji — February 3

Setsubun marks the eve of spring in the traditional calendar. At temples across Nara, the day involves the throwing of roasted soybeans (mamemaki) to drive away evil spirits. The ceremonies at Kofuku-ji and Todai-ji are among the most atmospheric, with monks in ceremonial dress scattering beans from temple platforms while crowds gather below.

## March

### Omizutori (Water-Drawing Ceremony at Todai-ji) — March 1-14

This is Nara's most sacred and most celebrated annual event. The Omizutori ceremony takes place at Todai-ji's Nigatsu-do (Second Month Hall) and has been performed every year without fail since 752 — an unbroken tradition of over 1,270 years.

The ceremony is a complex, two-week ritual of repentance performed by specially selected monks. For visitors, the most accessible element occurs each evening, when monks carry enormous pine torches along the balcony of Nigatsu-do, showering sparks down onto the gathered crowd. The burning embers are considered purifying, and people jostle to be touched by the falling sparks.

The ceremony's climax occurs in the early hours of March 13, when sacred water is drawn from a well beneath the hall — water that is said to flow from an underground source connected to a distant mountain. The drawing of this water (omizutori) gives the ceremony its name and signals the arrival of spring.

**Practical note**: The torch-bearing spectacle on March 12 draws the largest crowds. For a less crowded experience, attend on one of the earlier evenings (March 1-11), when the torches are smaller but the atmosphere is more intimate.

## April

### Cherry Blossom Viewing — Late March to Mid-April

Nara's cherry blossoms may lack the fame of those in Kyoto or Tokyo, but this works in the visitor's favour. The grounds of Nara Park, the slopes around Todai-ji, and the hills of Mount Yoshino (a short trip south) all offer superb hanami without the aggressive crowds that plague more famous locations.

Mount Yoshino, in particular, is considered one of Japan's greatest cherry blossom sites, with approximately 30,000 trees covering the mountainside in successive waves of pink from the lower to the upper slopes over a period of about three weeks.

### Kemari Hajime (Ancient Football Ceremony at Kasuga Taisha) — April

A ceremonial game of kemari — an ancient form of football in which players keep a deerskin ball aloft using their feet — is performed by participants in Heian-period court costume. The game is graceful rather than competitive, emphasising cooperation over scoring, and offers a rare glimpse into the leisure culture of the ancient court.

## May

### Takigi Noh (Torchlight Noh Performance) — May (Third Friday and Saturday)

One of Nara's most refined cultural events, Takigi Noh stages traditional Noh theatre outdoors at Kofuku-ji, illuminated only by the light of burning torchwood. The combination of the ancient performing art, the open-air setting, and the firelight creates an atmosphere of extraordinary beauty.

Performances typically begin at 17:30 and continue into the evening. Seating is available for purchase, though standing areas offer free viewing. Some familiarity with Noh conventions enhances the experience, but the visual and atmospheric power of the event is accessible to all.

## June and July

### Lily Festival (Saikusa Matsuri at Isagawa Shrine) — June 17

A small but beautiful procession in which young women carry bundles of lilies through the streets near Isagawa Shrine. The festival has roots in an ancient fertility rite and maintains a simplicity that larger events sometimes lose.

### Lantern-Lighting at Ukimido — July Evenings

During the summer months, the Ukimido floating pavilion on Sagi-ike Pond is illuminated in the evenings, creating one of Nara's most photogenic scenes. The hexagonal pavilion, reflected in the still water and framed by the surrounding park, is at its most evocative during this period.

## August

### Mantoro (Summer Lantern Festival at Kasuga Taisha) — August 14-15

The summer iteration of the Kasuga Taisha lantern lighting coincides with Obon, the Buddhist festival of the dead, giving the event a deeper emotional resonance than its February counterpart. The summer warmth, the sound of cicadas, and the knowledge that the lanterns are lit partly in remembrance of the departed create a particular atmosphere.

### Daimonji Okuribi — August 15

Large bonfires are lit on the hillsides around Nara to guide the spirits of the dead back to the afterlife at the close of Obon. While less famous than Kyoto's Gozan no Okuribi, Nara's version has its own quiet power.

## September to November

### Uneme Festival at Sarusawa Pond — Mid-September

A lantern-lit boat ceremony on Sarusawa Pond commemorating a court lady (uneme) who drowned herself in the pond after being rejected by the emperor. The festival has a melancholy beauty — paper lanterns are floated on the water and eventually set adrift, creating a scene of light and shadow.

### Shika no Tsunokiri (Deer Antler Cutting) — October (Early)

A unique Nara event in which the antlers of male deer in Nara Park are ceremonially cut. The practice dates to 1671 and was originally undertaken to prevent the deer from injuring people and each other during the autumn rutting season. The ceremony takes place at the Rokuen deer enclosure and involves men in traditional costume wrestling the deer into position for the painless procedure.

### Shosoin Exhibition at Nara National Museum — Late October to Early November

Each autumn, the Nara National Museum displays a selection of treasures from the Shosoin repository, the eighth-century storehouse at Todai-ji. Because the items are rotated annually, each year's exhibition is unique. The artefacts — Silk Road textiles, Tang Dynasty mirrors, Nara-period musical instruments — are of extraordinary quality, and the exhibition draws knowledgeable visitors from across Japan.

## December

### Kasuga Wakamiya On-Matsuri — December 15-18

The largest and oldest continuous festival in Nara, On-Matsuri has been performed annually since 1136. The four-day event includes a grand procession of participants in costumes spanning every era of Japanese history — from Nara-period court dress to Edo-period samurai armour — creating a walking timeline through the streets of the city. Ceremonial performances of Noh, Gagaku (court music), and Kagura (sacred dance) take place at temporary stages near Kasuga Taisha.

On-Matsuri is Nara's festival par excellence — a synthesis of history, religion, and community that distils the city's character into a single event.

## Planning Around Nara's Festivals

Timing a visit to coincide with one of Nara's major festivals adds immeasurably to the experience. For first-time visitors, the Omizutori ceremony in March and the Yamayaki fire festival in January are the most impactful. For returning visitors, the Shosoin Exhibition and the On-Matsuri in December offer deeper cultural engagement.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most famous festival in Nara?
Omizutori at Todai-ji, held annually from March 1-14, is considered Nara's most significant festival. The ceremony has been performed every year since 752 without interruption.

### When is the Nara fire festival?
The Yamayaki grass-burning ceremony takes place on the fourth Saturday of January on Mount Wakakusa. It begins with fireworks at approximately 17:15 and the grass lighting at 18:00.

### Are Nara's festivals free to attend?
Most festivals are free to observe. Some events, such as Takigi Noh and the Shosoin Exhibition, charge admission for seating or entry. Public viewing areas at major festivals are typically free.

### What is the best month to visit Nara for festivals?
March (for Omizutori), January (for Yamayaki), and December (for On-Matsuri) offer the most significant festivals. October and November combine the Shosoin Exhibition with autumn foliage.
