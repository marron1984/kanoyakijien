# Temple Bells of Nara: The Sound of the Sacred

There is a sound in Nara that predates everything you can see. Before the Great Buddha was cast, before Kasuga Taisha's first vermilion pillar was raised, before the deer were declared sacred messengers — there were bells. The low, resonant tone of a Japanese temple bell, a bonsho, carries across Nara's basin in the early morning and again at dusk, as it has for over twelve centuries. It is the oldest continuously produced sound in the city, and arguably the most important.

A bonsho is not rung for entertainment or decoration. It is a liturgical instrument with a specific purpose: to mark time, to summon the community, to dispel illusion, and to awaken all beings from the sleep of ignorance. When you hear a temple bell in Nara, you are hearing a sound that was designed to alter consciousness — and that has been doing so, ring by ring, for longer than most European cathedrals have existed.

## The Anatomy of a Japanese Temple Bell

### Form and Material

A bonsho is a large bronze bell, typically hanging in a dedicated bell tower called a shoro. Japanese temple bells differ fundamentally from Western church bells. They have no internal clapper. Instead, they are struck from the outside by a horizontal wooden beam called a shumoku, which is suspended by ropes and swung against the bell's exterior.

This external striking method produces the characteristic sound of a Japanese bell: a deep, complex tone that begins with a sharp attack and then unfolds into a long, slowly decaying resonance. The overtones are rich and shifting — as the sound fades, different frequencies emerge and recede, creating a sonic experience that can last thirty seconds or more from a single strike.

The bells are cast in bronze, an alloy of copper and tin, sometimes with small amounts of other metals. The exact composition affects the tone, and historical bell casters guarded their alloy formulas carefully. The surface of a bonsho is decorated with raised bands, lotus petal designs, and a striking point called a tsukiza, which is usually marked by a lotus flower or a raised circular boss.

### The Tsukiza: Where Sound Begins

The tsukiza is not merely decorative. It is the precise point at which the shumoku should strike the bell. Hitting the tsukiza produces the purest, most resonant tone. Striking elsewhere produces a different sound — still beautiful, but less focused, less rich in the specific harmonics that the bell caster intended. The placement and design of the tsukiza represent centuries of acoustic engineering, refined through trial and the trained ears of generations of craftsmen.

On the opposite side of the bell from the tsukiza, there is often a second tsukiza, sometimes used for the joya no kane ceremony at New Year. The two striking points can produce subtly different tones from the same bell, giving the bell a dual voice.

## Nara's Most Significant Bells

### Todai-ji's Bonsho

The bell of Todai-ji is one of the most famous in Japan. Cast in 752 CE — the same year the Great Buddha was consecrated — it weighs approximately 26.3 tonnes and stands over 3.8 meters tall. It is the second-largest historic bell in Japan and has been designated a National Treasure.

The bell hangs in a shoro on the hillside east of the Great Buddha Hall, partially hidden by trees. When it is struck — which occurs at specific times, most notably during the joya no kane ceremony on New Year's Eve — the sound carries across the entire Nara Basin. Residents of neighborhoods several kilometers distant report hearing it clearly on still winter nights.

The tone of Todai-ji's bell is extraordinarily deep, consistent with its massive size. The fundamental frequency is low enough to be felt as much as heard — a physical vibration that passes through the body. Standing near the bell tower when it is struck is a full-sensory experience that no recording can adequately reproduce.

### The Bell of Gangoji

Gangoji, tucked into the narrow streets of Naramachi, possesses a bell that is smaller than Todai-ji's but historically significant. Gangoji was one of the first Buddhist temples established in Japan, and its bell connects to the earliest period of Buddhism's presence in the country. The bell's tone is higher and brighter than Todai-ji's, fitting the intimate scale of the temple and the residential neighborhood that surrounds it.

### Kofuku-ji's Bell and the Evening Sky

Kofuku-ji's bell tower stands near the five-storey pagoda, and the sound of its bell at dusk — echoing across Sarusawa Pond and into the streets of central Nara — is one of the city's most evocative experiences. The combination of the bell's tone, the darkening sky, the silhouette of the pagoda, and the still water of the pond creates a multi-sensory moment that has inspired poets and painters for centuries.

### Toshodai-ji's Nara-Period Bell

Toshodai-ji, the temple founded by the Chinese monk Ganjin in 759, possesses a bell dating to the Nara period. This bell is among the oldest surviving bonsho in Japan and represents the pinnacle of eighth-century casting technique. Its tone has been described by bell scholars as particularly pure, with a harmonic profile that reflects the sophistication of the Nara-period bronze workers who created it.

## The Craft of Bell Casting

### A Tradition Spanning Millennia

Japanese bell casting arrived from the Asian continent along with Buddhism itself, in the sixth and seventh centuries. The earliest bells in Japan were modeled on Korean and Chinese prototypes, but Japanese casters quickly developed distinctive techniques and aesthetic preferences. By the Nara period, Japanese bonsho had evolved into a form recognizably different from their continental ancestors — larger, with a characteristic shape featuring a prominent crown and flared lip.

The casting process is extraordinary in its scale and complexity. A full-sized bonsho requires a mold constructed from multiple layers of clay, built around a core and surrounded by an outer shell. Molten bronze, heated to approximately 1,100 degrees Celsius, is poured into the space between core and shell. The pour must be continuous — a pause would create a seam in the metal that would compromise the bell's tone. For a large bell, this requires hundreds of kilograms of molten metal delivered in a single, uninterrupted flow.

After cooling, the mold is broken away and the bell is tuned. Unlike Western bells, which are tuned by shaving metal from the interior, Japanese bells are tuned by adjusting the thickness of the wall during the mold construction phase. The bell caster must predict, based on experience and accumulated knowledge, exactly how thick the wall must be at each point to produce the desired tone. There is no correction possible after casting — the bell either sounds as intended or it does not.

### The Sound of Imperfection

Not all bells achieve acoustic perfection, and Japanese aesthetic tradition does not always value perfection above all else. Some historic bells with irregular overtones or unusual harmonic profiles are prized precisely for their individuality. A bell that sounds slightly different from what was intended carries the mark of its specific creation — the particular conditions of that casting day, the exact composition of that batch of bronze, the hands of that specific craftsman. This appreciation for the unique qualities of individual bells connects to broader Japanese aesthetic principles of valuing the specific over the generic.

## The Spiritual Function of the Bell

### Joya no Kane: 108 Strokes at New Year

The most widely known use of the bonsho is the joya no kane ceremony, performed at midnight on New Year's Eve. The bell is struck 108 times, corresponding to the 108 bonnō — the worldly desires and delusions that, according to Buddhist teaching, bind human beings to suffering. Each stroke is believed to dispel one delusion, so that the listener enters the new year cleansed.

At Todai-ji, the joya no kane draws thousands of visitors who queue in the cold December night for the chance to hear — and sometimes to strike — the great bell. The sound rolling across the dark basin, repeated 108 times over the course of approximately an hour, is among the most profound auditory experiences available in Japan.

### Daily Timekeeping

Before clocks, the temple bell was the community's timekeeper. Bells were rung at dawn and dusk, and at intervals throughout the day corresponding to the monastic schedule. The sound of the bell divided the day into periods of work, prayer, and rest. In Nara, where multiple temples rang their bells at similar times, the overlapping sounds created a sonic landscape — a chorus of bronze voices at different distances and pitches, marking the hours.

This function is diminished but not extinct. Several of Nara's temples still ring their bells at traditional times, and the sound remains a quiet structuring presence in the lives of nearby residents.

## Hearing Nara's Bells Today

### Where and When

The most reliable way to hear a bonsho in Nara is to visit a temple during morning or evening services. Todai-ji, Kofuku-ji, and Yakushi-ji all maintain bell-ringing schedules, though these are not always publicized to tourists. Early morning — before 7:00 AM — is the most likely time to hear a bell ring as part of the daily liturgy rather than a special event.

New Year's Eve remains the supreme occasion. If you are in Nara on December 31st, the experience of walking through the city as bells ring from multiple temples simultaneously is unforgettable. The sounds overlap and interfere, creating complex acoustic patterns that shift as you move through the streets.

### Listening as Practice

The Japanese verb for listening to a bell is not simply "kiku" (to hear) but "kikiiru" — to listen deeply, to enter into the sound. This distinction suggests that hearing a bonsho is not passive. It is an active engagement with the sound, a form of meditation. The long decay of a single bell stroke — the way it fades and transforms over thirty seconds, moving through different harmonic states — rewards sustained, focused attention.

Guests staying at Kanoya ryokan in Naramachi are within earshot of several temple bells and can experience the early morning and evening ringings from the quiet of their rooms — a way to hear the bells as Nara's residents have heard them for centuries, woven into the texture of daily life rather than isolated as a tourist attraction.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do Japanese temple bells have no clapper?

Japanese temple bells are struck from the outside by a suspended wooden beam, which produces a deeper, more resonant tone with a longer decay than an internal clapper would create. This external striking method was inherited from Chinese and Korean bell-making traditions.

### How many times is a temple bell struck?

It varies by occasion. During the joya no kane ceremony at New Year, the bell is struck 108 times. For daily timekeeping, the number of strokes traditionally corresponded to the time of day. For general ceremonial purposes, the number varies by temple and occasion.

### Can visitors strike a temple bell?

Some temples allow visitors to strike the bell, particularly during the New Year period. Todai-ji's joya no kane ceremony permits members of the public to participate by pulling the shumoku rope. Outside of special occasions, bell striking is typically reserved for temple staff.

### What is the oldest temple bell in Nara?

*The oldest surviving temple bells in Nara date to the Nara period (710-794 CE). Toshodai-ji possesses a bell from this era, and several other Nara-period bells survive in temple collections. Todai-ji's famous bell, cast in 752 CE, is among the oldest large-scale bonsho still in regular use in Japan.*
