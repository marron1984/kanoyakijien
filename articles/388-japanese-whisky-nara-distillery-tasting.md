# Japanese Whisky and Nara: Distillery Visits and Tasting

Japanese whisky has become one of the world's most sought-after spirits, winning international awards and commanding prices that rival the finest Scotch single malts. While the distilleries that produce Japan's most famous expressions are scattered across the country, the Kansai region — and the area around Nara in particular — holds a special place in Japanese whisky history. The birthplace of commercial Japanese whisky production lies within easy reach of the ancient capital, and the region's bars and shops offer tasting experiences that range from introductory to deeply specialist.

For the whisky-interested visitor to Nara, the combination of distillery visits, knowledgeable bar culture, and the city's own traditions of craft and refinement creates a compelling drinking itinerary.

## The Birth of Japanese Whisky: Yamazaki

The story of Japanese whisky begins at Yamazaki, located on the border of Kyoto and Osaka, approximately one hour from Nara by train. In 1923, Shinjiro Torii — founder of what would become Suntory — chose this site for Japan's first commercial whisky distillery, drawn by the exceptional quality of the local water and the humid, misty climate that promotes maturation.

### Visiting Yamazaki Distillery

The Yamazaki Distillery offers guided tours that walk visitors through the whisky-making process: malting, mashing, fermentation in wooden washbacks, distillation in copper pot stills of various shapes and sizes, and maturation in a range of cask types including American oak, sherry, and the distinctly Japanese mizunara (Japanese oak). The tour concludes with a tasting session that typically includes several expressions not widely available outside the distillery.

Reservations are essential and should be made well in advance — Yamazaki is one of the most popular distillery tours in the world, and slots fill quickly, particularly on weekends and during peak travel seasons. Tours are offered in Japanese and English. The distillery also houses an excellent whisky library and tasting room where visitors can sample rare expressions by the glass, including vintage bottlings that are otherwise unobtainable.

The adjacent Yamazaki Whisky Museum provides historical context, displaying original equipment, archival photographs, and the personal effects of the distillery's founders. The museum shop sells distillery-exclusive bottlings and branded merchandise.

The journey from Nara to Yamazaki takes approximately one hour via Kintetsu and JR trains, making it a comfortable half-day excursion. Combine the visit with a stop in Kyoto on the return journey for a full day of cultural and gustatory exploration.

## Craft Distilleries in the Nara Region

While Yamazaki commands the most attention, the craft distillery movement has brought whisky production closer to Nara itself. Several small-scale distilleries have established operations in the Kansai region, producing distinctive spirits that reflect local terroir and individual philosophies.

### Nara Prefecture's Emerging Whisky Scene

Nara Prefecture's long tradition of sake brewing has provided a foundation of fermentation expertise, water knowledge, and grain-handling skill that translates naturally to whisky production. Several sake breweries in the region have diversified into spirit production, and a small number of dedicated craft distilleries have emerged.

These operations are typically small — producing hundreds rather than thousands of casks per year — and their whiskies are characterised by experimentation with local ingredients, unconventional cask types, and maturation environments unique to the Nara basin's climate. Some use locally grown barley or rice, while others experiment with ageing in casks made from Japanese cedar or plum wine barrels.

Visiting these smaller operations often provides a more intimate experience than the major distillery tours. Conversations with distillers are unhurried, and the opportunity to taste works in progress — new-make spirit, young whiskies at various stages of maturation — offers insight into the craft that finished bottle tastings cannot provide.

## Sake and Whisky: Connected Traditions

Understanding Nara's sake heritage deepens appreciation of Japanese whisky. The two traditions share fundamental elements: an obsession with water quality, meticulous attention to fermentation, and a reverence for seasonal variation. Many Japanese whisky distillers trained initially in sake brewing, and the precision and patience of sake culture inform whisky production throughout the country.

Nara is one of the birthplaces of refined sake brewing. The monks of Shoryaku-ji temple, in the hills south of the city, developed the pasteurisation technique known as hiire in the fifteenth century — three centuries before Louis Pasteur's European discovery. Visiting a Nara sake brewery and a whisky distillery on the same trip provides a connected understanding of Japanese fermentation culture that enriches both experiences.

## Where to Drink Whisky in Nara

### Whisky Bars

Nara's bar scene is intimate and personal, characterised by small, owner-operated establishments where the bartender's knowledge and curation are the primary attractions. Several bars in central Nara and Naramachi maintain impressive whisky collections, including rare Japanese expressions, Scottish single malts, and craft whiskies from smaller producers.

The best Nara whisky bars operate in the classic Japanese bar style: small rooms seating ten to fifteen guests, dimly lit, with the bartender presiding over a back bar of carefully selected bottles. Conversation is welcome but unhurried, and the bartender's role extends beyond serving drinks to guiding guests through the collection with personalised recommendations.

Expect to find bottles at these bars that are unavailable in ordinary retail — allocated Japanese whiskies, single-cask bottlings, and vintage expressions that the bar owner has acquired through long-standing trade relationships. Prices for rare pours can be significant, but the experience of tasting a twenty-year-old Yamazaki or a discontinued Karuizawa in a quiet Nara bar, with expert commentary from the bartender, justifies the investment.

### Hotel and Ryokan Bars

Several of Nara's upmarket accommodation options maintain curated whisky selections. The experience of ending a day of temple visits with a dram of Japanese whisky in the quiet of a ryokan lounge connects two forms of Japanese refinement: the ancient and the modern.

### Izakaya and Restaurants

Many of Nara's better izakaya and restaurants offer Japanese whisky alongside their food menus. Pairing whisky with Japanese cuisine — particularly grilled meats, rich stews, and aged cheeses — is an increasingly popular practice in Japan and one that Nara's restaurants handle with sophistication.

## Buying Whisky in Nara

Japanese whisky allocation has tightened considerably in recent years, and popular expressions like Yamazaki 12 and Hibiki Japanese Harmony are frequently out of stock at retail. However, Nara's smaller speciality shops and liquor stores sometimes carry bottles that have disappeared from shelves in larger cities. Building a relationship with a local shop — returning to browse, asking for recommendations, expressing genuine interest rather than investment motivation — can yield access to bottles that appear briefly and sell quickly.

The Yamazaki Distillery shop is one of the most reliable sources for distillery-exclusive bottlings, including single-cask expressions and limited seasonal releases. Purchases are limited per visitor to prevent resale hoarding.

For visitors wishing to bring whisky home, Japanese customs allows the export of reasonable quantities for personal use. Pack bottles carefully in checked luggage — Japanese retail shops will provide protective wrapping upon request. Be aware of your home country's import allowances and duty regulations.

## Whisky Tasting Events and Experiences

The Kansai region hosts periodic whisky tasting events, festivals, and masterclasses that bring together producers, collectors, and enthusiasts. These events range from large public festivals with dozens of exhibitors to intimate seated tastings guided by distillers or brand ambassadors. Timing a Nara visit to coincide with a whisky event in Osaka or Kyoto adds a social dimension to the experience.

For a more private experience, several Nara-based bartenders and whisky specialists offer private tasting sessions that can be arranged through accommodation. These sessions, typically lasting one to two hours, provide guided exploration of Japanese whisky styles, production methods, and regional characteristics. Kanoya ryokan in Naramachi can assist guests in arranging such experiences, connecting visitors with local whisky knowledge.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I visit Yamazaki Distillery from Nara?
Yes. Yamazaki is approximately one hour from Nara by train. Tours must be booked well in advance through the Suntory website, as they sell out quickly.

### Is Japanese whisky expensive?
Entry-level Japanese whiskies such as Suntory Toki and Nikka Days are moderately priced. Premium aged expressions like Yamazaki 12 or Hakushu 12 command higher prices, and rare or discontinued bottles can reach collector-level pricing.

### What is mizunara oak?
Mizunara is a species of Japanese oak used for whisky maturation casks. It imparts distinctive flavours of sandalwood, incense, and coconut that are unique to Japanese whisky. Mizunara-aged whiskies are highly prized.

### Can I buy rare Japanese whisky in Nara?
Small specialist shops in Nara occasionally stock allocated bottles that are unavailable in larger cities. Building a relationship with shop owners and visiting multiple times increases your chances.

### How should I drink Japanese whisky?
Japanese whisky tradition offers several serving styles: neat, on the rocks, with a small amount of water (mizuwari), or as a tall highball with soda water (highball). The highball is Japan's most popular whisky serve and pairs excellently with food.
