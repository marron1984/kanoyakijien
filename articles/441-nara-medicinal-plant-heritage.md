# Nara's Medicinal Plant Heritage: Yakusō-en and Herbal Traditions

Long before Nara was famous for its deer or its Great Buddha, it was famous for its medicine. The hills surrounding the ancient capital were among the first places in Japan where medicinal herbs were systematically gathered, cultivated, and studied — a tradition that stretches back more than 1,400 years and continues, quietly but persistently, to this day. Nara is, in a very real sense, the birthplace of Japanese pharmaceutical culture, and understanding this history transforms a visit to the region's gardens and temples into something considerably more layered than standard sightseeing.

## The Deep Roots of Nara's Herbal Tradition

### The Nihon Shoki and Emperor Suiko's Herb Gathering

The earliest recorded connection between Nara and medicinal plants appears in the Nihon Shoki, Japan's first official history, compiled in 720 CE. An entry for the year 611 describes Emperor Suiko organizing a kusagari — a ceremonial herb-gathering expedition — in the fields of Yamato Province, the area that encompasses modern Nara. This was not a casual stroll through meadows. The kusagari was a formal court event, reflecting the importance that the Yamato rulers placed on the collection of healing plants.

The entry is significant because it establishes that by the early seventh century, the Japanese court already possessed systematic knowledge of which plants had medicinal properties and where they grew. This knowledge had arrived, like much else in this period, from the Korean Peninsula and China, filtered through the Buddhist monks and continental scholars who served as the primary conduits of learning between the Asian mainland and Japan.

### The Shōsō-in Treasury: Medicine Preserved for a Millennium

The most remarkable physical evidence of Nara's pharmaceutical heritage sits inside the Shōsō-in, the eighth-century storehouse adjacent to Tōdai-ji. Among the thousands of treasures preserved in this climate-controlled wooden building are approximately sixty varieties of medicinal substances — dried herbs, mineral compounds, animal-derived materials, and aromatic woods — many of which were part of Emperor Shōmu's personal pharmacopoeia before his widow, Empress Kōmyō, donated them to the temple in 756 CE.

These substances have been analyzed by modern scientists and found to include cinnamon bark, cloves, rhubarb root, ginseng, camphor, and numerous other materials that remain in use in traditional East Asian medicine today. Some were locally gathered; others had traveled the Silk Road from as far as Persia and the Indian subcontinent. The fact that Nara in the eighth century was a terminus for a pharmaceutical trade network spanning half of Asia is a detail that deserves more attention than it typically receives.

The Shōsō-in medicines are not on permanent public display — the treasury opens its contents for a brief exhibition each autumn — but their existence confirms that Nara was not merely gathering wild herbs from nearby hillsides. It was the center of an international medicinal trade.

### Monks as Pharmacists

Throughout the Nara and Heian periods, Buddhist temples functioned as the primary institutions of medical care. Monks studied Chinese medical texts, cultivated medicinal gardens within temple grounds, and dispensed herbal preparations to the surrounding population. Gangō-ji, Yakushi-ji, and Tōdai-ji all maintained gardens of healing plants. The very name Yakushi-ji — Temple of the Medicine Buddha — reflects the inseparability of spiritual and physical healing in this era.

The monk Jianzhen, known in Japanese as Ganjin, who arrived in Nara in 753 after five failed attempts to cross from China, brought with him not only the Ritsu school of Buddhist discipline but also extensive knowledge of Chinese medicine and a collection of medicinal substances. His temple, Tōshōdai-ji, became an important center for pharmaceutical knowledge. The connection between Ganjin and medicine is commemorated to this day in Nara's traditional pharmaceutical industry.

## Yakusō-en: Nara's Medicinal Plant Gardens

### The Concept of the Yakusō-en

A yakusō-en is a garden dedicated to the cultivation of medicinal plants — a living pharmacy, organized not by aesthetics but by therapeutic function. While Japanese gardens are often celebrated for their visual beauty, the yakusō-en operates on a different principle: every plant is present because of what it does, not how it looks. That said, many medicinal plants are strikingly beautiful, and a well-maintained yakusō-en can be as visually rewarding as any ornamental garden.

### Nara's Notable Medicinal Gardens

Several locations in and around Nara maintain yakusō-en or medicinal plant collections that visitors can explore.

**The Nara Prefectural Medicinal Plant Garden** in Takatori, about thirty minutes south of central Nara, is the most significant. Operated by the prefectural government, this garden cultivates approximately 500 species of medicinal plants from Japan and abroad. The grounds are organized by therapeutic category — plants for digestive ailments, respiratory conditions, skin treatments, and so on — and each specimen is carefully labeled with its Japanese name, Latin classification, and traditional uses. Admission is free, and the garden is open year-round, though spring and early summer offer the most impressive displays.

**Temple gardens** throughout Nara preserve medicinal plantings that date back centuries. At Gangō-ji in Naramachi, the temple garden includes medicinal species that have been cultivated on the site since the Nara period. Tōshōdai-ji maintains plantings connected to Ganjin's pharmaceutical legacy. These are not always labeled as medicinal gardens, and visitors may walk past them without realizing what they contain — but informed visitors will recognize familiar herbs among the ornamental plantings.

**The Yoshino region**, in the mountains south of Nara city, has been a source of wild medicinal plants for centuries. The steep, forested slopes support a remarkable diversity of species, and herbal gathering traditions persist in some mountain communities. While casual visitors are unlikely to encounter these traditions directly, the herbal products of Yoshino are available in shops throughout Nara.

## Daranisuke and Nara's Living Pharmaceutical Tradition

### The Oldest Medicine in Japan

Nara's most famous medicinal product is daranisuke, a bitter stomach medicine made primarily from the bark of the kihada tree (Phellodendron amurense, or Amur cork tree). The medicine takes its name from the Dharani — Buddhist mantras — that monks reportedly chanted during its preparation. Its production in Nara is documented from at least the eighth century, making it one of the oldest continuously produced medicines in the world.

Daranisuke is intensely bitter — a characteristic that Japanese tradition considers evidence of its efficacy. The bark is dried, ground, and formed into small dark pills or dissolved in liquid. It is used as a digestive aid, a remedy for stomach upset, and a general tonic. The taste is memorable, and trying it is something of a rite of passage for visitors interested in Nara's pharmaceutical heritage.

### Naramachi's Medicine Shops

Several shops in Naramachi, the old merchant quarter, continue to sell traditional medicines including daranisuke. These shops often occupy buildings that have housed pharmaceutical businesses for generations. The experience of stepping into one of these establishments — with their wooden drawers of dried herbs, their medicinal aromas, and their unhurried service — offers a direct encounter with a commercial tradition that predates the founding of most European universities.

Some shops also sell herbal teas, medicinal bath preparations, and traditional cosmetic products derived from plant ingredients. These make distinctive souvenirs with the considerable advantage of being lightweight, compact, and unlike anything available at home.

## Visiting Nara's Herbal Heritage: Practical Information

### Getting Oriented

Begin with the Naramachi neighborhood, where pharmaceutical shops and small museums provide an accessible introduction to the tradition. The Naramachi Kōshi-no-Ie, a restored traditional townhouse open to the public, occasionally hosts exhibits related to local crafts and industries, including traditional medicine. From there, visitors with deeper interest can plan trips to the Prefectural Medicinal Plant Garden and to temple gardens with pharmaceutical connections.

**Stay in Naramachi or nearby** to experience the area's atmosphere in the morning and evening, when the tourist crowds have departed. Guests at traditional accommodations like Kanoya ryokan, located in the heart of the historic district, can walk the same streets where medicine merchants once conducted their trade, in the quiet of early morning before the shops have opened.

### Seasonal Timing

Spring and early summer are ideal for visiting medicinal gardens, when plants are most actively growing. Autumn brings the Shōsō-in Exhibition and the harvest season for many medicinal species. Winter, while quieter, offers the advantage of uncrowded gardens and the opportunity to see evergreen medicinal plants — camphor, camellia, nandina — against bare branches.

## The Philosophical Dimension

Nara's medicinal plant heritage offers something that few tourist attractions can provide: a direct encounter with the idea that the natural world is a pharmacy, that healing is embedded in the landscape, and that knowledge of plants is a form of cultural inheritance as valuable as any temple or sculpture. In a period when interest in traditional and plant-based medicine is surging worldwide, Nara's 1,400-year-old pharmaceutical tradition feels less like a historical relic and more like a living source of practical wisdom.

The gardens, the temples, the bitter medicine still sold in Naramachi shops — all of these are threads in a continuous tradition that connects the herb-gathering expeditions of the seventh century to the present day. Walking through a yakusō-en in Nara, reading the labels on plants that have been used to heal for a millennium and more, is a reminder that the relationship between humans and the plant kingdom is one of the oldest and most essential relationships we possess.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is daranisuke and where can I buy it in Nara?
Daranisuke is a traditional bitter stomach medicine made from kihada (Amur cork tree) bark, produced in Nara for over 1,300 years. It is available at traditional pharmaceutical shops in Naramachi and around Nara Park.

### Can I visit a medicinal plant garden in Nara?
Yes. The Nara Prefectural Medicinal Plant Garden in Takatori is open to the public free of charge and cultivates hundreds of medicinal species with informative labeling.

### Why is Nara important in Japanese pharmaceutical history?
*Nara is considered the birthplace of Japanese pharmaceutical culture because it was the center of systematic medicinal plant gathering, cultivation, and trade from as early as the seventh century, a tradition documented in the Nihon Shoki and preserved in the Shōsō-in treasury's collection of over sixty medicinal substances dating to 756 CE.*

### What is the best season to visit Nara's medicinal plant gardens?
Late spring through early summer (May to July) offers the most active plant growth and blooming. Autumn is ideal for the Shōsō-in Exhibition, which occasionally displays medicinal materials from the treasury.

### Is Nara's medicinal plant tradition still alive today?
Yes. Nara Prefecture remains a center of Japan's pharmaceutical industry, and traditional preparations like daranisuke continue to be produced and sold using methods that have changed little over centuries.
