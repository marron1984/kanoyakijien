# Asuka Village Day Trip: Japan's Pre-Nara Ancient Capital

Before Nara became the first permanent capital of Japan in 710, political power resided some twenty-five kilometres to the south, in a quiet valley now known as Asuka Village. For roughly a century and a half — from the mid-sixth century to the early eighth — Asuka was the seat of the imperial court, the site where Buddhism first took root in Japanese governance, and the birthplace of the centralised state. Today, the village is a remarkably peaceful landscape of rice paddies, ancient stone monuments, painted tomb chambers, and temple ruins that together tell the story of Japan's formative centuries.

A day trip from Nara to Asuka is one of the most rewarding excursions available to visitors interested in Japanese history. The journey takes barely forty minutes by train, yet the shift in atmosphere is dramatic: from Nara's busy temple precincts to Asuka's unhurried rural lanes, where farmers tend fields that have been cultivated since the age of the emperors who once lived here.

## Why Asuka Matters

The Asuka period (538–710) was one of the most transformative eras in Japanese history. It was during this time that Buddhism arrived from the Korean kingdom of Baekje and became intertwined with Japanese statecraft. Prince Shotoku, one of the most celebrated figures in Japanese history, promoted Buddhist teachings and Chinese political models from his base in the Asuka region. The Taika Reforms of 645, which established a centralised government modelled on Tang China, were enacted here. The first comprehensive law codes, the first official histories, and the first diplomatic missions to China were all products of the Asuka court.

Yet unlike Nara and Kyoto, which remained major cities after their time as capitals, Asuka reverted to its rural character once the court moved north to the new capital at Fujiwara-kyo and then to Nara. This accident of history preserved the landscape in something close to its ancient state. Archaeological discoveries continue to emerge from the fields and hillsides, and the village operates less as a museum than as a living palimpsest where contemporary agriculture overlies fifteen centuries of history.

## Getting to Asuka from Nara

The most convenient route is the Kintetsu Railway. From Kintetsu Nara Station, take the Kintetsu Nara Line to Yamato-Saidaiji, transfer to the Kintetsu Kashihara Line, and ride south to Asuka Station. The journey takes approximately thirty-five to forty minutes and costs around 600 yen. Alternatively, from JR Nara Station, take the JR Sakurai Line (Man'yo Mahoroba Line) to Sakurai and transfer, though the Kintetsu route is faster and more frequent.

Asuka Station is small and quiet, with a tourist information centre where you can pick up English-language maps and rent bicycles — the preferred and strongly recommended way to explore the village.

## Exploring by Bicycle

Asuka is spread across a gently rolling valley roughly four kilometres long and two kilometres wide. Walking is possible but slow; cycling allows you to cover the major sites comfortably in five to six hours while stopping frequently. Bicycle rental shops cluster around Asuka Station, offering standard and electric-assist models. Electric-assist bikes are worth the modest surcharge, as some hills are steep enough to test casual riders.

The standard cycling route forms a loose loop through the valley, passing the major monuments in roughly chronological order. Most sites are free or charge a nominal admission fee.

## Essential Sites

### Asuka-dera

Japan's first true Buddhist temple, founded in 596 by Soga no Umako, the powerful clan leader who championed Buddhism against rival aristocratic families. The original temple complex was vast, but only a modest hall survives today, sheltering the Asuka Daibutsu — a bronze seated Buddha cast in 609 by the sculptor Tori Busshi. This is the oldest known Buddha image in Japan still in its original location. The statue's face, with its archaic smile and elongated features, reflects the artistic style of sixth-century Korea and China, offering a direct visual link to the continental origins of Japanese Buddhism.

### Takamatsuzuka Tomb

Discovered in 1972, this small burial mound from the late seventh or early eighth century caused a sensation when its interior murals were revealed. The paintings — depicting court figures in Tang-dynasty dress, celestial constellations, and the Chinese directional guardians (the Azure Dragon, White Tiger, Vermilion Bird, and Black Tortoise) — demonstrated the sophistication of Asuka-period court culture. The original tomb is now sealed for conservation, but the adjacent Takamatsuzuka Mural Hall displays meticulous reproductions alongside explanatory exhibits. The quality of the original paintings, executed in vivid mineral pigments on plastered stone walls, remains astonishing.

### Kitora Tomb

A second painted tomb, slightly older than Takamatsuzuka, discovered in 1983. Its murals include the oldest known astronomical chart in East Asia, painted on the ceiling, along with the four directional guardian animals. The Kitora Tomb Experience Museum, opened in 2016, presents the restored murals in a climate-controlled environment with detailed interpretation. The astronomical chart alone — with its careful depiction of star positions and celestial equator — speaks to the scientific knowledge available at the Asuka court.

### Ishibutai Kofun

The most visually striking monument in Asuka is this massive stone burial chamber, its earthen mound long since eroded away, leaving the enormous granite slabs exposed to the sky. The capstone alone weighs an estimated seventy-seven tonnes. The tomb is traditionally attributed to Soga no Umako, who died in 626. Visitors can enter the chamber and stand beneath the colossal stones, contemplating the engineering skill and political power required to quarry, transport, and assemble them. In spring, the surrounding park fills with cherry blossoms, creating one of the most photographed scenes in rural Nara Prefecture.

### Oka-dera (Ryugai-ji)

Perched on a hillside above the valley, this seventh-century temple houses a large clay Buddha image — the largest of its kind in Japan — and offers sweeping views across the Asuka landscape. The climb is short but steep, and the temple's gardens are particularly beautiful during the hydrangea season in June.

### Stone Monuments

Scattered across the village are enigmatic stone carvings whose original purpose remains debated. The Monkey Stones (Saru-ishi) are carved with simian and human figures. The Turtle Stone (Kame-ishi) is a massive granite boulder shaped like a turtle. The Masuda no Iwafune is a rectangular stone structure, perhaps an unfinished tomb, sitting incongruously on a hilltop. And the Sake-bune Stone (Sakafune-ishi) features channels and basins carved into its surface, possibly used in sake production or water rituals. These mysterious objects contribute to Asuka's atmosphere of deep, not-fully-explained antiquity.

## The Asuka Historical National Government Park

The national park encompassing the village provides well-maintained cycling paths, rest areas, and interpretive signage in multiple languages. The landscape itself — terraced rice paddies backed by forested hills, narrow lanes lined with traditional farmhouses — is part of the experience. Asuka has been deliberately preserved against development, and the effect is of a countryside that feels continuous with the ancient past, even as tractors work the fields and schoolchildren cycle to class.

## Practical Tips

**Best time to visit**: Spring (late March through April) brings cherry blossoms to Ishibutai and the surrounding parks. Autumn offers warm colours and comfortable cycling weather. Summer is hot and humid; start early and carry water. Winter is quiet and cold but beautifully clear.

**Time needed**: A full day allows unhurried exploration. Five to six hours of cycling covers the major sites comfortably, with time for lunch.

**Lunch options**: Several small restaurants near Asuka-dera and Ishibutai serve local cuisine, including asuka nabe (a milk-based hot pot unique to the region) and kakinoha-zushi (pressed sushi wrapped in persimmon leaves). The village is not well supplied with convenience stores, so consider packing snacks.

**Combine with**: Kashihara Jingu, the grand Shinto shrine dedicated to Japan's legendary first emperor, is two stations north of Asuka on the Kintetsu Line and makes a natural pairing for a full day in the southern Nara basin.

**Access**: Most sites are wheelchair-accessible along the main cycling paths, though some temple grounds and the Ishibutai chamber involve steps.

## Staying in Nara

Asuka is best visited as a day trip from Nara, returning in the evening to the comforts of the city. Guests at Kanoya ryokan, situated in Nara's historic core, find that the short train journey south to Asuka and the return north in the late afternoon make for a satisfying rhythm — the contemplative emptiness of Asuka's ancient landscape balanced against the warmth and refinement of a traditional ryokan evening.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I get from Nara to Asuka Village?

Take the Kintetsu Railway from Kintetsu Nara Station, transferring at Yamato-Saidaiji to the Kashihara Line, then ride south to Asuka Station. The journey takes approximately thirty-five to forty minutes and costs around 600 yen.

### Is Asuka Village worth visiting?

Asuka is essential for anyone interested in early Japanese history. Its ancient tombs, Japan's oldest Buddha statue, mysterious stone monuments, and beautifully preserved rural landscape offer a depth of historical experience that few places in Japan can match.

### Can I explore Asuka Village on foot?

Walking is possible but the sites are spread across several kilometres. Renting a bicycle at Asuka Station is strongly recommended and allows you to cover all major sites comfortably in five to six hours.

### What is the Asuka Daibutsu?

The Asuka Daibutsu is a bronze seated Buddha cast in 609, housed at Asuka-dera. It is the oldest known Buddha image in Japan still in its original location, predating the Great Buddha at Todai-ji by over a century.
