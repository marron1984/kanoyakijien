# The History of Nara's Street Grid: Urban Planning in Ancient Japan

Stand at the Suzaku Gate reconstruction on the grounds of the Heijo Palace site and look south. The broad, grassy expanse before you — Suzaku Oji, the central boulevard of the ancient capital — runs straight as a drawn line toward the horizon. Twelve hundred years ago, this road was 70 meters wide, flanked by willow trees and drainage channels, lined with the residences of aristocrats and the offices of government. It was the spine of Heijo-kyo, the capital city built in 710 CE, and it was the most ambitious piece of urban planning that Japan had ever attempted.

The grid that radiated from this central axis organized not merely streets and buildings but an entire civilization's idea of how ordered human life should appear on the landscape. Understanding Nara's street grid is understanding the moment when Japan first imagined itself as an urban nation.

## The Model: Tang Dynasty Chang'an

### Why Japan Looked to China

The decision to build Heijo-kyo as a grid city was not an indigenous development but a deliberate importation of Chinese urban theory. In the seventh and eighth centuries, Japan's ruling elite was engaged in a comprehensive project of adopting Chinese systems of government, law, religion, writing, and urban design. The Chinese capital of Chang'an (modern Xi'an), then the largest and most cosmopolitan city in the world with a population exceeding one million, provided the template.

Japanese envoys to the Tang court returned with detailed descriptions of Chang'an's layout: a rectangular walled city oriented on a north-south axis, divided by a broad central avenue, with a palace compound at the northern end and the city arrayed symmetrically to east and west. The grid of streets created regular blocks (fang in Chinese, bo in Japanese) that organized residence, commerce, and administration with bureaucratic precision.

For Japan's reforming rulers — engaged in centralizing power under the emperor through the Ritsuryo legal codes — this geometric order was not merely practical but ideological. A grid city announced that the emperor, positioned at its northern apex, commanded a rational, organized realm. The city's geometry was a political statement rendered in earth and timber.

### Adaptations for Japanese Conditions

Heijo-kyo was not a copy of Chang'an but an adaptation. Several significant modifications reflected Japanese conditions and sensibilities:

**Scale.** Chang'an covered roughly 84 square kilometers; Heijo-kyo was approximately 25 square kilometers — large by any standard but more proportionate to Japan's smaller population and economic capacity.

**No city wall.** Unlike Chang'an, which was surrounded by massive walls, Heijo-kyo was an open city. Japan, as an island nation, did not face the threat of mounted invasion that made walls essential on the Asian mainland. The city's boundaries were defined by roads and ditches rather than fortifications.

**Asymmetric development.** While the plan called for symmetric growth east and west of the central axis, the eastern half (Sakyo, or "Left Capital") developed more rapidly and densely than the western half (Ukyo, or "Right Capital"). The western side's lower, wetter terrain made construction difficult, and it never achieved the population density envisioned by the planners. An eastern extension called Gaikyo was later added to accommodate growth.

**Integration with landscape.** Japanese planners incorporated the surrounding hills and waterways into the city's design in ways that Chinese precedent did not strictly require. Kasuga Mountain to the east and the Saho River were treated as elements of the urban composition, not obstacles to be overcome.

## The Jobo Grid System

### How It Worked

Heijo-kyo was organized according to the jobo system — a standardized grid of streets that divided the city into regular blocks. The system worked as follows:

**Jo (条)** — Horizontal streets running east-west, numbered from the palace southward. First Jo, Second Jo, through Ninth Jo.

**Bo (坊)** — Vertical streets running north-south, numbered outward from the central Suzaku Oji. First Bo, Second Bo, through Fourth Bo on each side.

The intersection of jo and bo created rectangular blocks called bo, each approximately 530 meters square. Each bo was further subdivided into 16 smaller lots called tsubo, creating a nested hierarchy of urban space.

This system served as both physical infrastructure and address system. A property described as "Sakyo, Third Jo, Second Bo, Fifth Tsubo" could be located precisely within the grid — an achievement of urban administration remarkable for the eighth century.

### Street Hierarchy

Not all streets were equal. The grid established a clear hierarchy:

**Suzaku Oji** — The central north-south boulevard, approximately 70 meters wide (some scholars estimate up to 75 meters). This was a ceremonial thoroughfare, wider than many modern highways, designed for imperial processions and official business.

**Oji (major avenues)** — The main grid streets separating bo blocks, approximately 24 meters wide. These served as primary transportation routes and were lined with drainage ditches and sometimes trees.

**Koji (secondary streets)** — Narrower streets within the bo blocks, subdividing them into smaller lots. These were approximately 12 meters wide.

**Alleys (roji)** — Even smaller lanes within the tsubo subdivisions, providing access to individual dwellings.

This hierarchical system managed traffic, drainage, fire prevention, and social organization simultaneously. The width of the street you lived on reflected your social status, with aristocrats occupying lots along wider avenues and commoners in the interior alleys.

## Archaeological Evidence

### What We Know

The Heijo Palace site, designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site, has been the subject of systematic archaeological excavation since the 1950s. The Nara National Research Institute for Cultural Properties has conducted extensive studies that have confirmed many aspects of the grid system and revealed details that historical texts alone could not provide.

Excavations have uncovered the foundations of streets, drainage channels, property boundaries, and building foundations that correspond closely to the jobo grid. Particularly revealing are the mokkan (wooden tablets) found in abundance at the palace site — administrative records written on strips of wood that document property assignments, construction projects, and urban administration with remarkable specificity.

The archaeology shows that the grid was indeed implemented as planned, though with the asymmetric development noted above. The western districts show less intensive construction, and some outlying blocks appear never to have been fully developed during the city's 74 years as capital (710-784 CE).

### Walking the Ancient Grid Today

The ancient grid is largely invisible in modern Nara, buried beneath centuries of subsequent development. However, traces survive for the observant walker:

**The Heijo Palace site** itself preserves the northern anchor of the grid. The reconstructed Suzaku Gate and First Audience Hall provide a visceral sense of scale.

**Saidai-ji and Hokke-ji temples** occupy positions that correspond to their original placements within the grid, providing fixed reference points.

**Certain modern roads** follow ancient street alignments, particularly in the area between Kintetsu Nara Station and the palace site. The correspondence is approximate but detectable.

**The Nara City street pattern south of Sanjo-dori** retains faint echoes of the jo grid, with east-west streets spaced at intervals that recall the ancient block dimensions. These alignments are not continuous or precise, but they are not accidental.

The Heijo Palace Site Museum (Heijokyo Izanai-kan) provides maps and models that overlay the ancient grid onto the modern city, making these connections visible and comprehensible.

## Legacy: How Nara's Grid Shaped Japanese Cities

### Heian-kyo (Kyoto)

When the capital moved from Nara to Nagaoka-kyo in 784 CE and then to Heian-kyo (Kyoto) in 794 CE, the jobo grid system went with it. Kyoto's famous grid of numbered streets — Ichijo (First Avenue), Nijo (Second Avenue), through Kujo (Ninth Avenue) — directly continues the system first implemented at Heijo-kyo. In Kyoto, the grid survived far more intact than in Nara, partly because Kyoto remained the capital for over a millennium, continuously reinforcing the original layout.

The next time you navigate Kyoto's logical, numbered street system, you are using an urban technology first tested at Nara.

### Later Japanese Cities

The jobo grid's influence extended beyond the two ancient capitals. Castle towns (jokamachi) built during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries adopted modified grid systems that reflected the same principle of using street geometry to organize society. Even Edo (Tokyo), which developed more organically than earlier capitals, incorporated grid elements in its samurai districts.

The modern Japanese address system, which uses numbered blocks (chome) and numbered buildings within blocks rather than street addresses, is a distant descendant of the tsubo subdivision system used in Heijo-kyo and Heian-kyo.

## Visiting the Grid

The most rewarding approach to experiencing Nara's ancient urban planning is to visit the Heijo Palace site in the early morning or late afternoon, when soft light and fewer visitors allow contemplation of the space. Begin at the Suzaku Gate, face south, and imagine the broad avenue extending to the city's southern boundary. Then explore the palace grounds, where reconstructed buildings and ongoing excavations reveal the northern complex in increasing detail.

The palace site museum provides essential context, including scale models and digital reconstructions that bring the eighth-century city to life.

Guests at Kanoya ryokan, located within the area that once formed the eastern districts of Heijo-kyo, are sleeping within the ancient grid — a thought that connects the modern experience of Nara to the city's foundational act of urban creation. An evening walk through the neighborhood, with the grid's history in mind, transforms ordinary streets into palimpsests of thirteen centuries of continuous habitation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What was Heijo-kyo?

Heijo-kyo was the capital city of Japan from 710 to 784 CE, located in what is now Nara city. Built on a Chinese-inspired grid plan, it covered approximately 25 square kilometers and housed an estimated 100,000-200,000 people. Its palace site is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

### Why was Nara built as a grid city?

Nara's grid design was modeled on the Tang Dynasty Chinese capital of Chang'an. Japanese rulers adopted the grid system as part of broader Chinese-inspired reforms to centralize government. The geometric layout symbolized imperial authority and rational governance, with the palace positioned at the city's northern apex.

### Can I see the ancient grid in modern Nara?

The original grid is largely buried, but traces survive. The Heijo Palace site preserves the northern anchor, with reconstructed gates and buildings. Certain modern roads follow ancient alignments, particularly between Kintetsu Nara Station and the palace site. The Heijo Palace Site Museum provides maps overlaying the ancient grid on the modern city.

### How wide was Nara's main street?

*Suzaku Oji, the central north-south boulevard of Heijo-kyo, was approximately 70 meters wide — wider than most modern highways. This monumental scale served ceremonial purposes, accommodating imperial processions and official functions. Major grid streets (oji) were approximately 24 meters wide, secondary streets (koji) about 12 meters, with narrower alleys providing access within blocks.*

### How did Nara's grid influence Kyoto?

When the capital moved to Heian-kyo (Kyoto) in 794 CE, the jobo grid system was transplanted and refined. Kyoto's famous numbered street system — from Ichijo to Kujo — directly continues the Nara model. Because Kyoto remained the capital for over 1,000 years, the grid survived there more completely than in Nara itself.
