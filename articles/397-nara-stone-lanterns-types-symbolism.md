# Nara's Stone Lanterns: Types, Symbolism, and Where to Find Them

No city in Japan is more closely identified with stone lanterns than Nara. The approach to Kasuga Taisha shrine alone is lined with approximately two thousand stone lanterns (ishidoro), donated over centuries by worshippers from every level of society — emperors and farmers, merchants and warriors. These lanterns, standing in ranks along the forest path like a congregation of silent witnesses, create one of the most atmospheric walkways in the world.

But Nara's stone lanterns extend far beyond Kasuga Taisha. They appear in temple gardens, along park paths, at street corners in Naramachi, and in private gardens throughout the city. Each lantern tells a story through its shape, its carving, its placement, and the patina of centuries. Understanding the language of stone lanterns transforms a walk through Nara from sightseeing into reading — deciphering a text written in stone across thirteen hundred years.

## The Anatomy of a Stone Lantern

Japanese stone lanterns, regardless of type, share a basic structural vocabulary. Understanding these elements is the key to reading the lanterns you encounter.

**Kiso (Base).** The foundation stone, sometimes buried and invisible, sometimes raised and elaborately carved. The base anchors the lantern physically and symbolically — connecting it to the earth.

**Sao (Shaft).** The vertical column that elevates the firebox above ground level. Shafts range from short and squat to tall and slender, and their proportions define the lantern's visual character. Some are plain cylinders; others are carved with lotus petals, sutras, or decorative bands.

**Nakadai (Middle Platform).** A transitional element between shaft and firebox, often carved with lotus petals pointing upward — symbolising the emergence of purity from the mud of the material world.

**Hibukuro (Firebox).** The enclosed chamber that holds the light. The firebox typically has openings on two or four sides, sometimes shaped as circles, crescents, or stylised windows. The interior was traditionally fitted with a small oil lamp or candle.

**Kasa (Roof).** The canopy that protects the flame from rain and wind. Roof shapes vary widely — some are elegantly curved like temple eaves, others are simple hexagonal or octagonal discs. The roof's form is often the most distinctive feature of a particular lantern type.

**Hoju (Jewel Finial).** The topmost element, usually a lotus bud or flaming jewel (hoshu-no-tama). This represents the spiritual aspiration of the offering — the flame of enlightenment rising above the material structure.

## Types of Stone Lanterns in Nara

### Kasuga-doro (Kasuga Lantern)

The Kasuga lantern is Nara's signature type and one of the most common lantern forms in Japan. Named after Kasuga Taisha shrine, this type features a tall shaft, a hexagonal firebox with deer carved on its panels, and a gracefully curved roof. The deer motif is specific to Kasuga — referencing the shrine's sacred deer — and helps distinguish Kasuga-type lanterns from similar forms at other locations.

Kasuga lanterns line the approach paths to the shrine in extraordinary profusion. The oldest date to the Heian period (794-1185), though the majority were donated during the Edo period (1603-1868). Each lantern bears an inscription recording the donor's name, the date of donation, and often a prayer or dedication. Reading these inscriptions is like browsing a register of devotion spanning centuries.

### Yukimi-doro (Snow-Viewing Lantern)

The yukimi-doro is designed to be beautiful when capped with snow — a wide, flat-topped roof supported by multiple short legs rather than a single shaft. Snow-viewing lanterns are typically placed beside water, where their broad roofs reflect in the surface and accumulated snow creates a white cap that contrasts with the dark stone.

These lanterns appear in Nara's finest gardens, including the gardens at Isuien and Yoshikien. Their low, horizontal profile provides a visual counterweight to the vertical lines of surrounding trees and buildings.

### Oribe-doro (Oribe Lantern)

Named after the tea master Furuta Oribe, this distinctive lantern type features a shaft that is partially buried in the ground, giving the appearance of a cross or figure rising from the earth. The shaft sometimes bears a carved figure that scholars have variously identified as the Virgin Mary, a Bodhisattva, or a purely decorative motif — a debate that reflects the tantalising ambiguity of Japan's "hidden Christian" history.

Oribe lanterns are associated with tea gardens, where their intimate scale and unusual form complement the aesthetic of rustic simplicity. Several tea gardens in Nara contain examples, though they are less common than Kasuga-type lanterns.

### Rankei-doro (Legged Lantern)

Rankei lanterns stand on curved legs rather than a straight shaft, giving them a dynamic, almost animate quality. The legs are often shaped like animal limbs or scroll brackets, and the overall effect is lighter and more elegant than shaft-mounted types. Rankei lanterns are common in garden settings and along the paths of Nara Park.

### Oki-doro (Portable Lantern)

Small, shaft-less lanterns designed to sit directly on the ground or on a flat stone. Oki-doro are intimate objects, used in tea gardens and private spaces where their small scale and proximity to the ground create a soft, low light. They are easily overlooked but repay close attention — their carving is often exceptionally fine.

## Symbolism and Spiritual Meaning

Stone lanterns in Japan are more than decorative objects. They are offerings of light — representations of the Buddhist concept that wisdom (light) dispels ignorance (darkness). Donating a lantern to a shrine or temple is an act of devotion, and the act of lighting a lantern is understood as a spiritual practice.

The five structural elements of a stone lantern correspond to the five elements of Buddhist cosmology: earth (base), water (shaft), fire (firebox), wind (roof), and void (finial). The lantern thus represents the entire cosmos in miniature — a microcosm in stone that connects the human world to the transcendent.

In Shinto practice, lanterns illuminate the path for kami (deities) and guide worshippers toward the sacred. The forest path to Kasuga Taisha, lined with thousands of lanterns, is literally a passage from the mundane world to the divine — each lantern a marker on the spiritual journey.

## The Mantoro Festivals

Twice each year — in early February and mid-August — all of Kasuga Taisha's approximately three thousand lanterns (two thousand stone and one thousand bronze) are lit simultaneously during the Mantoro festival. The effect is extraordinary: the shrine precincts glow with flickering firelight, each lantern casting its own small pool of illumination into the surrounding darkness.

The February Mantoro, held during Setsubun, takes place in winter darkness. The cold, clear air sharpens the light, and the forest setting amplifies the atmosphere of sacred mystery. Arriving early and walking the lantern-lined paths before the main crowds is strongly recommended.

The August Mantoro coincides with the Obon festival, when the spirits of the dead are believed to return to the living world. The lanterns serve as beacons for returning spirits, and the atmosphere is simultaneously festive and solemn. The warm summer evening allows comfortable lingering, and the combination of lantern light, forest darkness, and ceremonial music creates one of Nara's most memorable sensory experiences.

## Where to Find Notable Stone Lanterns

Beyond Kasuga Taisha, significant stone lanterns appear throughout Nara:

**Todai-ji Precinct.** Large, ornate lanterns flank the approach to the Great South Gate and the main hall. Several date to the Kamakura period and display sculptural qualities that approach fine art.

**Kofuku-ji.** Stone lanterns around the temple grounds include some of the oldest in the city, with inscriptions dating to the medieval period.

**Nara Park Paths.** Hundreds of lanterns line the park's main paths, creating a continuous stone gallery that extends from Kofuku-ji to Kasuga Taisha.

**Naramachi.** Several private gardens and small shrines in the historic quarter contain lanterns of notable age and quality. Walking Naramachi's back streets with attention to lanterns reveals treasures hidden in plain sight.

**Isuien and Yoshikien Gardens.** Both gardens incorporate stone lanterns as essential compositional elements, demonstrating how lanterns function within the broader aesthetics of Japanese garden design.

For visitors wishing to experience stone lanterns as part of daily life rather than museum display, staying in Naramachi provides constant exposure. Kanoya ryokan's own garden includes traditional stone lanterns that are lit in the evening, and the short walk to Kasuga Taisha passes hundreds more — a daily commute through thirteen centuries of stone-carved devotion.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many stone lanterns are at Kasuga Taisha?
Kasuga Taisha has approximately two thousand stone lanterns and one thousand bronze lanterns, for a total of roughly three thousand — the largest collection at any shrine in Japan.

### When is the Mantoro festival?
Mantoro is held twice yearly: in early February (during Setsubun) and in mid-August (during Obon). Both events illuminate all of the shrine's lanterns simultaneously.

### What do the carvings on Kasuga lanterns depict?
Many Kasuga lanterns feature deer — the sacred animals of the shrine — along with wisteria (the crest of the Fujiwara clan), lotus motifs, and inscriptions recording the donor's name and date.

### Can I buy a stone lantern in Nara?
Small decorative stone lanterns are available from garden suppliers and craft shops. Full-sized garden lanterns require specialist stone carvers and are typically made to order.

### What is the oldest stone lantern in Nara?
Some lanterns in the Kasuga Taisha precinct and around Kofuku-ji date to the Kamakura period (1185-1333), making them over seven hundred years old. Precise dating of the oldest examples is debated among scholars.

*Nara has the largest collection of stone lanterns in Japan, with approximately two thousand lining the approach to Kasuga Taisha alone — best experienced during the Mantoro festival in February or August, when all three thousand lanterns at the shrine are lit simultaneously in one of Japan's most atmospheric sacred ceremonies.*
