# Hase-dera Temple: The Flower Temple of Nara Prefecture

An hour east of Nara city, where the Yamato plain gives way to forested mountains, a temple clings to a steep hillside above the Hase River. Hase-dera has been called the "Flower Temple" (花の御寺, hana no mitera) for centuries, and the name is not metaphorical. From the peony gardens of late April through the hydrangeas of June, the cherry blossoms of spring, and the blazing maples of November, the temple is designed as a place where architecture, sculpture, and the natural world are experienced as a single composition. It is one of those rare sites where beauty is not incidental but intentional — cultivated, maintained, and offered as a form of devotion.

Founded in 686 CE, Hase-dera is the eighth temple on the Saigoku Kannon Pilgrimage, the 33-temple circuit of western Japan dedicated to Kannon, the bodhisattva of compassion. The temple's principal image — an 11-headed Kannon standing over 10 metres tall — is the largest wooden sculpture in Japan and one of the most venerated Buddhist images in the country. Pilgrims have been climbing the covered stairway to reach this figure for over a thousand years.

## The Noborirou: The Covered Stairway

### 399 Steps of Arrival

The approach to Hase-dera's main hall is not a simple path but a ritualistic ascent. The Noborirou, a covered stairway of 399 stone steps, climbs the steep hillside in a series of roofed sections that channel the visitor's movement and frame the landscape in carefully composed views. Peony bushes line both sides of the lower sections, and in late April to early May, the stairway becomes a corridor of colour — deep reds, pale pinks, and pure whites cascading down the hillside.

The climb takes approximately ten minutes at a moderate pace. The covered roof protects visitors from rain and sun, and benches at intervals allow rest. The gradual ascent serves a psychological function as well as a physical one: by the time you reach the main hall, you have left the world below and entered a different register of experience.

## The Eleven-Headed Kannon

### Japan's Largest Wooden Kannon

The Hondō (Main Hall) houses Hase-dera's principal image: an Eleven-Headed Kannon (Jūichimen Kannon) carved from a single camphor tree trunk. The figure stands 10.18 metres tall, making it the largest wooden Kannon statue in Japan. The current carving dates from 1538, replacing an earlier figure destroyed by fire, though the tradition of a monumental Kannon at Hase-dera extends back to the 8th century.

The statue's eleven heads, each with a different expression representing different aspects of compassion, crown the main face in tiers. The Kannon holds a lotus flower in the left hand and a staff in the right — an unusual iconography specific to Hase-dera that combines the attributes of the traditional Kannon with those of Jizō Bosatsu, the protector of travellers and children. This dual nature reflects Hase-dera's role as both a pilgrimage temple (welcoming travellers) and a site of popular devotion (offering comfort to all).

### The Inner Chamber

During special worship periods (typically spring and autumn, with additional dates announced by the temple), visitors can enter the inner chamber and stand directly at the feet of the Kannon. The experience of looking upward at a 10-metre gilded figure from within arm's reach is qualitatively different from viewing it across the hall. The scale becomes overwhelming, the detail visible in the carving becomes astonishing, and the golden surface glows in the candlelight. If special worship is available during your visit, it is worth the additional fee (typically ¥1,000–¥1,500).

## The Seasonal Gardens

### Peonies (Late April–Early May)

Hase-dera's peony collection — approximately 7,000 plants of 150 varieties — is the centrepiece of its "flower temple" identity. The peonies bloom in terraced beds along the Noborirou stairway and around the temple grounds, creating a display that peaks in late April to early May. The combination of vermilion temple buildings, green hillside forest, and cascading peony colours makes this one of the most visually rich temple experiences in Japan.

### Cherry Blossoms (Late March–Early April)

Cherry trees throughout the temple grounds bloom in late March to early April, with the view from the Hondō's stage (butai) — looking out across blossoming trees to the forested valley — being particularly memorable.

### Hydrangeas (June)

Approximately 2,500 hydrangea plants bloom along the temple's hillside paths in June, transforming the rainy season into an unexpectedly beautiful time to visit. The combination of mist, wet stone, and blue-purple flowers creates an atmosphere that is quite different from the vivid clarity of peony season.

### Autumn Foliage (Mid-November–Early December)

The hillside's maple trees create a dramatic autumn display that rivals any site in Nara Prefecture. The view from the Hondō's stage — a wooden platform that extends out from the hall over the valley — places you within a canopy of red and gold. Evening illuminations during peak foliage season (check dates annually) add another dimension, with the lit trees reflecting in the temple's structures.

### Winter Peonies (January–February)

A smaller collection of winter-blooming peonies (kan-botan), sheltered under traditional straw covers (warabōshi) to protect them from frost, creates a delicate winter display. The image of bright peony flowers emerging from straw cones against a backdrop of bare winter branches has become one of Hase-dera's signature scenes.

## The Hondō Stage

The Hondō's butai — an extended wooden stage that projects from the main hall over the hillside, similar in concept to Kiyomizu-dera's famous stage in Kyoto — provides panoramic views across the Hase River valley. The valley, forested and quiet, stretches toward the mountains of the Kii Peninsula. In every season, this view rewards a long pause. Bring a thermos of tea if you have one.

## Literary and Cultural Connections

Hase-dera appears throughout Japanese literature. The 11th-century novel The Tale of Genji includes a scene set at the temple, and numerous Heian-period diaries and poem collections reference pilgrimages to Hase-dera. For Heian aristocrats, the journey from the capital to Hase-dera was both a devotional act and a social event — a chance to demonstrate piety while experiencing the countryside. The temple's enduring literary associations give it a cultural depth that complements its visual beauty.

## Getting There from Nara

### By Train

Take the Kintetsu Osaka Line from Kintetsu Nara Station (transfer at Yamato-Saidaiji) to Hasedera Station. The journey takes approximately 50–60 minutes and costs around ¥600. From Hasedera Station, the temple is a 15-minute walk uphill along the Hase River — a pleasant approach past traditional shops and small restaurants.

Alternatively, take the JR Manyo-Mahoroba Line from JR Nara to Sakurai, then transfer to the Kintetsu Osaka Line for two stops to Hasedera Station.

### Recommended Itinerary

- **8:30am**: Depart Nara (Kintetsu line)
- **9:30am**: Arrive Hasedera Station, walk to temple
- **9:45am–12:00pm**: Explore temple, climb Noborirou, visit Hondō and gardens
- **12:00pm–12:45pm**: Lunch near the station (sōmen noodles are a local specialty)
- **1:00pm**: Return to Nara

This makes a comfortable half-day trip. Guests at Kanoya ryokan can combine it with an afternoon in central Nara or pair it with a visit to Murō-ji (30 minutes further by bus), creating a full-day excursion through the mountain temples of eastern Nara Prefecture.

## Practical Information

- **Hours**: 8:30am–5:00pm (April–September); 9:00am–5:00pm (October–November, March); 9:00am–4:30pm (December–February)
- **Admission**: ¥500 (adults); special inner chamber worship ¥1,000–¥1,500 when available
- **Getting there**: Kintetsu Hasedera Station (50–60 minutes from Nara); 15-minute walk from station
- **Time needed**: 1.5–2 hours at the temple
- **Peak seasons**: Peonies (late April–early May), hydrangeas (June), autumn foliage (mid-November)

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What flowers bloom at Hase-dera and when?

Peonies (late April–early May, 7,000 plants), cherry blossoms (late March–early April), hydrangeas (June, 2,500 plants), autumn foliage (mid-November–early December), and winter peonies under straw covers (January–February).

### How tall is the Kannon at Hase-dera?

The Eleven-Headed Kannon stands 10.18 metres, making it the largest wooden Kannon statue in Japan. The current figure dates from 1538.

### Is Hase-dera worth the trip from Nara?

Yes, especially during peony season or autumn foliage. The combination of monumental sculpture, seasonal gardens, mountain scenery, and literary history makes it one of the most rewarding day trips in Nara Prefecture. Allow a half day including travel.

### Can I visit Hase-dera and Murō-ji in one day?

Yes. Murō-ji is approximately 30 minutes from Hase-dera by bus (from Hasedera Station area to Murō-ji). Combining both makes a full day trip requiring an early start from Nara.

Admission is ¥500. Reach it via Kintetsu line to Hasedera Station (50–60 minutes from Nara).
