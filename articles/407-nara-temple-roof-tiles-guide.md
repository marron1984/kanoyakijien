# Understanding Japanese Roof Tiles: A Visual Guide in Nara's Temples

Look up in Nara. Most visitors aim their cameras at the Great Buddha, the deer, the torii gates, the cherry blossoms. Relatively few examine the roofs — and yet the roofs of Nara's temples and shrines are among the most accomplished expressions of Japanese architectural craft in existence. The curved ridgelines, the ordered rows of grey tiles, the decorative end-caps and ridge ornaments: all of this is the product of a tile-making tradition that came to Japan from continental Asia in the 6th century and reached extraordinary refinement in Nara during the 8th.

Understanding Japanese roof tiles — what they are called, how they work, what their decorative motifs mean — adds a dimension to temple visits that most guidebooks overlook. Once you start noticing tiles, you cannot stop. Every roof becomes a text to be read, every temple a conversation between function and beauty.

## A Brief History of Kawara

Japanese roof tiles are collectively called kawara. The word itself may derive from the Sanskrit kapala (skull or vessel), suggesting the technology's ultimate origins in the Buddhist cultural sphere. Tile-making arrived in Japan in 588 CE, brought by Korean craftsmen invited to build Asuka-dera — Japan's first full-scale Buddhist temple.

Before kawara, Japanese buildings were roofed with thatch, bark, or wooden shingles. Tiles were revolutionary: fireproof, waterproof, durable, and magnificent. They also required specialised kilns, skilled labour, and significant resources, which meant that tile roofs were initially restricted to temples, shrines, and government buildings. Ordinary residences continued to use thatch or wood for centuries.

Nara was the first city in Japan where tile production reached industrial scale. The construction of Heijo-kyu (the imperial palace) and the great state temples in the 8th century required millions of tiles. Archaeologists have recovered vast quantities of Nara Period tiles from excavation sites, many still bearing the marks of their makers — stamps, fingerprints, and inscriptions that identify individual workshops and production dates.

## Types of Tiles

Japanese roof tiles are not a single form but a system of interlocking components, each with a specific function and name.

### Hiragawara (Flat Tiles)

The foundational element. Hiragawara are broad, slightly concave tiles laid in overlapping rows across the roof surface. They channel rainwater downward, functioning as miniature gutters. On a large temple roof, thousands of hiragawara create the smooth, flowing surface that gives Japanese roofs their characteristic appearance.

### Marugawara (Round Tiles)

Semi-cylindrical tiles placed over the joints between rows of hiragawara, creating the raised ridges visible on any tiled roof. The combination of flat channels and round ridges produces the distinctive undulating pattern that defines the Japanese tiled roof — a rhythm of concavity and convexity that manages water flow while creating visual texture.

### Nokimarugawara (Eave-End Round Tiles)

At the bottom edge of the roof, the marugawara terminate in circular end-caps — the nokimarugawara. These are among the most decorative elements on the roof and the most easily observed from ground level. The circular face of each end-cap typically bears a moulded design: a lotus flower (the most common Buddhist motif), a tomoe (comma-shaped swirl), a family crest, or a temple emblem.

In Nara, the lotus-design eave tiles from the Nara Period are objects of extraordinary beauty. The Nara National Museum and the Heijo Palace museum both display examples, and their clean lines and balanced proportions reflect the sophistication of 8th-century ceramic arts.

### Onigawara (Demon Tiles)

The most dramatic roof tiles are the onigawara — large, sculpted tiles placed at the ends of the main roof ridge. Despite their name ("demon tiles"), they do not always depict demons. Many feature fierce guardian faces intended to ward off evil spirits and fire. Others display floral patterns, family crests, or abstract designs.

The onigawara at Todai-ji are among the most impressive in Japan — massive sculpted faces that stare down from the ridgeline of the Great Buddha Hall, visible even from the ground far below. At Yakushi-ji and Toshodai-ji, smaller but equally fine examples can be observed with binoculars.

### Shibi (Ridge-End Ornaments)

At the ends of the main roof ridge, some temples display shibi — upward-curving ornaments that resemble fish tails or stylised flames. These are believed to have originated as fire-prevention charms (the fish association invokes water). The shibi on Todai-ji's Great Buddha Hall are gilded and visible from considerable distance.

## Decorative Motifs and Their Meanings

The designs on roof tiles are not arbitrary decoration. Each motif carries meaning:

**Lotus flower (renge)**: The most common motif on Buddhist temple tiles. The lotus represents purity — it grows from muddy water but blooms unblemished. Counting the number of petals on a lotus tile can sometimes help date the tile, as petal counts changed across historical periods.

**Tomoe (comma swirl)**: A circular design of one, two, or three comma-shaped elements. The mitsu-domoe (triple tomoe) is particularly common on shrine tiles and represents the cyclical nature of existence. It is also associated with the war deity Hachiman.

**Chrysanthemum (kiku)**: The imperial flower. Chrysanthemum tiles indicate imperial patronage or connection. Look for them on buildings associated with the imperial household.

**Arabesque (karakusa)**: Flowing vine patterns that represent continuous growth and prosperity. Common on both Buddhist and secular buildings.

**Guardian faces (onimen)**: Fierce expressions designed to repel evil. The style of the face — its degree of ferocity, its specific features — varies by period and region, making onigawara useful for dating buildings.

## Where to See the Best Examples in Nara

### Todai-ji

The Great Buddha Hall's roof is a masterwork of tile engineering. The current roof, from the 1709 reconstruction, bears approximately 112,000 tiles. The onigawara at the ridge ends and the gilded shibi are the most prominent decorative elements, but look also at the eave-end tiles along the lower edges — rows of lotus designs that are among the finest on any building in Nara.

### Toshodai-ji

The Golden Hall of Toshodai-ji has the most authentically preserved Nara Period roofline of any building in the city. The roof was restored in the early 2000s using a combination of original tiles and faithful reproductions. Standing before the building and examining the roofline with attention reveals the full tile system in clear, unobstructed view.

### Gangou-ji

The rear buildings of Gangou-ji preserve roof tiles from the Asuka Period — predating the founding of Nara itself. These tiles, originally from Asuka-dera and relocated when the temple moved to Nara in 718, are over 1,400 years old and are among the oldest roof tiles in Japan still in their functional position. The difference in colour and texture between these ancient tiles and later replacements is visible on close inspection.

### Heijo Palace Museum

The on-site museum at Heijo Palace displays excavated tiles from the palace complex, including stamped production tiles, decorated eave ends, and ridge ornaments. The collection provides an archaeological perspective on tile production — not the finished architectural effect but the raw craft of making and marking individual tiles.

### Nara National Museum

The museum's archaeological collection includes outstanding examples of Nara Period tiles, displayed as art objects. Seeing a single lotus eave tile isolated in a museum case, lit from the side, reveals a level of sculptural refinement that is invisible when the same tile is one of hundreds on a distant roofline.

## Reading Roofs: A Practical Exercise

Next time you stand before a temple in Nara, try this exercise:

1. **Identify the ridge**: The horizontal line at the peak of the roof. Note whether it is straight, curved, or stepped.
2. **Look at the ridge ends**: Are there onigawara (sculpted faces or designs)? Are there shibi (upward-curving ornaments)?
3. **Scan the roof surface**: Can you see the alternating pattern of flat tiles (hiragawara) and round tiles (marugawara)?
4. **Examine the eaves**: The bottom edge of the roof, closest to you. Can you see the circular end-caps (nokimarugawara)? What design do they bear?
5. **Compare**: Move to the next building and repeat. Are the tile styles the same? Different periods, different patrons, and different functions produce different tile assemblages.

This exercise transforms passive viewing into active reading. A temple roof is no longer a grey mass above the doors — it becomes a structured composition with identifiable elements, historical information, and decorative intent.

For visitors staying at a traditional accommodation like Kanoya ryokan, the building itself may offer an intimate introduction to tile architecture. Traditional Japanese inns and machiya often feature kawara roofing, and the scale — much smaller than a temple — makes the individual tile elements easier to examine at close range.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why are most Japanese roof tiles grey?

The grey colour results from the firing process. Japanese kawara are typically fired in a reduction atmosphere (oxygen-starved kiln), which produces the characteristic grey tone. Some tiles are glazed green or blue for decorative effect, particularly on Chinese-influenced buildings.

### How long do Japanese roof tiles last?

Well-made kawara can last several hundred years. Many tiles on Nara's temple roofs are original to their period of construction, though roofs are periodically restored and damaged tiles replaced. Gangou-ji has tiles over 1,400 years old.

### Can I buy Japanese roof tiles as souvenirs?

Decorative miniature tiles and tile-themed crafts are available at some temple gift shops and Nara craft stores. Antique original tiles occasionally appear in antique markets but are expensive and heavy.

### Are temple roofs repaired or replaced?

Major temples undergo periodic roof restoration, typically every 50 to 100 years. The process involves removing all tiles, repairing the underlying timber structure, and relaying the tiles — reusing originals where possible and replacing damaged ones with new tiles made to match.

### What is the difference between temple and shrine roofs?

Buddhist temples typically use tiled roofs with kawara. Shinto shrines traditionally use thatched or bark (hinoki cypress bark) roofs, though many modern shrines have adopted copper or tile roofing. The roof shape also differs: temple roofs tend toward broad, sweeping curves, while shrine roofs often feature steeper, straighter lines with distinctive forked finials (chigi) at the ridge ends.
