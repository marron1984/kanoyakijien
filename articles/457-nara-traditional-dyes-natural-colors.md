# Nara's Traditional Dyes: Colors from Nature

Before the arrival of synthetic aniline dyes in the late nineteenth century, every color in every fabric in Japan came from the natural world — from plants, minerals, insects, and shellfish. The process of extracting, preparing, and fixing these colors was a body of knowledge as complex and as carefully guarded as any other craft tradition, and in Nara, where textile production for the imperial court and the great temples created intense demand for specific colors, the art of dyeing reached extraordinary sophistication. Today, that tradition survives in the work of a small number of practitioners who continue to derive their palette from the same natural sources that colored the robes of Nara-period courtiers and monks.

## Color and Status in Ancient Nara

### The Kan'i Jūnikai: Twelve Ranks, Twelve Colors

In 603 CE, Prince Shōtoku established the Kan'i Jūnikai, a twelve-rank court system in which each rank was identified by a specific cap color. This system, modeled on Chinese and Korean precedents, created an immediate and powerful association between color and social status that would persist in Japanese culture for centuries. Purple occupied the highest rank. Blue, red, yellow, white, and black, in various combinations and shades, indicated descending ranks.

The practical consequence was that the production of specific colors became a matter of state importance. The ability to produce a true, deep purple from the roots of the murasaki plant, or a vivid red from safflower petals, was not merely a craft skill — it was a political resource. Dyers who could reliably produce the court's mandated colors enjoyed patronage and protection, and their techniques were closely held.

Nara, as the seat of the imperial court from 710 to 784, was the center of this color-conscious culture. The dyers who served the court worked with the natural materials available in the Yamato region, developing techniques specifically adapted to local plants and water sources.

### Temple Colors

The Buddhist temples that dominated Nara's landscape also required specific dye colors. Monastic robes followed color codes that indicated rank, school, and function within the temple hierarchy. The saffron and ochre tones associated with Buddhist monasticism worldwide had their Japanese expressions in dyes derived from kihada bark (yellowish), gardenia fruit (warm yellow), and various combinations that produced the muted earth tones considered appropriate for renunciant life.

Temple banners, altar cloths, and ceremonial textiles required a wider palette, including the expensive purples and reds restricted at court. The temples' textile needs sustained a community of dyers whose skills were passed through family lineages and master-apprentice relationships.

## The Natural Dye Palette of Nara

### Ai (Indigo)

Indigo is perhaps the most important dye in the Japanese textile tradition, and its use in Nara predates the capital's founding. Derived from the leaves of the tade-ai plant (Persicaria tinctoria), Japanese indigo production involves fermenting the leaves into a compost-like substance called sukumo, which is then dissolved in an alkaline vat. The chemistry is complex — the dye vat is essentially a living fermentation culture that must be maintained through careful temperature control and pH management.

The range of blues achievable from a single indigo vat is remarkable, from the palest ice blue (achieved with a single dip) to a near-black that the Japanese call nou-kon. Nara's dyers historically produced the full spectrum, with deeper blues reserved for higher-status garments. The practice of repeated dipping — some deep blues require twenty or more immersions, with drying between each — made dark indigo textiles expensive and prestigious.

### Murasaki (Purple from Gromwell Root)

The most prestigious color in the Japanese dyeing tradition, murasaki purple was extracted from the roots of the murasaki plant (Lithospermum erythrorhizon). The process was extraordinarily labor-intensive. The roots yield pigment reluctantly, requiring repeated extraction cycles, and the resulting dye requires a mordant — typically alum or an ash-based alkaline solution — to bind permanently to fabric. The difficulty of production and the scarcity of wild murasaki plants made this the most expensive dye in ancient Japan, which is precisely why it was reserved for the highest court ranks.

In the Nara period, cultivated murasaki fields existed in the Yamato region, and their management was a matter of official concern. The color produced is not the brilliant purple of modern synthetic dyes but a softer, more complex hue with reddish or bluish undertones depending on the mordant and the number of dyeing cycles.

### Beni (Safflower Red)

Safflower (Carthamus tinctorius), called benibana in Japanese, produces a vivid red pigment from its petals. The extraction process is counterintuitive: the petals initially yield a yellow pigment, which must be washed away before the red pigment can be extracted using an alkaline solution. The resulting beni dye produces pinks and reds of extraordinary vibrancy, and in the Nara and Heian periods, safflower red was second only to purple in prestige and cost.

The Yamato Plain's warm climate suited safflower cultivation, and fields of the orange-red flowers were a characteristic sight in the ancient landscape. The dried petals were also used as a cosmetic pigment — the red lip color worn by women of the court was derived from the same beni.

### Kakishibu (Persimmon Tannin)

A distinctly Japanese contribution to the dyeing palette, kakishibu is extracted from unripe persimmon fruit. The juice is pressed and fermented for several years, developing a rich, warm brown that deepens with exposure to sunlight. Kakishibu is not merely a dye — it is also a preservative and waterproofing agent. Fabrics treated with persimmon tannin resist water, insects, and rot, making them practical for everyday use.

In Nara, kakishibu was used extensively for work clothing, paper treatment, and the finishing of wooden surfaces. The color it produces — a warm, reddish brown that varies with concentration and sun exposure — has become associated with traditional Japanese aesthetic sensibility, and modern artisans continue to use it both for its color and its protective properties.

### Kariyasu and Kihada (Yellows)

Yellow dyes were derived from several sources. Kariyasu grass (Miscanthus tinctorius) produced a clear, bright yellow used extensively in court textiles. Kihada bark (Phellodendron amurense), the same Amur cork tree whose bark produces the bitter medicine daranisuke, yielded a warm, slightly greenish yellow. Gardenia fruit (kuchinashi) produced a deep golden yellow that was widely used for Buddhist ceremonial textiles.

These yellows also served as base colors in combination dyeing. Yellow over-dyed with indigo produced green. Yellow combined with safflower red produced orange. The Japanese dyer's palette, though derived from a relatively limited number of source materials, expanded through combination into a range of colors that could express subtle distinctions of rank, season, and occasion.

## The Dyeing Process

### Mordanting

Most natural dyes require a mordant — a metallic salt or other substance that bonds the dye molecule to the fiber. Japanese traditional dyeing uses several mordants, with results that differ notably in final color. Alum produces bright, clear tones. Iron darkens and mutes the color, pushing reds toward brown and yellows toward olive. Wood ash creates an alkaline environment that shifts colors in yet another direction. The mordant is as important as the dye itself in determining the final color, and mastering mordant chemistry was a key component of the dyer's expertise.

### Water

The quality and mineral content of the local water supply significantly affects dyeing results. Nara's water, sourced from the surrounding mountains, has characteristics that influenced the development of local dyeing techniques. Dyers historically chose their workshop locations partly based on water quality, and the specific colors achievable in Nara were partly a function of what the local water made possible.

### Seasonality

Natural dyeing is seasonal work. Plants are harvested at specific times — safflower in midsummer, indigo leaves in late summer, murasaki roots in autumn. The fermentation processes that transform raw materials into usable dyes require specific temperature ranges. Even the dyeing itself is affected by season: indigo vats behave differently in summer heat than in winter cold. A natural dyer's year is structured around these rhythms in a way that connects the craft intimately to the agricultural calendar.

## Experiencing Natural Dyes in Nara Today

### Workshops and Studios

A small number of artisans in the Nara area continue to practice kusaki-zome (plant dyeing) using traditional methods. Some offer workshops where visitors can experience basic dyeing techniques — typically indigo dipping or persimmon tannin application — and produce a small textile to take home. These workshops range from one-hour introductory sessions to full-day immersions. The Nara tourist information center can provide current listings, and advance reservation is generally necessary.

### Museum Collections

The Nara National Museum and the Shōsō-in repository (viewable during the annual autumn exhibition) contain textile fragments that preserve Nara-period dye colors in remarkable condition. Seeing these 1,200-year-old fabrics — their colors faded but still legible — provides a direct connection to the dyers who produced them.

### Shopping for Naturally Dyed Textiles

Shops in Naramachi and around Nara Park sell textiles, scarves, and small items dyed with natural pigments. Prices are higher than for synthetic-dyed equivalents, reflecting the labor involved, but the depth and complexity of natural dye colors — their subtle variations, their responsiveness to light — justify the premium for those who value the distinction.

Guests at Kanoya ryokan may notice the naturally dyed fabrics used in the inn's interior — noren curtains, cushion covers, and seasonal decorations whose muted, warm tones reflect the same palette that colored Nara's ancient textiles. These details are easy to overlook, but they connect the present-day experience to a tradition that stretches back to the founding of the city.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What natural dyes were used in ancient Nara?
The primary natural dyes used in Nara included indigo (ai) from tade-ai leaves, purple from murasaki gromwell root, red from safflower (benibana) petals, yellow from kariyasu grass and kihada bark, and brown from persimmon tannin (kakishibu).

### Why was purple the most prestigious color in ancient Japan?
Purple was the most prestigious because it was the most difficult and expensive to produce. The murasaki plant root yields pigment reluctantly, requiring extensive labor for small quantities, and the resulting dye was reserved for the highest ranks in the court system established by Prince Shōtoku in 603 CE.

### Can I try natural dyeing in Nara?
*Yes, several artisans in the Nara area offer kusaki-zome (plant dyeing) workshops where visitors can experience techniques such as indigo dipping or persimmon tannin application and create a small textile to take home. Sessions range from one hour to a full day, and advance reservation is generally required — check with the Nara tourist information center for current listings.*

### What is kakishibu?
Kakishibu is a dye and preservative made from the fermented juice of unripe persimmons. It produces a warm reddish-brown color that deepens with sun exposure and also waterproofs and protects fabric and paper from insects and decay.

### Are naturally dyed textiles available to buy in Nara?
Yes. Shops in Naramachi and around Nara Park sell scarves, fabrics, and accessories dyed with natural pigments including indigo, persimmon, and botanical extracts. Prices reflect the labor-intensive production process.
