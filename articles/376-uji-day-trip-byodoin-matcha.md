# Uji Day Trip from Nara: Byodo-in, Matcha, and the Tale of Genji

Uji sits between Nara and Kyoto like a jewel lodged between two crowns. This small city on the banks of the Uji River has a cultural weight far beyond its modest size: it is home to Byodo-in, whose Phoenix Hall graces the ten-yen coin and stands as one of the most beautiful buildings in Japan; it is the centre of Japan's finest tea-growing tradition, producing matcha that is prized worldwide; and it is the setting for the final chapters of The Tale of Genji, the eleventh-century novel widely considered the first masterpiece of world literature. A day trip from Nara to Uji — barely thirty minutes by train — offers an extraordinary concentration of beauty, history, and flavour.

## Getting to Uji from Nara

Uji is remarkably accessible from Nara. The JR Nara Line runs directly from JR Nara Station to JR Uji Station, with the journey taking approximately thirty minutes and costing around 500 yen. Trains run frequently throughout the day. Alternatively, the Kintetsu Line connects to Keihan Uji Station via a transfer, though the JR route is simpler. From either station, the main sights are within easy walking distance.

## Byodo-in: The Phoenix Hall

No building in Japan has been more reproduced, more photographed, or more symbolically laden than the Phoenix Hall (Hoo-do) of Byodo-in. Built in 1053 by Fujiwara no Yorimichi, the son of the powerful regent Fujiwara no Michinaga, the hall was designed to evoke the Western Paradise of Amida Buddha — a vision of the afterlife that dominated late Heian-period religious imagination. Its reflection in the surrounding pond, its outstretched wing corridors, and the phoenix ornaments on its roof create a symmetry so perfect that it has become an emblem of Japanese aesthetics.

The hall's interior houses a gilded wooden statue of Amida Buddha by the master sculptor Jocho, seated on a lotus throne and surrounded by a halo of smaller bodhisattva figures suspended from the walls as though floating on clouds. The ceiling and walls were originally covered in paintings of the Western Paradise, many of which survive in fragmentary form. The effect, even a thousand years after its creation, is of a space designed to transport the viewer from the earthly realm to the divine.

### The Byodo-in Museum Hoshokan

Adjacent to the Phoenix Hall, this modern museum displays temple treasures in climate-controlled galleries. The collection includes original phoenix roof ornaments, the cloud-riding bodhisattva figures removed from the hall for preservation, temple bells, and fragments of the original paintings. The museum's design — subdued lighting, minimal commentary, generous space around each object — allows the works to speak for themselves. The fifty-two bodhisattva figures, each playing a different musical instrument or dancing in a unique pose, are particularly extraordinary.

### Visiting Tips

Byodo-in opens at 8:30, and arriving early is strongly recommended. Entry to the Phoenix Hall interior is by timed ticket (purchased separately within the grounds), with groups limited to approximately fifty visitors. During peak seasons, interior tickets can sell out by mid-morning. The exterior and gardens, however, can be enjoyed at leisure throughout the day.

## Uji Tea Culture

Uji's association with tea dates to the thirteenth century, when seeds brought from China were planted in the region's misty hillsides. The combination of climate, soil, and the Uji River's humidity created ideal growing conditions, and Uji tea soon became the standard against which all Japanese green tea was measured. Today, Uji remains synonymous with the highest quality matcha, gyokuro, and sencha.

### Tea Experiences

The streets around Byodo-in and along the Uji River are lined with tea shops, many of which have been in continuous operation for generations. Several offer seated tea tasting or informal matcha preparation experiences:

- **Nakamura Tokichi Honten**: One of Uji's most celebrated tea merchants, operating since 1854. The tea house serves matcha and hojicha paired with traditional sweets, and the atmosphere — overlooking the river from a historic wooden building — is quintessentially Uji.
- **Tsuen Tea**: Claims to be the oldest tea shop in Japan, with a history stretching back to 1160. Its location near the Uji Bridge makes it a natural starting or finishing point.
- **Taihoan Tea House**: A municipal tea ceremony room on an island in the Uji River, offering simplified tea ceremony experiences with English-language guidance. Reservations are not required, and the setting is beautiful.

Beyond formal tea experiences, Uji offers matcha in every conceivable form: matcha soft-serve ice cream, matcha soba noodles, matcha mochi, matcha beer. The quality of even casual matcha products in Uji is noticeably superior to what you will find elsewhere in Japan — a testament to the seriousness with which the town treats its signature product.

### Tea Farms and Production

For visitors with deeper interest, some tea farms in the surrounding hills offer tours during the growing season (April through June). The process of shading tea plants to produce tencha (the leaves ground into matcha) is particularly fascinating — elaborate canopy structures cover the fields, forcing the plants to produce more chlorophyll and creating the vivid green colour and rich umami flavour that define premium matcha.

## The Tale of Genji

The final ten chapters of Murasaki Shikibu's The Tale of Genji, known collectively as the "Uji Chapters," are set along the Uji River and in the surrounding hills. Written around the year 1000, The Tale of Genji is a sprawling narrative of court life, romantic entanglement, and the Buddhist understanding of impermanence. The Uji Chapters, which follow a new generation of characters after the death of the protagonist Prince Genji, are considered among the most psychologically complex and emotionally devastating passages in pre-modern literature.

### The Tale of Genji Museum

Located a short walk from Byodo-in, this museum brings Murasaki Shikibu's world to life through reconstructed Heian-period room settings, animated film presentations, and displays of the clothing, furnishings, and artistic objects described in the novel. The museum is particularly effective at evoking the aesthetic sensibility of the Heian court — the layered silk robes, the incense-scented rooms, the poetry exchanged between lovers on coloured paper. Even visitors unfamiliar with the novel will find the museum a vivid introduction to Japan's classical culture.

Along the river, the Genji Monogatari Millennial Monument and a statue of Murasaki Shikibu mark the town's literary connections.

## Walking the Uji River

The Uji River itself is one of the town's great pleasures. The river flows broad and fast through the town centre, crossed by several bridges including the historic Uji Bridge — one of the oldest bridge sites in Japan, mentioned in texts dating to the seventh century. Walking along the riverbanks connects the major sites naturally: Byodo-in on the west bank, the Tale of Genji Museum nearby, and the Ujigami Shrine on the east bank.

### Ujigami Shrine

This unassuming Shinto shrine, tucked into a hillside across the river from Byodo-in, is the oldest surviving shrine building in Japan. Its honden (main hall) dates to approximately 1060, making it contemporaneous with the Phoenix Hall. Despite its UNESCO World Heritage designation, Ujigami Shrine receives a fraction of Byodo-in's visitors, and its quiet, mossy grounds offer a contemplative counterpoint to the more celebrated site across the river.

## Practical Information

**Time needed**: Four to five hours covers the major sites comfortably, including Byodo-in (ninety minutes), tea tasting (thirty to sixty minutes), the Tale of Genji Museum (forty-five minutes), and riverside walking.

**Best time to visit**: Spring brings cherry blossoms along the river. Autumn foliage frames Byodo-in beautifully. The tea harvest season (April through June) is ideal for those interested in tea culture. Summer is hot; early starts are advisable.

**Lunch**: Uji's restaurants serve excellent tea-infused cuisine. Matcha soba, cha-kaiseki (tea-themed multi-course meals), and local river fish are all available. The streets near Byodo-in have numerous options.

**Combine with**: Uji sits on the train line between Nara and Kyoto, making it a natural stopover. Visitors can combine a morning in Uji with an afternoon in Fushimi (sake district) or southern Kyoto.

## Returning to Nara

The thirty-minute train ride back to Nara passes through the gentle countryside of southern Kyoto Prefecture, and the return to the city feels like coming home to a familiar companion after a brief, exhilarating encounter. Guests at Kanoya ryokan arriving back in the late afternoon have time to decompress in the ryokan's calm atmosphere before dinner — perhaps still tasting the lingering vegetal sweetness of Uji's matcha, a flavour that will forever after be inseparable from the memory of the Phoenix Hall reflected in still water.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I get from Nara to Uji?

Take the JR Nara Line from JR Nara Station directly to JR Uji Station. The journey takes approximately thirty minutes and costs around 500 yen. Trains run frequently throughout the day.

### Is Uji worth a day trip from Nara?

Absolutely. Uji offers the iconic Byodo-in Phoenix Hall, Japan's finest matcha tea culture, the Tale of Genji Museum, and the oldest surviving shrine building in Japan — all within a compact, walkable area just thirty minutes from Nara.

### What is the best matcha experience in Uji?

Nakamura Tokichi Honten and Tsuen Tea are two of the most respected tea houses. For a more formal experience, Taihoan Tea House on the river island offers simplified tea ceremony sessions.

### Can I see the interior of Byodo-in's Phoenix Hall?

Yes, but interior visits require a separate timed ticket purchased within the grounds. Groups are limited, and tickets can sell out during peak seasons, so arriving early is strongly recommended.
