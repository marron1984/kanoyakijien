# The Monks Who Built Nara: Buddhist Clergy as Architects of Culture

The great temples of Nara were not built by emperors alone. Behind every imperial decree, every ambitious construction project, and every artistic achievement stood Buddhist monks — men who served as architects, engineers, educators, diplomats, social workers, and political advisers. The monks of the Nara period were not cloistered contemplatives removed from the world. They were the builders of a civilisation.

Understanding their role transforms a visit to Nara. The temples you see were not simply ordered into existence by imperial fiat. They were conceived, designed, constructed, and maintained by communities of monks whose combination of spiritual dedication, practical skill, and institutional ambition created the cultural infrastructure that survives to this day.

## Gyōki: The People's Monk

No monk of the Nara period had a more remarkable career than Gyōki (668–749 CE). Born to a family of Korean immigrant descent, Gyōki was trained in the Hossō school of Buddhism at Yakushi-ji but chose to work outside the institutional temple system, travelling the countryside to preach, teach, and — most distinctively — build.

### The Engineer-Monk

Gyōki's practical achievements were extraordinary:

- **Bridges**: He supervised the construction of bridges across rivers that had previously been crossable only by boat or ford
- **Irrigation systems**: He organised the building of reservoirs and canals that brought water to agricultural land
- **Roads**: He improved roads connecting towns and provinces
- **Harbours**: He developed port facilities along the coast
- **Charitable institutions**: He established hospices (fuseya) for travellers and the sick along major routes

These were not small projects. Gyōki mobilised large numbers of followers — thousands, according to contemporary records — who volunteered their labour in exchange for his Buddhist teachings. He was, in effect, running a combination of a religious movement and a public works programme.

### Conflict with the State

Gyōki's popularity alarmed the government. In 717 CE, the court issued an edict criticising him for preaching to the masses without authorisation and for drawing people away from productive labour. The official Buddhist establishment — the state-regulated temples of Nara — viewed Gyōki as an uncontrolled competitor who was building his authority outside their institutional framework.

The tension between Gyōki's popular Buddhism and the state's institutional Buddhism illustrates a fundamental conflict of the Nara period: who controlled religion? The state wanted Buddhism to serve its purposes — legitimising imperial authority, providing spiritual protection, maintaining social order. Gyōki offered a different model, in which Buddhism served the people directly through practical good works.

### Reconciliation and the Great Buddha

The conflict resolved itself dramatically. When Emperor Shōmu undertook the construction of the Great Buddha at Tōdai-ji in the 740s, he needed popular support — both voluntary labour and donations — on a scale the state bureaucracy could not provide. He turned to Gyōki.

In 745 CE, Gyōki was appointed Daisōjō (Great Archbishop) — the highest rank in the Buddhist clergy — a remarkable reversal for a monk who had been officially censured just decades earlier. Gyōki used his vast network of followers to mobilise support for the Great Buddha project, persuading thousands of ordinary people to contribute labour and resources.

Gyōki died in 749 CE, just before the Great Buddha's completion. His legacy is the model he established: the monk as public servant, using spiritual authority to organise practical action for the common good. A statue of Gyōki stands in front of Kintetsu Nara Station today — the city's most visible monument to a monk whose work shaped the physical landscape of an entire nation.

## Ganjin: The Monk Who Crossed the Sea

If Gyōki represents the monk as social activist, Ganjin (Chinese: Jianzhen, 688–763 CE) represents the monk as bearer of knowledge across cultural boundaries. His story — six attempts over twelve years to reach Japan from China — is one of the great narratives of dedication in Buddhist history.

### The Mission

The Japanese court invited Ganjin to Japan to establish proper ordination procedures for Buddhist monks. Without legitimate ordination by a qualified master, the status of Japanese monks was doctrinally questionable. Ganjin, a renowned vinaya (monastic discipline) master in Yangzhou, agreed to make the journey.

His first five attempts failed. Storms destroyed his ships. Chinese authorities blocked his departure. His companions died. He contracted an eye disease that left him blind. Any reasonable person would have given up. Ganjin did not.

On his sixth attempt in 753 CE, he reached Japan — blind, aged, and accompanied by a handful of surviving companions. He was received with extraordinary honour. Emperor Shōmu himself met him, and the court allocated resources for him to establish a proper ordination platform.

### Tōshōdai-ji

In 759 CE, Ganjin founded Tōshōdai-ji, which became the centre of the Ritsu (Vinaya) school of Buddhism in Japan. The temple's main hall (kondō) is one of the finest surviving examples of Nara-period architecture, and the dry-lacquer portrait of Ganjin kept in the Founder's Hall is one of the most revered works of art in Japan.

Ganjin's contribution was not only religious but cultural. He brought with him knowledge of Chinese medicine, architecture, and calligraphy. He introduced new varieties of sugar, and the techniques for producing certain medicines. His arrival enriched Japanese culture far beyond the strictly religious sphere.

The portrait statue, displayed each year for a few days around the anniversary of Ganjin's death in June, shows an elderly man with closed eyes, his face marked by suffering but composed in deep tranquility. It is one of the most human works of art from the ancient world — not an idealised deity but a specific person, rendered with compassion and accuracy.

## Rōben: The Founder of Tōdai-ji

While Shōmu provided the imperial authority and Gyōki the popular support, the monk Rōben (689–773 CE) provided the religious and administrative framework for Tōdai-ji.

Rōben was trained in the Kegon school of Buddhism, which taught the interpenetration of all phenomena — a philosophy that suited Shōmu's vision of a cosmic Buddha presiding over a unified nation. Rōben was appointed the first bettō (head administrator) of Tōdai-ji and oversaw the temple's development from a modest institution into the greatest Buddhist complex in Japan.

His role was part spiritual leader, part construction manager, part political negotiator. Building Tōdai-ji required coordinating with the court, managing enormous budgets, supervising thousands of workers, and maintaining the religious legitimacy that justified the project. Rōben handled all of these responsibilities for decades.

A small hall within the Tōdai-ji complex — the Rōben-dō — preserves his memory. It contains a wooden portrait statue and is open to the public only on December 16th, the anniversary of his death.

## Dōkyō: The Cautionary Tale

Not all of Nara's influential monks are remembered admiringly. Dōkyō (died 772 CE) rose from obscure origins to become the most powerful man in Japan during the second reign of Empress Kōken/Shōtoku, and his story serves as the cautionary counterpart to the positive narratives of Gyōki and Ganjin.

Dōkyō was a Hossō monk who gained the empress's favour through (according to various accounts) healing prayers, personal charisma, or romantic attachment. She elevated him to unprecedented positions — Daijin Zenji (Great Minister Monk), then Hōō (Dharma King), a title that placed him effectively equal to the emperor.

When an oracle from Usa Hachiman-gū shrine appeared to suggest that Dōkyō should become emperor, the court official Wake no Kiyomaro was sent to verify the message. Kiyomaro reported that the oracle was false, and Dōkyō's attempt at the throne was thwarted. After the empress's death in 770 CE, Dōkyō was exiled, and his rise and fall became the primary justification for moving the capital away from Nara's monastery-heavy environment.

The Dōkyō affair illustrates the fundamental tension in Nara's religio-political system: the same institutional power that allowed monks like Gyōki and Rōben to build great things also allowed monks like Dōkyō to threaten the political order. The move to Kyoto was, in large part, an attempt to resolve this tension.

## The Scholarly Monks

Beyond the famous names, hundreds of monks contributed to Nara's cultural achievement through scholarship, translation, and education:

**Sutra copying**: Enormous teams of monks — sometimes hundreds at a time — produced the manuscript copies of Buddhist texts that filled temple libraries. The copying bureaus (shakyō-jo) operated like scriptoria, with specialised roles for copyists, proofreaders, and binding craftsmen.

**The Six Schools**: The six Buddhist schools of Nara — Sanron, Jōjitsu, Hossō, Kusha, Kegon, and Ritsu — maintained vigorous scholarly traditions, debating doctrinal questions and producing commentaries that contributed to the intellectual life of the capital.

**Education**: Temple schools trained not only monks but also the sons of aristocrats in literacy, mathematics, and Chinese classics. The temples were, in effect, the universities of Nara-period Japan.

## Visiting the Monks' Legacy

To trace the story of Nara's monks through the city:

**Kintetsu Nara Station**: Begin at the statue of Gyōki, which faces the old capital from its position outside the modern station. This is where the people's monk greets the people's city.

**Tōdai-ji**: Rōben's creation, where Gyōki's mobilisation made the Great Buddha possible. Visit the Hokke-dō for the finest surviving Nara-period sculptures. The Rōben-dō is open only on December 16th.

**Tōshōdai-ji**: Ganjin's temple, with its exquisite kondō and the portrait statue displayed annually in June. The temple's gardens are among the most beautiful in Nara.

**Yakushi-ji**: Where Gyōki trained before taking his ministry to the people. The bronze Yakushi Trinity remains one of the supreme achievements of the monks' artistic commissions.

An overnight stay makes it possible to visit both Tōdai-ji (in the eastern part of the city) and Tōshōdai-ji (in the western suburbs) without rushing. Kanoya ryokan offers a restful base between these sites, allowing visitors to absorb the legacy of the monks who built Nara at a pace that honours their dedication.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who was Gyōki?

Gyōki (668–749 CE) was a Buddhist monk who built bridges, roads, and irrigation systems across Japan. Initially censured by the government, he was later appointed Great Archbishop and helped mobilise popular support for the Great Buddha at Tōdai-ji.

### Why did Ganjin come to Japan?

Ganjin was invited to establish proper Buddhist ordination procedures. He made six attempts over twelve years to cross from China, arriving in 753 CE blind from illness. He founded Tōshōdai-ji in Nara.

### What was the Dōkyō affair?

The monk Dōkyō gained enormous political power through his relationship with Empress Kōken/Shōtoku and allegedly attempted to claim the throne. His rise and fall was a major reason for the capital's eventual move from Nara to Kyoto.

### Can I see the Ganjin portrait statue?

Yes, but only during a brief annual display period at Tōshōdai-ji, typically around June 5–7 each year. Check the temple's schedule for exact dates.
