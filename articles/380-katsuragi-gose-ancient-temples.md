# Katsuragi and Gose: Ancient Temples Along the Yamato Foothills

The western edge of the Nara basin rises abruptly into the Katsuragi and Kongo mountain ranges, a wall of forested peaks that has served since prehistory as the boundary between Yamato (Nara) and Kawachi (Osaka). Along the foothills where the mountains meet the plain, a string of ancient temples, mountain shrines, and historic towns traces a route through some of the oldest layers of Japanese civilisation. The cities of Katsuragi and Gose, nestled against these slopes, offer a day of exploration that is rich in history, light on crowds, and deeply connected to the mythological and spiritual origins of Japan.

This is not a well-known excursion, even among experienced Japan travellers. The temples here lack the fame of Todai-ji or Horyu-ji, and the towns have none of Kyoto's international profile. But for visitors who have already absorbed Nara's major sights and want to go deeper into the region's history, the Yamato foothills deliver an experience of quiet intensity that is difficult to find elsewhere.

## The Katsuragi Clan and Ancient Yamato

The Katsuragi mountains are named after the Katsuragi clan (Katsuragi-shi), one of the most powerful families in Japan during the fifth century — the era before the formal establishment of the imperial court system. The Katsuragi were closely intermarried with the imperial family and wielded enormous political influence during the Kofun period, when the great burial mounds that dot the Nara basin were being constructed.

According to mythology, the mountains were also the domain of En no Gyoja, the semi-legendary founder of Shugendo mountain asceticism. En no Gyoja is said to have been born at the foot of Mount Katsuragi and to have practised his first austerities on its slopes before establishing the pilgrimage routes of the Omine range. The Katsuragi mountains thus represent the birthplace of Shugendo, one of Japan's most distinctive spiritual traditions.

This interweaving of political power, mythology, and mountain religion gives the Katsuragi foothills a historical density that rewards slow exploration. Every temple and shrine along these slopes connects to networks of meaning that extend deep into the Japanese past.

## Taima-dera

The most important temple in the area, Taima-dera lies at the western edge of the Nara basin in Katsuragi city, backed by the mountains and surrounded by gardens that are spectacular in spring and autumn. Founded in 612 according to temple tradition — making it one of the oldest temples in Japan — Taima-dera is best known for its association with the Taima Mandala, a large tapestry depicting the Western Paradise of Amida Buddha that is one of the most celebrated religious artworks in Japanese history.

According to legend, the mandala was woven in a single night by a celestial being for Princess Chujo, a Nara-period noblewoman who had taken vows at the temple. The original mandala, now extremely deteriorated, is preserved in the temple's main hall, while high-quality reproductions allow visitors to appreciate the extraordinary detail of the composition — a panoramic vision of the Pure Land populated by bodhisattvas, celestial musicians, and the Buddha himself, rendered in silk and lotus-fibre thread.

The temple complex is extensive, with two pagodas (east and west, both dating to the Nara period and designated National Treasures), a main hall housing the mandala, several sub-temples, and gardens planted with peonies that bloom spectacularly in late April and early May. The Nakano-bo sub-temple maintains a garden attributed to the Muromachi-period master Juko, and the grounds include one of the finest collections of stone Buddhist carvings in the region.

Taima-dera also hosts the annual Nerikuyo ceremony each May, a dramatic processional in which costumed participants re-enact the arrival of Amida Buddha and his retinue to welcome the dying Princess Chujo to paradise. The ceremony, performed on a specially constructed bridge extending from the main hall, is one of the most visually striking Buddhist rituals in Japan.

## Katsuragi Hitokotonushi Shrine

Perched on the slopes of Mount Katsuragi above Gose, this ancient shrine is dedicated to Hitokotonushi, a deity whose name means "Lord of One Word" — a god who answers prayers with a single definitive word. The shrine's origins predate formal Shinto and are rooted in the indigenous nature worship of the Yamato region. The approach through forest, the moss-covered stone steps, and the small, unadorned shrine buildings create an atmosphere of primal sanctity.

The shrine is also associated with En no Gyoja, who according to legend commanded the gods of the Katsuragi mountains to build a stone bridge between the peaks. Hitokotonushi, displeased with his appearance, refused to work during daylight hours, earning En no Gyoja's displeasure and, in some versions of the story, leading to the ascetic's eventual exile. This mythological tension between the mountain ascetic and the local deity speaks to the complex relationship between Shugendo and the indigenous religious traditions it encountered.

## Gose: A Preserved Merchant Town

The city of Gose, at the foot of the Katsuragi mountains, preserves one of the most intact traditional townscapes in Nara Prefecture. The Shinmachi district, a street of Edo-period merchant houses, has been designated a Preservation District for Groups of Traditional Buildings — one of Japan's official designations for historically significant streetscapes.

The houses in Shinmachi display the characteristic features of Yamato merchant architecture: latticed front windows, earthen-walled storehouses (kura), deep interior courtyards, and carefully maintained wooden facades. Several houses are open to visitors, offering a glimpse of the domestic and commercial life of Edo-period merchants who traded in the agricultural products of the fertile Nara basin.

Walking through Shinmachi is a quiet pleasure. The street is not commercialised; there are no souvenir shops or theme cafes, only the weathered beauty of buildings that have been continuously maintained for centuries. The contrast with more famous preserved districts — Takayama's Sanmachi or Kanazawa's Higashi Chaya — is one of scale and atmosphere: Gose is smaller, less visited, and more intimate.

## Kongo-san: The Highest Peak in Osaka-Nara

Mount Kongo (1,125 metres), the highest peak on the border between Nara and Osaka prefectures, rises directly west of Gose. A well-established hiking trail ascends from the Nara side, offering a moderately strenuous climb of about two hours to the summit. The mountain has been a site of religious practice since the Shugendo tradition's earliest days, and a small shrine and temple occupy the summit area.

The summit views on clear days are exceptional, spanning from the Nara basin and the distant mountains of the Kii Peninsula to the east, to the Osaka plain and, on the clearest days, the coast of Awaji Island to the west. In winter, the summit occasionally receives enough snow for a modest dusting, and the frosted trees (juhyo) create a magical landscape that draws photographers and hikers from across the Kansai region.

A cable car (ropeway) on the Osaka side provides an alternative ascent for those who prefer not to hike, though the Nara-side trail is more scenic and historically significant.

## Segan-ji and the Mountain Temples

Several smaller temples occupy the slopes between the basin floor and the mountain ridgeline. Segan-ji, founded in the Asuka period, contains stone Buddhist carvings and a meditation hall set among ancient cryptomeria trees. The temple's isolation and its setting — deep forest, the sound of water, filtered light — embody the ideal of mountain Buddhism as a practice inseparable from the natural world.

Other hillside temples, including Hase-dera's satellite temples and small Shugendo training halls, dot the foothills and can be discovered on foot by following the old pilgrimage paths that crisscross the lower slopes. These paths are not well signposted in English, and a degree of improvisational navigation is part of the experience.

## Practical Information

### Getting There

**Taima-dera**: From Kintetsu Nara Station, take the Kintetsu Line to Yamato-Saidaiji, transfer to the Kintetsu Minami-Osaka Line, and ride to Taimadera Station. The journey takes approximately fifty minutes. The temple is a fifteen-minute walk from the station.

**Gose**: Gose Station is on the JR Wakayama Line, reachable from JR Nara Station in approximately fifty minutes with a transfer at Takada. Alternatively, the Kintetsu Line serves nearby stations.

**By car**: The Katsuragi-Gose area is approximately forty-five minutes to one hour from central Nara by car via the Nara Prefecture Route 30 or the Keinawa Expressway.

### Time Needed

A full day allows comfortable exploration of Taima-dera, Gose's Shinmachi district, and one or two additional sites (Katsuragi Hitokotonushi Shrine or a mountain hike). A focused half-day can cover Taima-dera and Gose's historic streets.

### Best Time to Visit

Late April through early May brings peony blossoms to Taima-dera's gardens. Autumn foliage colours the mountain slopes from mid-November. The Nerikuyo ceremony at Taima-dera is held on 14 May. Winter offers clear mountain views and solitude.

### Dining

Gose has several small restaurants and cafes, but options are limited compared to Nara city. A picnic lunch, assembled from Nara's excellent food shops, is a pleasant option for a day along the foothills.

## The Return to Nara

The journey back from the Yamato foothills to Nara city is short — under an hour by any route — but the shift in atmosphere is marked. The mountains recede, the basin opens up, and the familiar silhouettes of Nara's temples and pagodas reappear on the horizon. Guests returning to Kanoya ryokan carry with them the memory of a quieter, less visited Nara Prefecture: the ancient peony gardens of Taima-dera, the mossy steps of a mountain shrine, the patient beauty of a merchant town that has not needed to change. It is these experiences, as much as the famous temples, that deepen the understanding of Nara as a living landscape with roots reaching back to the very origins of Japanese civilisation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is Taima-dera known for?

Taima-dera, founded in 612, is one of the oldest temples in Japan. It is famous for the Taima Mandala — a legendary tapestry of the Western Paradise — its twin Nara-period pagodas (National Treasures), spectacular peony gardens, and the dramatic Nerikuyo processional ceremony held each May.

### How do I get to Katsuragi and Gose from Nara?

Taima-dera is approximately fifty minutes from Kintetsu Nara Station via the Kintetsu Line. Gose is reachable in about fifty minutes from JR Nara Station via the JR Wakayama Line. By car, the area is forty-five minutes to one hour from central Nara.

### Is Gose's Shinmachi district worth visiting?

Yes. Shinmachi is one of the best-preserved Edo-period merchant streetscapes in Nara Prefecture — officially designated as a Preservation District for Groups of Traditional Buildings — and offers an intimate, uncrowded glimpse of historic Japanese townscape architecture.

### Can I hike Mount Kongo from the Nara side?

Yes. A well-maintained trail ascends from the Gose area to the summit of Mount Kongo (1,125 metres) in approximately two hours. The summit offers panoramic views across the Nara basin and Osaka plain.
