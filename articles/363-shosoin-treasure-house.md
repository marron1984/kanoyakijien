# The Shōsō-in Treasure House: 9,000 Objects from the 8th Century

Behind Tōdai-ji's Great Buddha Hall, in a quiet corner of the temple compound that most tourists never reach, stands a wooden building that contains one of the most extraordinary collections of artefacts in the world. The Shōsō-in is an 8th-century storehouse holding approximately 9,000 objects — musical instruments, textiles, medicines, weapons, games, mirrors, glassware, and manuscripts — that have survived largely intact for over 1,200 years.

No museum in the world can match what the Shōsō-in offers: a virtually complete picture of material culture from a single period and place, preserved not through archaeological recovery but through continuous, deliberate care. The objects inside were not buried and dug up. They were placed there in the 8th century and have been maintained there ever since.

## How the Collection Was Created

The core of the Shōsō-in collection was created in a single act of devotion. When Emperor Shōmu died in 756 CE, his widow, Empress Kōmyō, donated approximately 600 of his personal belongings to Tōdai-ji. Her dedicatory document survives and lists the objects in detail — an inventory of a life that is also an inventory of an era.

The items Kōmyō donated included:

- **Musical instruments**: Biwa lutes, harps, flutes, and mouth organs, many decorated with intricate inlays
- **Medicines**: Over sixty varieties, including substances from China, India, and Southeast Asia
- **Textiles**: Silk garments, screens, and banners showing techniques from across Asia
- **Writing implements**: Brushes, inkstones, and papers
- **Games**: Go boards, backgammon sets, and dice
- **Weapons**: Swords, bows, and ceremonial armour
- **Mirrors**: Bronze mirrors with designs reflecting both Chinese and Japanese traditions
- **Personal items**: Combs, rulers, and devotional objects

Subsequent donations added to the collection over the following decades. Temple furnishings, ritual objects, and items used in the Great Buddha's eye-opening ceremony of 752 CE were stored alongside Shōmu's personal effects. By the end of the 8th century, the Shōsō-in held a cross-section of the material world of Nara-period Japan.

## The Building Itself

The Shōsō-in is a log-cabin style structure (azekura-zukuri) built of triangular-section cypress logs stacked horizontally without nails. The building is raised on forty stilts approximately 2.7 metres above the ground, which protects the contents from moisture and vermin.

A longstanding popular explanation held that the triangular logs expanded in humid weather to seal out moisture and contracted in dry weather to allow ventilation. Modern research has shown this effect is minimal — the preservation of the contents is more likely due to the building's elevation, the stable temperature provided by the massive log walls, and centuries of attentive management by temple custodians.

The building has three sections: the north section (hokusō), the south section (nansō), and the central section (chūsō). The north section holds Shōmu's personal items and Kōmyō's donation. The south section contains temple furnishings and ritual objects. The central section, an open-air corridor, separates the two.

The Shōsō-in has been designated a National Treasure. Since 1953, the building and its contents have been managed by the Imperial Household Agency rather than Tōdai-ji, reflecting the national significance of the collection.

## What the Objects Reveal

The Shōsō-in collection is important not only for its age and completeness but for what it tells us about the connectivity of the 8th-century world.

### Silk Road Connections

Among the most celebrated objects are those that clearly originated far from Japan:

**The Raden Shitan no Gogenbiwa**: A five-stringed biwa (lute) decorated with mother-of-pearl inlay depicting a figure riding a camel and playing the instrument. The five-stringed biwa is a Central Asian instrument, and this particular piece — with its Persian-influenced imagery — is evidence of cultural connections stretching across the entire Eurasian continent.

**Cut glass bowls**: Several pieces of glass in the collection show Persian (Sassanid) influence, suggesting they were made in Western or Central Asia and transported eastward along the Silk Road.

**Textiles with Persian motifs**: Fabrics featuring paired animals, hunting scenes, and geometric patterns that derive from Sassanid Persian design traditions, filtered through Central Asian and Chinese intermediaries.

**Medicines from Southeast Asia**: The pharmacological collection includes substances that could only have come from tropical regions, demonstrating maritime trade connections to the south.

These objects do not prove that Nara had direct contact with Persia or Central Asia. Most likely, they passed through multiple intermediaries — traded along the Silk Road to China, then brought to Japan by diplomatic missions, monks, or merchants. But they do prove that Nara was connected to a global network of cultural exchange, however indirect.

### Japanese Craftsmanship

The collection also contains outstanding examples of Japanese workmanship from the period:

**Lacquerware**: Boxes, trays, and containers decorated with techniques including maki-e (sprinkled gold), raden (mother-of-pearl inlay), and painted designs.

**Textiles**: Japanese-made fabrics using techniques learned from China and Korea, including brocade, gauze, and tie-dyeing.

**Wooden objects**: Masks used in Gigaku performances (a form of masked dance imported from the Asian continent), musical instruments, and furniture.

**Documents**: Administrative records, sutra copies, and personal correspondence that provide invaluable information about daily life, governance, and religious practice in 8th-century Japan.

### Daily Life

Perhaps the most revealing objects are the ordinary ones. Game boards tell us that the court played Go and backgammon. Combs and cosmetic implements describe grooming practices. Medicines reveal what ailments people feared and how they treated them. Writing instruments show the physical process of literacy. Together, these objects reconstruct a world with a vividness that texts alone cannot achieve.

## The Annual Exhibition

The Shōsō-in building itself is not open to the public. Its exterior can be viewed from outside the fence on the Tōdai-ji grounds, but the contents are shown only during the annual Shōsō-in Exhibition (Shōsō-in Ten) at the Nara National Museum.

### Practical Information

**When**: The exhibition typically runs from late October to mid-November, lasting approximately two weeks. Exact dates are announced each summer by the Nara National Museum.

**Where**: Nara National Museum, located along the main path through Nara Park between Kōfuku-ji and Kasuga Taisha.

**Tickets**: Advance tickets are recommended, as the exhibition draws large crowds. Weekday mornings tend to be less crowded than weekends.

**What you'll see**: Each year, a selection of approximately 60–70 objects is displayed. The selection rotates annually, so repeat visits reveal different aspects of the collection. Some of the most famous pieces — like the five-stringed biwa — appear only once every several years.

**Duration**: Allow at least ninety minutes. The objects are small and detailed, and the contextual information provided is extensive.

### Making the Most of the Exhibition

The Shōsō-in Exhibition is one of the most important annual cultural events in Japan, and planning your Nara visit around it is strongly recommended for anyone with an interest in history or art. Because the exhibition period is short and crowds are significant, staying overnight in Nara offers a practical advantage: you can arrive at the museum when it opens, before the tour groups from Osaka and Kyoto.

Kanoya ryokan is well positioned for early museum visits, and the autumn timing of the exhibition coincides with some of Nara's finest seasonal scenery — the maples in Nara Park are often at or near peak colour during the exhibition weeks.

## Conservation and Continuing Research

The Shōsō-in collection has been the subject of ongoing scholarly research since the Meiji period. Modern techniques — X-ray analysis, chemical testing, carbon dating — have revealed new information about the objects' origins, materials, and construction methods.

Conservation is painstaking and continuous. Objects are repaired using traditional techniques where possible, and the environmental conditions inside the repository are carefully monitored. The Imperial Household Agency publishes detailed catalogues and research reports that are essential references for scholars of East Asian art and history.

In recent years, digital imaging projects have made high-resolution photographs of many objects available online, expanding access to the collection beyond the brief annual exhibition. The Nara National Museum's website provides a searchable database of objects that is worth exploring before your visit.

## Why the Shōsō-in Matters

The Shōsō-in's significance extends beyond art history. It is evidence of a world that was more connected than we tend to imagine — a world in which objects, ideas, and techniques moved across continents through networks of trade, diplomacy, and religious exchange. A Persian glass bowl in a Japanese temple is not an anomaly but a normal product of 8th-century globalisation.

It is also a testament to the value of preservation. Empress Kōmyō's decision to donate her husband's belongings, and Tōdai-ji's commitment to maintaining them for over twelve centuries, created a resource that no amount of money could recreate. Every object in the Shōsō-in exists because someone — many people, across many generations — decided it was worth keeping.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I go inside the Shōsō-in?

No, the building is not open to the public. You can view its exterior from the Tōdai-ji grounds. The contents are displayed only during the annual Shōsō-in Exhibition at the Nara National Museum, typically in late October to mid-November.

### How many objects are in the Shōsō-in?

The collection contains approximately 9,000 objects, ranging from musical instruments and textiles to medicines and documents. About 60–70 items are displayed each year during the annual exhibition.

### Why is the Shōsō-in important?

It provides the most complete picture of 8th-century East Asian material culture anywhere in the world, including objects that travelled the Silk Road from Persia and Central Asia. The collection has been preserved continuously since the 8th century.

### When is the best time to see the Shōsō-in Exhibition?

Weekday mornings during the first week of the exhibition typically have smaller crowds. Check the Nara National Museum website for exact dates, which are announced each summer.
