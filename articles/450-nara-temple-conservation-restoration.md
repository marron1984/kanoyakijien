# How Nara's Temples Are Maintained: Conservation and Restoration

The wooden temples of Nara are among the oldest standing structures in the world. Tōdai-ji's Daibutsuden, Hōryū-ji's five-story pagoda, Yakushi-ji's East Pagoda — these buildings have survived for centuries not because wood is a durable material, but because each generation has committed to maintaining, repairing, and when necessary rebuilding them. The conservation of Nara's temples is one of the most sustained and sophisticated preservation efforts in human history, involving techniques passed down through master-apprentice relationships spanning a millennium, institutional knowledge accumulated over centuries, and a philosophical framework that treats buildings as living entities requiring continuous care rather than static monuments to be frozen in time.

## The Philosophy of Japanese Conservation

### Repair, Not Preserve

Western conservation philosophy, shaped by the nineteenth-century writings of John Ruskin and the Athens Charter of 1931, has traditionally emphasized preserving original material. The ideal is to keep the original fabric of a building intact, intervening as little as possible and clearly distinguishing new material from old.

Japanese conservation philosophy is fundamentally different. It prioritizes the continuity of the building as a functional, living entity over the preservation of original material. If a timber is decayed, it is replaced. If a roof is failing, it is rebuilt. The goal is not to preserve the building exactly as it was at some arbitrary point in its history but to maintain it in a state that allows it to continue fulfilling its purpose — as a place of worship, as a repository of cultural meaning, and as a work of architecture.

This does not mean that original material is discarded carelessly. When a timber is replaced, the new piece is shaped using the same tools and techniques as the original. The replacement timber is of the same species, from the same region if possible, and prepared using methods that replicate the original craftsmanship. The result is a building that may contain very little eighth-century wood but that is, in a meaningful sense, still an eighth-century building — because the form, the techniques, and the intent are continuous.

### The Ise Shrine Model

The most extreme expression of this philosophy is the shikinen sengū of Ise Grand Shrine, where the entire shrine complex is rebuilt from scratch every twenty years. This practice, which has been maintained for over 1,300 years, ensures that the construction techniques are continuously transmitted and that the shrine is always structurally sound. Nara's temples do not follow this extreme model, but the underlying principle — that maintenance is an ongoing process, not a one-time event — is the same.

## The Craft Traditions

### Miyadaiku: Temple Carpenters

The craftspeople responsible for maintaining Nara's temples are called miyadaiku — literally "shrine and temple carpenters." These are not ordinary construction workers. They are specialists in traditional timber-frame joinery, trained through apprenticeships that typically last ten to fifteen years. They work with hand tools — planes, chisels, saws, adzes — that have not changed fundamentally in centuries, and they possess knowledge of wood properties, structural engineering, and architectural history that exists nowhere in written form.

The miyadaiku tradition is recognized by UNESCO as an Intangible Cultural Heritage — an acknowledgment that the skills required to maintain Japan's wooden architecture are as culturally significant as the buildings themselves. Without miyadaiku, the temples cannot be maintained. Without the temples, the miyadaiku have no reason to exist. The relationship is symbiotic.

Nara is one of the primary centers of the miyadaiku tradition. The Nara National Research Institute for Cultural Properties, established in 1952, conducts research into traditional construction techniques and trains craftspeople in methods that might otherwise be lost. Several private workshops in the Nara region specialize in temple carpentry, passing skills from master to apprentice in the same way they have been passed for generations.

### The Knowledge in the Hands

What makes miyadaiku knowledge distinctive is that much of it cannot be written down. The feel of a properly sharpened blade. The sound of a chisel cutting clean grain versus cross grain. The way a joint should resist when tested by hand. The slight flex in a timber that indicates adequate moisture content. These are sensory skills that can only be learned through years of practice under the guidance of an experienced master.

When a miyadaiku examines a centuries-old joint during a restoration, they are reading the work of a predecessor — understanding the original craftsman's intentions, assessing the current condition, and determining how to maintain the structural integrity while respecting the original design. This reading is possible only because the examiner's training replicates, as closely as possible, the training of the original builder.

### Specialist Crafts Beyond Carpentry

Temple conservation involves numerous specialties beyond carpentry:

- **Kawara-shi** (roof tile makers) produce replacement tiles using traditional clay bodies and firing techniques
- **Sakan** (plasterers) apply and repair the earthen plaster walls using methods that have changed little in centuries
- **Shikkō-shi** (lacquer workers) apply and maintain the lacquer finishes on architectural elements and Buddhist statuary
- **Kinpaku-shi** (gold leaf artisans) restore the gold leaf on Buddhist images and architectural ornament
- **Chōkoku-shi** (sculptors) repair and restore wooden and bronze statuary
- **Saishiki-shi** (painters) restore polychrome decoration on temple buildings and objects

Each of these specialties has its own tradition of master-apprentice training, its own body of embodied knowledge, and its own vulnerability to the passage of time and the shrinking pool of practitioners.

## Major Restoration Projects in Nara

### Yakushi-ji East Pagoda

One of the most significant recent restoration projects in Nara has been the dismantling and reassembly of Yakushi-ji's East Pagoda (Tōtō), a National Treasure dating originally to the eighth century. This project, which began in 2009 and was completed in 2021, involved completely dismantling the pagoda, examining every component, replacing damaged timbers with new material prepared using traditional techniques, and reassembling the structure.

The project revealed extraordinary information about eighth-century construction methods. Inscriptions and tool marks on hidden surfaces provided evidence of how the original builders organized their work. Structural analysis revealed the engineering logic behind the pagoda's earthquake resistance. The restoration was not merely a repair job but an archaeological investigation conducted in three dimensions.

### Tōdai-ji Daibutsuden Roof Restoration

The Great Buddha Hall of Tōdai-ji undergoes periodic roof maintenance — a massive undertaking given that the building is the largest wooden structure in the world. Roof restoration involves removing and replacing thousands of tiles, inspecting and repairing the timber framework that supports them, and ensuring that the drainage systems that channel rainwater away from the structure are functioning properly.

These projects take years to complete and are conducted while the temple remains open to worshippers and visitors. Scaffolding is erected around sections of the building, and work proceeds methodically through each area. Visitors during restoration periods may find portions of the exterior obscured by scaffolding, but the interior — including the Great Buddha — remains accessible.

### Kōfuku-ji Central Golden Hall

Kōfuku-ji's Central Golden Hall (Chūkondō), destroyed by fire in 1717, was reconstructed between 2010 and 2018. This was not a restoration but a reconstruction — a new building based on historical evidence of the original's form. The project used traditional construction techniques and materials, providing both a new landmark for Nara and a training ground for the next generation of miyadaiku.

## The Institutional Framework

### Government Oversight

Japan's system for protecting cultural properties is among the most comprehensive in the world. The Agency for Cultural Affairs, under the Ministry of Education, designates and oversees National Treasures and Important Cultural Properties. Nara Prefecture and Nara city maintain their own cultural property programs. The designation system provides both legal protection and funding for conservation work.

The designation of a building as a National Treasure or Important Cultural Property does not transfer ownership — Tōdai-ji's buildings, for example, remain the property of the temple — but it does bring the building under a regulatory framework that requires conservation work to meet specific standards and to be supervised by qualified professionals.

### The Nara National Research Institute for Cultural Properties

This institute, established in the early postwar period, is the primary research institution for architectural conservation in Japan. Its staff conduct research into traditional materials and techniques, provide technical guidance for restoration projects, and maintain archives of architectural documentation spanning centuries. The institute's work ensures that restoration decisions are based on the best available historical and scientific evidence.

### Funding

Temple conservation in Japan is funded through a combination of government subsidies (typically covering 50-85 percent of the cost for designated cultural properties), temple revenue (including admission fees, donations, and income from religious activities), and private donations. Major restoration projects can cost billions of yen and take decades to complete. The financial sustainability of conservation is an ongoing concern, particularly for smaller temples without the tourism revenue of major sites like Tōdai-ji.

## What Visitors Can See

### Restoration in Progress

Encountering a temple under restoration can initially feel disappointing — scaffolding and tarps are not what most visitors hope to photograph. But restoration work, when visible, offers a rare opportunity to see the building's internal structure exposed, to observe craftspeople at work, and to understand the ongoing human effort required to maintain these structures.

Some temples make their restoration work explicitly accessible. Informational displays explain the project, viewing platforms allow visitors to watch craftspeople at work, and special exhibitions present findings from the restoration process. These opportunities are worth seeking out.

### The Evidence of Care

Even when no active restoration is underway, the evidence of continuous maintenance is visible throughout Nara's temples. Freshly oiled wooden surfaces. Recently replaced roof tiles, distinguishable by their slightly different color from their neighbors. Repaired plaster walls. Newly lacquered columns. These details, easily overlooked, are evidence of a commitment to preservation that has been sustained for more than a thousand years.

Visitors who stay in Naramachi, at accommodations such as Kanoya ryokan, and spend multiple days exploring Nara's temples develop an eye for these details. The first visit to a temple is inevitably dominated by the overall impression — the scale, the beauty, the age. Subsequent visits allow attention to shift to the evidence of care: the craftsmanship of a joint, the texture of freshly applied plaster, the precision of a newly laid tile. This is when the conservation story becomes visible and the full achievement of Nara's preservation tradition becomes clear.

## The Future of Conservation

The greatest threat to Nara's temple conservation is not structural or financial but human. The pool of skilled craftspeople is shrinking. Fewer young people are willing to undertake the long, demanding apprenticeships required to master traditional techniques. The raw materials — large-diameter timbers of specific species, traditional clay for plaster, natural lacquer — are becoming scarcer and more expensive.

Japan is addressing these challenges through institutional programs, UNESCO recognition, and efforts to raise the profile and compensation of traditional craftspeople. But the challenge is real. The temples of Nara have survived for over a millennium because each generation produced people willing and able to maintain them. Whether the next generation will do the same is not guaranteed — but the structures themselves, standing in their ancient precincts, provide a powerful argument for the continuing value of the effort.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How old are the oldest buildings in Nara?
The oldest buildings in the greater Nara area are at Hōryū-ji, dating to the early seventh century, making them the oldest wooden structures in the world. Within Nara city, major structures date from the eighth century onward.

### How do wooden buildings last over 1,000 years?
*Wooden buildings in Nara survive not because wood is inherently durable but because of continuous maintenance and periodic restoration by skilled craftspeople called miyadaiku. When timbers decay, they are replaced using the same species, tools, and techniques as the originals. This philosophy prioritizes the continuity of the building as a living entity over the preservation of original material, ensuring structural integrity across centuries.*

### What is a miyadaiku?
A miyadaiku is a traditional Japanese temple and shrine carpenter, trained through a 10-15 year apprenticeship in timber-frame joinery using hand tools and methods that have been passed down for generations. UNESCO recognizes this craft tradition as an Intangible Cultural Heritage.

### Can visitors watch temple restoration work?
Sometimes. Major restoration projects occasionally provide viewing platforms or informational displays. The timing is unpredictable, but encountering active restoration offers a unique opportunity to see internal structures and traditional craftsmanship in action.

### Who pays for temple conservation in Nara?
Conservation is funded through government subsidies (50-85 percent for designated cultural properties), temple revenue from admission fees and donations, and private contributions. Major restoration projects can cost billions of yen and take decades to complete.
