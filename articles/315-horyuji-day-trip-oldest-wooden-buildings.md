# Hōryū-ji Day Trip from Nara: The World's Oldest Wooden Buildings

Eleven kilometres southwest of Nara, in the town of Ikaruga, stands a group of buildings that have been standing longer than any other wooden structures on earth. Hōryū-ji was founded in 607 CE by Prince Shōtoku, one of the most important figures in Japanese history, and its Western Precinct contains the world's oldest surviving wooden buildings — the five-storey pagoda and the main hall (Kondō), both dating from the late 7th century. To stand before them is to encounter a fact that resists easy comprehension: these buildings have been standing for approximately 1,340 years.

Hōryū-ji was the first site in Japan to be designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site, in 1993. It is also one of the most rewarding day trips from Nara — close enough for a half-day visit, yet far enough from the city's main tourist circuit to feel like a separate journey.

## History

### Prince Shōtoku's Vision

Prince Shōtoku (574–622 CE) is credited with establishing Buddhism as a central institution of Japanese governance and culture. His construction of Hōryū-ji — originally named Ikaruga-dera — was part of a broader programme of temple building that aimed to position Japan as a serious Buddhist nation. Shōtoku is also traditionally credited with authoring Japan's first constitution (the Seventeen-Article Constitution of 604) and with establishing diplomatic relations with Sui Dynasty China.

The original Hōryū-ji burned in 670 CE and was rebuilt shortly after, probably by 711 CE. This reconstruction is what survives today. The buildings of the Western Precinct — the pagoda, Kondō, Inner Gate (Chūmon), and surrounding cloister — form the oldest surviving wooden architectural ensemble in the world.

### Why the Buildings Survived

Hōryū-ji's survival for over thirteen centuries is partly a matter of luck — it escaped the fires, earthquakes, and wars that destroyed most of its contemporaries. But it also reflects continuous care. The temple has been maintained by successive generations of monks and craftsmen who understood traditional building techniques and intervened when necessary. Japanese wooden architecture is designed for periodic repair: individual components can be replaced without compromising the overall structure, a philosophy of conservation that prioritises continuity over pristine originality.

The wood itself — Japanese cypress (hinoki) — is naturally resistant to insects and decay. Dendrochronological analysis of the temple's timbers has confirmed that some date from trees felled in the late 7th century, with the oldest measuring over 2,000 years in age at the time of felling.

## The Western Precinct (Sai-in)

### The Five-Storey Pagoda

At 32.5 metres, the pagoda is not the tallest in Japan, but it is the oldest. Its proportions — each successive storey slightly smaller than the one below, with broad, sweeping eaves — create an impression of upward movement that is both dynamic and stable. The pagoda does not contain a viewing platform; its function is symbolic, housing sacred relics beneath its central pillar.

At the base of the pagoda, visible through small windows, are four tableaux of painted clay figures depicting scenes from the life of the Buddha. These Nara-period sculptures are among the oldest surviving clay figures in Japan and are easily overlooked — bend down and peer through the openings.

### The Kondō (Main Hall)

The Kondō houses Hōryū-ji's principal images, including a Shaka Nyorai triad by the sculptor Tori Busshi (623 CE) — one of the earliest dated Buddhist sculptures in Japan. The gilt-bronze figure, with its archaic, frontal pose and enigmatic smile, reflects the aesthetic of 7th-century Korean and Chinese Buddhist sculpture. Around the central triad, guardian figures and attendant deities create a devotional programme that has remained essentially intact for thirteen centuries.

The building itself is a masterwork. Its two-storey structure with an encircling veranda, its cloud-bracket supports (kumo-hijiki), and its deep, overhanging eaves represent an architectural vocabulary that was already sophisticated when these buildings were new.

### The Chūmon (Inner Gate)

The Inner Gate, with its pair of Niō guardian figures (Japan's oldest surviving examples, dating from 711 CE), frames the entrance to the Western Precinct. The gate's unusual feature is its central column, which divides the entrance into two passages — a rarity in Japanese temple architecture that may reflect continental Asian prototypes.

## The Eastern Precinct (Tō-in)

### The Yumedono (Hall of Dreams)

The Eastern Precinct centres on the Yumedono, an octagonal hall built in 739 CE on the site of Prince Shōtoku's former palace. Inside, the Guze Kannon — a gilt-wood figure believed to represent Shōtoku himself — is one of the most celebrated sculptures in Japanese art. For centuries, the statue was kept hidden, wrapped in cloth, and was not publicly shown until Ernest Fenollosa and Okakura Tenshin opened it in 1884. The Guze Kannon is displayed during limited periods in spring (April 11 – May 18) and autumn (October 22 – November 22).

### The Tōin Garden

The garden surrounding the Yumedono, with its carefully placed stones and seasonal plantings, offers a quiet rest point between the two precincts. The walk from the Western to the Eastern Precinct takes about five minutes through a path lined with earthen walls — a transitional space that allows the visual and emotional shift between the two architectural programmes.

## The Daihōzōin (Great Treasure Hall)

Hōryū-ji's modern treasure museum houses one of the finest collections of Buddhist art in Japan. The Kudara Kannon — a tall, slender wooden figure of mysterious origin and extraordinary grace — is the collection's most famous work. Its attenuated proportions and gentle forward lean give it an otherworldly quality that has inspired artists and writers for centuries. The museum also displays the Tamamushi Shrine (a miniature shrine decorated with iridescent beetle-wing inlay), textiles, metalwork, and manuscripts spanning the temple's 1,400-year history.

Allow at least 45 minutes for the museum. The Kudara Kannon alone is worth the visit.

## Getting There from Nara

### By Train

The most straightforward route is by JR Yamatoji Line from JR Nara Station to Hōryū-ji Station (12 minutes, ¥220). From the station, it is a 20-minute walk north to the temple, along a pleasant road that passes through the quiet town of Ikaruga. Alternatively, a bus runs from the station to the temple in about 8 minutes.

### By Bus

Direct buses from Kintetsu Nara Station to Hōryū-ji are available but less frequent. Check schedules in advance.

### Recommended Itinerary

A comfortable Hōryū-ji visit from Nara requires three to four hours including travel time:
- 9:00am: Depart JR Nara Station
- 9:15am: Arrive Hōryū-ji Station, walk to temple
- 9:35am–12:00pm: Visit Western Precinct, Eastern Precinct, and Treasure Museum
- 12:15pm: Walk back to Hōryū-ji Station
- 12:30pm: Return to Nara

This allows a leisurely pace with time to absorb the buildings and art. Afternoon visitors should note that the temple can feel hurried if you arrive after 2:00pm, particularly outside summer months when closing time comes earlier.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are the buildings at Hōryū-ji really the oldest wooden buildings in the world?

Yes. The five-storey pagoda and Kondō in the Western Precinct date from the late 7th century (c. 670–711 CE) and are confirmed by dendrochronology as the world's oldest surviving wooden structures.

### How long should I spend at Hōryū-ji?

Allow two to two-and-a-half hours at the temple to see both precincts and the Treasure Museum without rushing. Including travel from Nara, plan for a half-day trip of three to four hours.

### When can I see the Guze Kannon?

The Guze Kannon in the Yumedono is shown during two annual periods: spring (April 11–May 18) and autumn (October 22–November 22). Outside these dates, the hall is open but the statue is not visible.

### Is Hōryū-ji crowded?

Hōryū-ji receives far fewer visitors than Nara's central temples. Weekday mornings are especially quiet, and even during peak seasons the temple rarely feels crowded. The spacious layout and multiple precincts distribute visitors effectively.

### Can I combine Hōryū-ji with other sites?

The nearby Chūgū-ji nunnery (adjacent to the Eastern Precinct) houses a beautiful Miroku Bosatsu statue and takes only 20 minutes to visit. Hokki-ji temple, a 15-minute walk north, has another ancient pagoda. Both combine naturally with a Hōryū-ji visit.
