# Nara's Ancient Capital History: From Heijō-kyō to Modern City

There is a particular quality to walking through Nara that no other Japanese city quite replicates. Beneath the parkland where deer graze and beside the temples that draw millions of visitors each year lies something older and deeper — the foundations of Japan itself. Nara was not merely one of Japan's early capitals; it was the city where Japanese civilisation first took coherent, monumental form. Understanding that history transforms a pleasant sightseeing visit into something genuinely profound.

## Before Heijō-kyō: The Asuka Period Prelude

Japan's capital was not always fixed. During the Asuka period (538–710), the imperial court moved frequently — sometimes with every new emperor, following the Shinto belief that death polluted a location. The Asuka region, south of present-day Nara, served as the loose political centre during this era, and it was there that Buddhism first took institutional root after its introduction from Korea in the sixth century.

The pivotal moment came in 694, when Empress Jitō established Fujiwara-kyō — Japan's first Chinese-style grid capital — near present-day Kashihara. But Fujiwara-kyō proved too small for the growing ambitions of the Japanese state. Within just sixteen years, a new and grander capital was planned.

## The Founding of Heijō-kyō (710)

In 710, Empress Genmei ordered the capital moved to Heijō-kyō — the "Citadel of Peace" — on a broad plain at the northern edge of the Nara Basin. The site was chosen according to Chinese geomantic principles: mountains to the north, east, and west, with open land to the south. The city was modelled on Chang'an (modern Xi'an), the Tang Dynasty capital that was then the largest and most cosmopolitan city in the world.

### The City Layout

Heijō-kyō covered approximately 25 square kilometres. A broad central avenue, Suzaku Ōji, ran north to south, dividing the city into symmetrical eastern and western halves. The Imperial Palace (Heijō Palace) occupied the northern centre, with government ministries, aristocratic residences, markets, and temples arranged in a strict grid pattern.

At its peak, Heijō-kyō housed an estimated 100,000 to 200,000 people — making it one of the larger cities in the eighth-century world. It was a cosmopolitan place: diplomats from Tang China, scholars from the Korean kingdoms of Silla and Baekje, monks from as far as India, and traders connected to the Silk Road all passed through its gates.

### The Heijō Palace Site Today

The Heijō Palace site (Heijō-kyū-seki) is now a vast archaeological park in western Nara. Excavations beginning in the 1950s have revealed building foundations, gardens, wooden tablets (mokkan) containing government records, and everyday objects that collectively paint a vivid picture of eighth-century court life. The reconstructed Daigoku-den (Great Audience Hall) and Suzaku Gate give visitors a physical sense of the capital's original scale and grandeur. The site was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1998.

## The Nara Period Golden Age (710–784)

The seventy-four years of the Nara period represent one of the most culturally productive eras in Japanese history. With a stable capital and strong connections to continental Asia, Japan absorbed, adapted, and began to synthesise the cultural influences that would define its civilisation for the next millennium.

### Buddhism as State Project

Emperor Shōmu (r. 724–749) made Buddhism the spiritual foundation of the state. His most ambitious project was the construction of Tōdai-ji and its Great Buddha (Daibutsu) — a 15-metre seated bronze Vairocana Buddha completed in 752. The dedication ceremony drew dignitaries from across Asia and marked Japan's emergence as a major Buddhist civilisation.

Shōmu also established the kokubunji system — a network of state-sponsored temples in every province — with Tōdai-ji as the head temple. This was not merely religious devotion; it was political architecture, using Buddhism to unify a geographically dispersed nation under imperial authority.

### The Great Temples

The great temples of Nara were not only spiritual centres but also repositories of art, learning, and political power. Kōfuku-ji, the family temple of the powerful Fujiwara clan, rivalled the imperial court in influence. Yakushi-ji and Tōshōdai-ji became centres of Buddhist scholarship. Gangō-ji preserved the oldest Buddhist architectural elements in Japan. Kasuga Taisha, the Shinto shrine of the Fujiwara, demonstrated the already-developing synthesis of Buddhism and Shinto that would characterise Japanese religion for centuries.

### The Shōsōin Treasury

Perhaps the single most remarkable legacy of the Nara period is the Shōsōin, the imperial treasury at Tōdai-ji. This simple log-cabin storehouse has preserved over 9,000 objects from the eighth century — textiles, musical instruments, medicines, games, glass vessels, and ceremonial objects — many originating from Persia, Central Asia, China, and Southeast Asia. The collection is an unparalleled window into the cosmopolitan world of Nara-period Japan and its connections along the Silk Road.

### Literature and Learning

The Nara period produced two foundational texts of Japanese civilisation. The Kojiki (712) and Nihon Shoki (720) were the first written histories of Japan, establishing the mythological and historical narrative of the imperial line. The Man'yōshū, compiled around 759, is the oldest surviving collection of Japanese poetry, containing over 4,500 poems by emperors, courtiers, soldiers, and commoners alike — a breadth of voice unmatched in contemporary world literature.

## The Capital Moves On (784)

In 784, Emperor Kanmu made the decision to move the capital — first briefly to Nagaoka-kyō, then permanently to Heian-kyō (Kyoto) in 794. The reasons were complex: the growing political power of the Nara Buddhist temples, which had begun to interfere directly in governance; the desire for a fresh start unconstrained by established clerical interests; and perhaps practical concerns about water supply and disease.

The move could have meant Nara's destruction — abandoned capitals often decayed rapidly in premodern Japan. But the great temples remained. Unlike the imperial court, which needed to move, the Buddhist institutions were rooted to their sites by centuries of accumulated sacred architecture, art, and tradition. The temples preserved Nara through the centuries that followed.

## Medieval and Early Modern Nara (794–1868)

After losing its capital status, Nara entered a long period as a temple town — politically marginal but culturally rich. The great temples continued to wield economic power through their landholdings, and Nara remained an important pilgrimage destination.

### Conflict and Destruction

Nara was not spared from Japan's medieval turbulence. In 1180, during the Genpei War, the warrior-monk armies of Kōfuku-ji clashed with the Taira clan, and the resulting fire destroyed much of Tōdai-ji and Kōfuku-ji — including the Great Buddha Hall. The rebuilding, led by the monk Chōgen with support from the Kamakura shogunate, produced the structures that largely survive today.

The Sengoku period (15th–16th centuries) brought further disruption, as temples lost much of their landholding power. But Nara's identity as a sacred city provided a degree of protection that purely political capitals did not enjoy.

### Edo Period Stability

Under the Tokugawa shogunate (1603–1868), Nara settled into quiet prosperity. The temples maintained their treasures, the deer were protected by religious decree (killing a deer in Nara was a capital offence), and the town developed the merchant quarter now known as Naramachi. Pilgrims continued to visit, and Nara's cultural artifacts were carefully preserved through centuries of relative peace.

## Modern Nara (1868–Present)

The Meiji Restoration of 1868 brought dramatic change. The new government's initial anti-Buddhist policies (haibutsu kishaku) threatened temples nationwide, and Nara's institutions suffered significant losses of land, artwork, and status. Kōfuku-ji's five-storey pagoda was reportedly offered for sale as scrap — a deal that fortunately fell through.

### Preservation and Recognition

The late 19th century brought a counter-movement: recognition that Nara's temples and artworks represented irreplaceable national heritage. The establishment of the Nara National Museum in 1895, early cultural property protection laws, and growing international awareness gradually secured the city's treasures.

The creation of Nara Park in 1880 formalised the parkland that connected the great temples, and the arrival of the railway in 1890 made Nara accessible to modern tourists. Throughout the 20th century, archaeological excavations — particularly at the Heijō Palace site beginning in 1959 — continued to reveal the scope of the ancient capital.

### UNESCO and the 21st Century

In 1998, the "Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara" were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, encompassing eight locations: Tōdai-ji, Kōfuku-ji, Kasuga Taisha, the Kasuga Primeval Forest, Gangō-ji, Yakushi-ji, Tōshōdai-ji, and the Heijō Palace site. This recognition cemented Nara's status as one of the world's great historical cities.

Today, Nara balances preservation with livability. The population of approximately 350,000 supports a modern economy while maintaining the atmospheric historic core that draws visitors. The city continues to invest in archaeological research, temple restoration, and cultural tourism infrastructure — including the growing number of traditional ryokan and guesthouses that allow visitors to experience Nara's heritage intimately.

## Experiencing Nara's History Today

The remarkable thing about Nara is that its history is not confined to museums. The deer that graze in the park are descendants of the sacred deer that roamed here in the eighth century. The temples that line the eastern hills contain sculptures and architectural elements dating to the Nara period itself. The Kasuga Primeval Forest has been protected from logging for over a thousand years.

For visitors who wish to absorb this history at an unhurried pace, staying overnight in Nara is essential. A ryokan stay — such as at Kanoya, located in the heart of the historic district — allows the experience of waking to temple bells and walking the park in the early morning quiet, before the day-trip crowds arrive from Osaka and Kyoto. It is in those quiet moments that the continuity of Nara's 1,300-year history feels most tangible.

### A Suggested Historical Itinerary

**Morning**: Begin at the Heijō Palace archaeological site to understand the scale of the original capital. The museum and reconstructed buildings provide essential context.

**Midday**: Walk east to Tōdai-ji, following roughly the path that courtiers would have taken from the palace to the great temple. The Great Buddha Hall and Nigatsu-dō offer the most powerful connection to Nara-period ambition.

**Afternoon**: Explore Kōfuku-ji's National Treasure Hall for its extraordinary sculpture collection, then walk through Naramachi to see the merchant-quarter architecture that represents Nara's post-capital evolution.

**Evening**: Visit Kasuga Taisha at dusk, when the 3,000 lanterns create an atmosphere that has changed little in centuries.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why was Nara the capital of Japan?

Nara (Heijō-kyō) became Japan's capital in 710 because the previous capital, Fujiwara-kyō, was too small for the growing state. The Nara site was chosen for its favourable geography according to Chinese geomantic principles and its proximity to existing Buddhist temples and transport routes.

### How long was Nara the capital?

Nara served as Japan's capital for 74 years, from 710 to 784, when Emperor Kanmu moved the capital to escape the political influence of Nara's powerful Buddhist temples.

### What is the Nara period famous for?

The Nara period is famous for the construction of Tōdai-ji and its Great Buddha, the compilation of Japan's first written histories (Kojiki and Nihon Shoki) and oldest poetry collection (Man'yōshū), and the flourishing of Buddhist art and architecture influenced by the Silk Road.

### Can you still see the ancient capital in Nara?

Yes. The Heijō Palace archaeological site preserves the footprint of the original capital with reconstructed buildings and an excellent museum. The great temples — Tōdai-ji, Kōfuku-ji, Kasuga Taisha, and others — contain structures, sculptures, and artifacts dating to or near the Nara period.
