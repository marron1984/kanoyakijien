# Nara's Sake Lees Cuisine: Cooking with Kasu Beyond Narazuke

Most visitors to Nara encounter sake lees in a single form: narazuke, the pungent, amber-colored pickles that are the city's most famous food souvenir. These deeply fermented vegetables, preserved in sake kasu for months or even years, are an acquired taste — rich, complex, and unapologetically funky. But narazuke, celebrated as it is, represents only one expression of a much broader culinary tradition. In Nara, where sake brewing has roots stretching back to the temples of the fifteenth century, the creative use of sake lees — kasu — extends far beyond pickles into soups, marinades, confections, and contemporary innovations that are redefining what this ancient ingredient can do.

Understanding kasu cuisine opens a window into a food culture built on fermentation, resourcefulness, and the Japanese principle that nothing of value should be wasted.

## What is Sake Kasu?

### The Byproduct That Became an Ingredient

Sake kasu is the solid residue left after sake is pressed from its fermented rice mash. When the moromi — the bubbling mixture of rice, water, koji mold, and yeast that constitutes fermenting sake — has completed its transformation, it is pressed through cloth or mesh to separate the clear liquid (sake) from the solids. Those solids are kasu.

Fresh sake kasu is a pale, fragrant, slightly crumbly paste with a texture somewhere between cream cheese and modeling clay. It smells of sake — fruity, slightly sweet, with the complex aroma of fermentation — and it tastes mildly alcoholic, sweet, and savory. It retains significant nutritional value: protein from the rice, B vitamins produced during fermentation, amino acids, and beneficial enzymes.

In a less resourceful food culture, kasu might be treated as waste. In Japan, it became a versatile cooking ingredient, a preservation medium, a health food, and even a beauty product. The transformation of a brewing byproduct into a culinary tradition is characteristic of Japanese food culture's fundamental commitment to using every part of every process.

### Nara's Sake Connection

Nara's claim to sake kasu cuisine rests on the city's historical role in the development of sake brewing itself. The temples of Nara, particularly Shoryakuji in the southern hills, are credited with developing the techniques of polished-rice brewing that produced the clear, refined sake recognized today. During the Muromachi period (1336-1573), temple breweries — soboshu — produced sake of such quality that it was sought after throughout Japan.

Where there was sake brewing, there was kasu — large quantities of it, produced seasonally and requiring use before spoilage. The concentration of brewing activity in and around Nara naturally produced a concentration of kasu cuisine, and the recipes that developed over centuries became part of the local food identity.

Today, Nara Prefecture maintains an active sake brewing industry, with numerous kuramoto (breweries) producing both sake and the fresh kasu that supplies local kitchens and food producers.

## Beyond Narazuke: The Breadth of Kasu Cuisine

### Kasujiru: The Warming Soup

Kasujiru — sake lees soup — is the most widespread kasu dish in Japanese home cooking and one of the great comfort foods of the Japanese winter. The basic preparation dissolves sake kasu into a dashi-based broth along with root vegetables (daikon radish, carrot, gobo burdock), tofu, and often salmon or pork. The kasu thickens the soup and gives it a rich, slightly sweet, deeply savory character that warms from the inside on cold days.

In Nara, kasujiru appears on restaurant menus and in household kitchens from late autumn through early spring. Regional variations include the addition of local vegetables, the use of white miso alongside the kasu for a double-fermented richness, and the inclusion of konnyaku (konjac) for textural contrast. Some versions are thin and brothy; others are thick enough to stand a spoon in. All share the characteristic warmth and complexity that fermented rice solids bring to a liquid dish.

The alcohol in fresh kasu is largely cooked off during the soup's preparation, leaving the flavor without the intoxication — though some cooks deliberately add the kasu late, retaining a whisper of sake warmth in the finished bowl.

### Kasu Marinades and Preservation

Marinating fish or meat in sake kasu — kasuzuke — is a technique that predates refrigeration and survives because its results are genuinely delicious. The method is straightforward: a bed of kasu, seasoned with mirin and salt, is spread in a container, the protein is placed on top, and more kasu is spread over it. The container is sealed and refrigerated for one to three days.

During marination, the enzymes in the kasu tenderize the protein while the sugars and amino acids penetrate its surface, creating a complex flavor when the fish or meat is later grilled. The Maillard reaction between the kasu-derived sugars and the protein's surface produces a beautiful caramelized crust — golden brown, slightly sweet, intensely aromatic — that is one of the signature flavors of Japanese grilling.

Kasuzuke salmon (sake no kasuzuke) is the most famous preparation, and it appears frequently on ryokan breakfast trays across Japan. But the technique works beautifully with other fish — cod, sea bream, yellowtail — and with pork and chicken. Some Nara cooks also apply it to vegetables, particularly root vegetables that benefit from the tenderizing effect of the enzymes.

### Kasu in Confections and Sweets

The sweet side of kasu is less well known internationally but deeply established in Japanese food culture. Sake kasu's natural sweetness and aromatic complexity make it a natural partner for wagashi (Japanese confections) and Western-style baked goods alike.

In Nara, several confectioners produce kasu-flavored sweets. These range from traditional manju (steamed buns) filled with kasu-blended sweet bean paste to modern cakes, chocolates, and ice creams that use kasu as a flavoring element. The resulting sweets have a sophisticated, adult character — the sake aroma adds depth without dominating, and the slight umami undertone of the fermented rice distinguishes them from purely sugar-driven confections.

Amazake — the sweet, thick, low-alcohol drink made from rice koji and sometimes enriched with sake kasu — is another expression of this sweet tradition. In Nara, amazake is served warm at temple stalls during winter festivals and cold as a summer refreshment, a versatile beverage that bridges the gap between food and drink.

### Kasu in Contemporary Cooking

Younger chefs in Nara and across Japan are finding new applications for sake kasu that move beyond traditional preparations. Kasu is being used as a base for salad dressings, blended into pasta sauces, incorporated into bread doughs (where its enzymes enhance fermentation and its flavor adds complexity), and used as a marinade base for grilled vegetables in plant-based cooking.

These contemporary applications are not departures from tradition so much as extensions of the same principle that has always governed kasu cuisine: this is a flavor-rich, nutritionally valuable ingredient, and it would be wasteful not to use it creatively.

## Where to Experience Kasu Cuisine in Nara

### Narazuke Shops

The starting point for any kasu food exploration in Nara is the narazuke shops that line the city's shopping streets and cluster around Nara Station and Naramachi. Establishments like Imanishi Seibei Shoten have been producing narazuke for generations and offer tastings that allow you to experience the full range of the product — from lightly pickled, mildly flavored young narazuke to deeply fermented, intensely complex aged versions.

Many narazuke shops also sell fresh sake kasu for home cooking, particularly during the pressing season in late winter and early spring when the kasu is freshest. Buying a block of kasu and experimenting with it in a kitchen — even a simple hotel room preparation like mixing it with miso for a dip — is one of the most direct ways to engage with Nara's fermented food culture.

### Restaurants and Izakaya

Restaurants serving kasujiru, kasuzuke grilled fish, and other kasu dishes are found throughout Nara, particularly during the colder months. Izakaya (informal drinking establishments) are often the best places to find creative kasu preparations, as the ingredient's affinity for sake makes it a natural accompaniment to drinking.

Ask for kasu-related dishes by name — kasujiru, kasuzuke, kasu-ae (vegetables dressed with kasu) — or simply tell the staff you are interested in sake kasu cuisine. In a city where sake heritage is a point of civic pride, this inquiry is likely to be met with enthusiasm and recommendations.

### Sake Brewery Visits

Several of Nara's sake breweries offer tours and tastings that include discussion of kasu and its uses. Visiting a brewery during the pressing season (typically January through March) gives you the chance to see fresh kasu being produced and to taste it at its most vibrant. Some breweries sell kasu directly, and a few have affiliated restaurants or cafes where kasu dishes are served alongside their sake.

## Kasu and the Ryokan Table

The kaiseki cuisine served at Kanoya ryokan draws on the full vocabulary of Japanese cooking techniques, and fermentation — in the form of miso, soy sauce, vinegar, and sake kasu — is woven throughout. The kasuzuke fish that may appear on a breakfast tray, the subtle kasu note in a winter soup course, or the narazuke offered as part of the evening's selection of pickles all represent connections to Nara's sake brewing heritage.

For guests attuned to these flavors, the kasu presence in the meal becomes a thread connecting the table to the breweries of the surrounding region, to the rice paddies that supply them, and to the centuries of fermentation expertise that transformed a byproduct into a cuisine.

## The Nutritional and Health Dimension

Sake kasu has attracted attention from nutritional researchers for its content of resistant protein, dietary fiber, B vitamins, and compounds called alpha-ethyl glucoside, which have shown preliminary evidence of beneficial effects on blood sugar regulation. While health claims should be treated with appropriate skepticism, the long Japanese tradition of regarding kasu as a healthful food — good for skin, digestion, and winter vitality — is not without scientific basis.

The fermentation process that creates kasu also produces lactic acid bacteria and other microorganisms that are consistent with the growing scientific interest in fermented foods and gut health. Whether or not specific health benefits are proven, sake kasu is undeniably a nutrient-dense, minimally processed food with a complex biochemical profile — qualities that align with contemporary nutritional thinking.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is sake kasu?
Sake kasu is the solid residue remaining after sake is pressed from its fermented rice mash. It is a pale, fragrant paste rich in protein, B vitamins, amino acids, and beneficial enzymes. In Japanese cooking, it is used as a pickling medium, soup ingredient, marinade, and flavoring agent.

### What does narazuke taste like?
Narazuke has a rich, complex flavor that combines sweetness, umami, and a distinctive fermented character. The taste deepens with aging — young narazuke is mild and lightly sweet, while aged versions develop intense, complex flavors. The residual sake aroma gives narazuke an alcoholic warmth.

### Can you cook with sake kasu at home?
Yes, sake kasu is very accessible for home cooking. The simplest preparations include dissolving it into miso soup for added richness, using it as a marinade for fish (spread kasu mixed with mirin and salt over fillets and refrigerate overnight), or blending it into smoothies for a probiotic boost.

### Is sake kasu alcoholic?
Fresh sake kasu contains residual alcohol, typically around 8 percent. Most of this alcohol evaporates during cooking, making cooked kasu dishes effectively non-alcoholic. Uncooked preparations, such as kasu used in pickling, retain more alcohol.

### Where can I buy sake kasu in Nara?
*Sake kasu can be purchased at narazuke shops throughout Nara, at sake breweries that sell directly to visitors, and at local supermarkets — particularly during the pressing season from January through March when the freshest kasu is available. Narazuke specialty shops in Naramachi and near Nara Station carry both fresh kasu for cooking and prepared kasu products. For the best quality, seek out kasu from Nara Prefecture breweries, which often use locally grown rice and traditional brewing methods that produce kasu with superior flavor and aroma.*
