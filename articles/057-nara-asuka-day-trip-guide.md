# Day Trip to Asuka from Nara: Where Japanese Civilisation Began

Forty-five minutes south of Nara by train lies a village that, for all its rural quietness, contains the origins of Japanese civilisation. Asuka was the political and cultural centre of Japan before Nara became the capital — the place where Buddhism first took institutional root, where the first written laws were created, where the foundations of the Japanese state were laid. Today it is a landscape of rice paddies, ancient stone monuments, burial mounds, and a scattering of early temples, all set against a backdrop of gentle hills that has changed remarkably little in fourteen centuries.

For travellers who have experienced Nara's great temples and want to understand what came before them — the origins from which all that grandeur grew — Asuka is an essential and deeply rewarding excursion.

## Why Visit Asuka

### Historical Significance

The Asuka period (538–710 CE) was the era in which Japan transformed from a collection of clans into a centralised state. Buddhism arrived from the Korean peninsula. The first Buddhist temples were built. Written Chinese was adopted for government and literature. Diplomatic relations with China and Korea brought new technologies, artistic traditions, and political models. The Japan that emerged from the Asuka period — the Japan that would build Nara's great temples — was fundamentally shaped in this small valley.

### Atmosphere

Asuka offers something that Nara, for all its beauty, cannot: the experience of an ancient landscape that remains genuinely rural. The villages, rice fields, and wooded hills look much as they did when emperors walked here. There are no significant modern buildings, no commercial strips, no theme-park interpretations of history. The past is simply present, embedded in the landscape itself.

### Contrast

After the monumental scale of Todai-ji and the crowds of Nara Park, Asuka's intimate, human-scale archaeology provides a valuable counterpoint. The sites are small — a stone monument here, a burial mound there, a modest temple — but their cumulative effect, scattered across a landscape you traverse by bicycle, is profound.

## Getting to Asuka

**By train**: From Kintetsu Nara Station, take the Kintetsu Line south to Kashiharajingu-mae (approximately 35 minutes), then change to the Kintetsu Yoshino Line for two stops to Asuka Station (approximately 5 minutes). Total journey time: approximately 45 minutes.

**Cost**: Approximately ¥650 each way.

**First train**: Departures from around 6:00am allow an early start.

## Exploring Asuka

### By Bicycle

Cycling is the ideal way to explore Asuka. The terrain is gently rolling, the distances between sites are 1–3 km, and the experience of pedalling through rice paddies between ancient monuments is one of Japan's great travel pleasures.

**Bicycle rental**: Available at Asuka Station from multiple shops. Rates are typically ¥900–¥1,500 per day. Electric-assist bicycles are available for those who prefer easier pedalling (recommended for a full day's exploration). Rental shops open from approximately 9:00am.

**Routes**: Rental shops provide English-language maps showing suggested cycling circuits. The main circuit covers approximately 15 km and can be completed in 4–5 hours with stops.

### Key Sites

**Ishibutai Kofun** (Stone Stage Tomb): The most dramatic site in Asuka — a massive 7th-century burial mound whose earth covering has eroded to reveal enormous stone slabs forming a megalithic chamber. The largest stone weighs approximately 77 tonnes. The tomb is believed to belong to Soga no Umako, the powerful nobleman who championed Buddhism's adoption in Japan. You can enter the chamber — standing inside a 1,400-year-old burial vault, surrounded by stones that required engineering skill comparable to the construction of Stonehenge, is a powerful experience.

**Takamatsuzuka Kofun** (Takamatsuzuka Tomb): A circular burial mound from the late 7th or early 8th century, famous for its vivid wall paintings depicting court figures, celestial symbols, and the Chinese directional guardians. The original tomb is sealed for preservation, but an adjacent museum displays faithful reproductions that convey the extraordinary quality of the original artwork.

**Asuka-dera**: The oldest Buddhist temple in Japan, founded in 588 CE. The current buildings are modest — rebuilt after fire — but the temple houses the Asuka Great Buddha (Asuka Daibutsu), cast in 609 CE. This is the oldest surviving Buddha image in Japan — a bronze figure that predates Todai-ji's Great Buddha by nearly 150 years. It is smaller, simpler, and, in its archaic directness, deeply moving.

**Tachibana-dera**: A temple associated with Prince Shotoku, the regent who championed Buddhism and shaped early Japanese governance. The temple grounds are quiet and contemplative.

**Oka-dera** (Ryugai-ji): A hillside temple with fine views over the Asuka Valley. The climb is moderate and rewarding.

**Mysterious Stone Monuments**: Scattered through the village are enigmatic stone carvings whose purpose remains debated — the Monkey Stone (Saru-ishi), the Turtle Stone (Kame-ishi), and the Sake-brewing Stone (Sakafune-ishi). These objects, carved with channels and basins, may have served astronomical, ritual, or hydraulic functions. Their mystery is part of Asuka's appeal — not everything here is explained.

**Itabuki Palace Site**: The excavated remains of a 7th-century imperial palace. Open fields marked with posts indicate the building footprints. The scale is modest compared to Nara's Heijo Palace, but this is where the Taika Reforms of 645 CE — the political revolution that created centralised government in Japan — were conceived.

## A Suggested Itinerary

**9:30am**: Arrive at Asuka Station. Rent bicycles.
**10:00am**: Cycle to Asuka-dera. See the Great Buddha.
**10:45am**: Continue to the Itabuki Palace site and surrounding monuments.
**11:30am**: Cycle to Ishibutai Kofun. Explore the burial chamber.
**12:30pm**: Lunch at one of the small restaurants near Ishibutai (local specialities include asuka nabe, a milk-based hot pot unique to the region).
**1:30pm**: Cycle to Takamatsuzuka Kofun and museum.
**2:30pm**: Visit Oka-dera for hillside views.
**3:30pm**: Explore stone monuments and any sites of interest along the return route.
**4:30pm**: Return bicycles. Train back to Nara.

This itinerary is flexible — Asuka rewards unhurried exploration, and the bicycle allows spontaneous detours to anything that catches your eye.

## Practical Information

**Season**: Asuka is beautiful year-round. Spring (cherry blossoms among burial mounds), autumn (rice harvest and foliage), and winter (stark, atmospheric landscapes) are particularly fine.

**Facilities**: Small restaurants and convenience stores are available near Asuka Station and Ishibutai. Toilets are available at major sites. Bring water, particularly in summer.

**Entrance fees**: Most outdoor sites are free. Ishibutai Kofun: ¥300. Takamatsuzuka Museum: ¥300. Temples: small donations appreciated.

**Language**: English signage at major sites is improving but not comprehensive. The cycling map from the rental shop is usually sufficient for navigation.

**Fitness**: The cycling is gentle — Asuka is a valley with mild gradients. Electric-assist bicycles eliminate any physical challenge. Walking the full circuit is possible but time-consuming (allow a full day).

Travellers staying in Naramachi — at properties such as Kanoya — can reach Asuka Station within an hour of leaving their accommodation, making this an easy and deeply rewarding day excursion that reveals the origins of everything they have seen in Nara.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should I spend in Asuka?

A half day (4–5 hours) covers the main sites comfortably. A full day allows more leisurely exploration and lunch.

### Is Asuka suitable for children?

Yes. Cycling through rice paddies, exploring stone chambers, and hunting for mysterious stone carvings appeal to children's sense of adventure.

### Can I visit Asuka without cycling?

Yes, though it requires more time. Local buses connect some sites, and walking between them is possible if you focus on a smaller area.

### Is Asuka crowded?

Rarely, except during cherry blossom season (late March–early April) and autumn foliage (November). On most days, you will have the sites largely to yourself.
