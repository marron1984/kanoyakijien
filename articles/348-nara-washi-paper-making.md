# Japanese Paper Making in Nara: Washi Craft Experiences

Japanese handmade paper — washi — is one of the country's most remarkable traditional crafts, and one that visitors can experience firsthand in the Nara area. UNESCO inscribed washi on its Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2014, recognising not just the paper itself but the entire system of knowledge, technique, and cultural practice that sustains its production. For visitors to Nara, a washi-making workshop offers an encounter with a material so refined that it has been used continuously for over 1,300 years in applications ranging from temple scrolls to sliding doors, from calligraphy to clothing.

The Nara region has a specific and distinguished connection to washi through Yoshino washi — a tradition centred in the mountainous areas south of the city. This paper, made from the inner bark of the kozo (mulberry) tree using the pure waters of the Yoshino River, is renowned for its strength, translucency, and beauty. Making a sheet of washi by hand — dipping a screen into a vat of suspended fibres, feeling the water drain away, and lifting a sheet of paper that is simultaneously fragile and strong — is one of the most satisfying craft experiences available in Japan.

## Understanding Washi

### What Makes Washi Different

Washi differs fundamentally from Western machine-made paper. The distinction begins with the raw material: where Western paper is made from wood pulp (short, broken fibres that produce a relatively weak sheet), washi is made from the long, flexible fibres of plants such as kozo (mulberry), mitsumata, and gampi. These fibres interlock to produce paper that is:

- **Extraordinarily strong**: A sheet of well-made kozo washi is stronger than a sheet of machine-made paper many times its weight.
- **Translucent**: The long fibres create a paper that filters light beautifully, which is why washi is used in shoji screens and lanterns.
- **Long-lasting**: Washi documents from the 8th century survive in excellent condition. The Shoso-in at Todai-ji contains washi manuscripts over 1,200 years old.
- **Textured**: Each sheet carries the texture of the screen on which it was formed and the variations inherent in handwork. No two sheets are identical.

### The Production Process

Traditional washi production follows a seasonal rhythm:

1. **Harvesting** (winter): Kozo branches are cut in late autumn or winter, when the bark is richest in fibre.
2. **Steaming and stripping**: The branches are steamed to loosen the bark, which is then stripped by hand.
3. **Cleaning**: The outer bark is removed, leaving only the white inner bark (shirokawa). This is rinsed repeatedly in cold running water.
4. **Cooking**: The bark is boiled in an alkaline solution (traditionally wood ash lye) to break down non-cellulose materials.
5. **Beating**: The softened fibres are beaten by hand or with wooden mallets until they separate into individual strands.
6. **Sheet forming**: The fibres are suspended in water with a natural mucilage (neri) derived from the tororo-aoi plant. A screen (su) mounted in a wooden frame (keta) is dipped into the vat, and the papermaker uses a rhythmic rocking motion (nagashi-zuki) to distribute the fibres evenly across the screen.
7. **Pressing and drying**: The wet sheets are stacked, pressed to remove excess water, then dried individually on wooden boards in the sun or on heated metal plates.

The sheet-forming step — nagashi-zuki — is the heart of the craft and the aspect most commonly experienced in visitor workshops.

## Washi Traditions Near Nara

### Yoshino Washi

The Yoshino area, located in the mountains south of Nara city, has produced washi for centuries. Yoshino washi is particularly prized for its use in:

- **Urushi lacquerware**: Thin Yoshino washi is used as a reinforcing layer in the production of lacquered objects.
- **Calligraphy and painting**: The paper's smooth surface and absorbency make it excellent for brush work.
- **Conservation**: Museum conservators worldwide use Yoshino washi for repairing damaged documents and artworks, testament to its quality and reliability.

The Yoshino washi tradition has diminished in scale — only a few active papermakers remain — but those who continue the practice maintain the highest standards of technique and material quality.

### Kuzu and Paper

Nara's connection to kuzu (arrowroot) extends beyond cuisine into papermaking. Kuzu fibres have been used historically in the production of certain types of washi, creating a paper with distinctive properties. While kuzu-fibre paper is rare today, it represents another thread in Nara's rich textile and material culture.

## Workshop Experiences

### Basic Sheet-Forming Workshops

The most common visitor experience introduces the fundamental technique of nagashi-zuki — the rhythmic dipping and rocking motion that forms a sheet of paper. These workshops typically last 60 to 90 minutes and allow participants to make two to four sheets of paper.

The experience follows this pattern:

1. **Introduction**: The instructor explains the materials, tools, and process.
2. **Demonstration**: A skilled papermaker demonstrates the sheet-forming technique, showing the controlled rocking motion that distributes fibres evenly.
3. **Practice**: Participants form their own sheets, with guidance from the instructor. The motion is deceptively simple — achieving an even sheet requires a feel for the weight and flow of the fibre suspension that develops only with practice.
4. **Decoration** (optional): Many workshops allow participants to add botanical materials — pressed flowers, leaves, coloured fibres — to their sheets while still wet.
5. **Drying**: Sheets are pressed and placed on drying boards. In some workshops, participants can take their paper home the same day; others ship the dried paper later.

### Extended Workshops

Some providers offer longer experiences that cover additional stages of the process — preparing the fibre, beating the pulp, or experimenting with different sheet-forming techniques. These sessions, typically lasting two to four hours, provide a deeper understanding of the craft and produce more refined results.

### Combined Cultural Experiences

Washi-making pairs naturally with other traditional arts. Some providers offer combined workshops:
- **Washi + calligraphy**: Make your own paper, then practice brush writing on it.
- **Washi + bookbinding**: Form sheets and then bind them into a simple Japanese-style book (watoji-toji).
- **Washi + printmaking**: Create paper and use it for woodblock printing.

These combinations extend the experience meaningfully and produce more substantial keepsakes.

## Where to Experience Washi-Making

### Yoshino Area Workshops

The most historically authentic washi experiences are found in the Yoshino mountains, approximately 90 minutes south of Nara city by train and bus. Several small operations here accept visitors by appointment, offering workshops in settings surrounded by the forests and rivers that supply their raw materials.

Visiting Yoshino for washi-making can be combined with other experiences in the area — the region is famous for cherry blossoms in spring, autumn foliage, and mountain temple pilgrimage routes.

### Central Nara Experiences

Cultural experience providers in central Nara offer washi-making workshops that are more convenient for visitors with limited time. These typically operate from studios or cultural facilities in the Naramachi area and use materials sourced from traditional suppliers.

While the setting lacks the immersive quality of a mountain workshop, the instruction quality can be excellent, and the convenience factor is significant for visitors fitting multiple cultural experiences into a stay.

### Museum and Gallery Connections

The Nara Prefecture Complex of Man'yo Culture and other cultural institutions occasionally host paper-related workshops and exhibitions. These provide historical context alongside hands-on experience.

Guests at accommodation like Kanoya can often arrange washi experiences through their hosts, who connect visitors with artisans and workshops that match their interests and schedule. This personal facilitation is particularly valuable for reaching Yoshino-area workshops that may not have English-language booking systems.

## Practical Information

**Cost**: Basic workshops cost ¥2,000–¥4,000 per person. Extended workshops and combined experiences cost ¥5,000–¥10,000.

**Duration**: 60 to 90 minutes for basic workshops; 2 to 4 hours for extended sessions.

**Booking**: Advance reservation is required, typically one to two weeks ahead. Yoshino workshops may require more advance notice.

**What to wear**: Comfortable clothing that can get wet. The process involves working with water, and splashing is common. Roll up sleeves or wear short sleeves.

**What you take home**: Two to four sheets of handmade washi, either dried on-site or shipped to your address. Decorated sheets (with pressed flowers or coloured fibres) are particularly beautiful.

**Language**: Most visitor workshops offer instruction in English or rely on demonstration. The physical, visual nature of the process makes it accessible regardless of language.

**Getting to Yoshino**: From Kintetsu Nara Station, take the Kintetsu line south to Yoshino (approximately 90 minutes with one transfer). Local bus connections to specific workshops vary; confirm transport details when booking.

## Buying and Using Washi

### Where to Buy

- **Naramachi paper shops**: Several specialty shops in the historic district sell washi in various weights, colours, and sizes.
- **Stationery shops**: High-quality washi notebooks, envelopes, and cards are available at stationery stores near Kintetsu Nara Station.
- **Workshop studios**: Most workshops sell additional sheets and washi products.

### Uses at Home

Washi is remarkably versatile:
- Wrapping gifts (the paper's texture and strength are ideal)
- Book covering and bookbinding
- Lamp shades (the translucency creates beautiful diffused light)
- Calligraphy and watercolour painting
- Collage and mixed-media art

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need any experience to make washi?

No. Workshops are designed for complete beginners. The instructor will demonstrate the technique and guide you through the process.

### Can children participate in washi-making?

Yes. The process is tactile, visual, and satisfying for children. Most workshops welcome participants aged five and above with parental assistance.

### How long does washi paper last?

Properly stored, washi can last for centuries. The Shoso-in at Todai-ji preserves washi documents over 1,200 years old in excellent condition. Your handmade sheets, while perhaps not museum quality, will outlast any machine-made paper.

### Is Yoshino washi the same as other types of Japanese washi?

Each washi-producing region has its own traditions, materials, and characteristics. Yoshino washi is particularly known for its fineness and strength, qualities that have made it prized for lacquerware, conservation, and calligraphy. Other famous traditions include Echizen washi (Fukui), Mino washi (Gifu), and Tosa washi (Kochi).
