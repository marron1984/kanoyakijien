# The Nara Period (710–794): A Visitor's Guide to Japan's Foundational Era

The Nara period — the 84 years during which Heijo-kyo served as Japan's capital — is one of the most consequential eras in Japanese history. In less than a century, Japan established its first permanent capital, produced its first great literature, created its finest Buddhist art, built the world's largest bronze statue, codified its legal system, and transformed from a loosely organised confederation of clans into a centralised state modelled on the Tang-dynasty Chinese empire.

Everything you see in Nara today — the temples, the palace site, the sculptural treasures, the deer protected as sacred messengers — originates in or is directly connected to this brief, extraordinary period. Understanding the Nara period is not merely historical interest — it is the key that unlocks the meaning of every temple visit, every sculpture encounter, and every walk through the ancient city.

## The Founding

### Why Here

In 710, Empress Genmei ordered the construction of a new capital at Heijo-kyo — the site that would become modern Nara. The location was chosen for practical and geomantic reasons: a broad, fertile basin protected by mountains on three sides, with reliable water sources and good agricultural land. Chinese geomantic principles (fengshui) informed the specific siting: mountains to the north (protection), water to the south (prosperity), and a flat plain for the city grid.

The decision to establish a permanent capital — breaking from the previous practice of moving the capital with each new emperor — reflected a fundamental shift in how Japan conceived of governance. A permanent capital signified a permanent state: a government that would endure beyond any individual ruler, housed in buildings that would stand for generations.

### The City

Heijo-kyo was designed as a grid city, modelled on the Tang-dynasty capital of Chang'an:

- **Size**: Approximately 4.8 by 4.3 kilometres
- **Grid**: Streets running north-south and east-west divided the city into regular blocks (cho)
- **Palace**: At the city's northern centre, covering approximately 120 hectares
- **Suzaku-oji**: The broad central avenue (approximately 70 metres wide) running from the palace to the southern gate
- **Population**: Approximately 100,000–200,000 at its peak

The city was a statement of ambition: Japan was building not merely a capital but a declaration that it could create urban civilisation at the same scale and complexity as the continent's greatest empires.

## Political Achievement

### The Ritsuryo System

The Nara period saw the full implementation of the ritsuryo system — a comprehensive legal and administrative code adapted from Tang-dynasty China. The code established:

- A centralised bureaucracy with defined ministries and offices
- A tax system (grain, labour, and textile taxes from the provinces)
- A land distribution system (kubunden — the allocation of farmland to adult males)
- A provincial administration system (kokufu — provincial capitals with appointed governors)
- A military organisation
- Criminal and civil law codes

This was Japan's first systematic attempt to govern as a unified nation rather than a collection of clan territories. The system was imperfect — powerful clans, particularly the Fujiwara, manipulated it for advantage — but it established the framework of central governance that, in evolving forms, would persist for centuries.

### The Fujiwara

The Fujiwara clan dominated Nara-period politics through strategic marriage alliances with the imperial family. Fujiwara daughters became empresses; Fujiwara men occupied the highest government positions. The clan's temple, Kofuku-ji, and their tutelary shrine, Kasuga Taisha, remain two of Nara's most important sites — physical evidence of the family's political and cultural influence.

### Emperor Shomu

The most consequential ruler of the period, Emperor Shomu (r. 724–749) used Buddhism as an instrument of national unity and spiritual protection. His construction of Todai-ji and the Great Buddha, his establishment of the provincial temple system, and his patronage of the arts created the cultural achievements that define the Nara period.

## Cultural Achievement

### Literature

The Nara period produced Japan's foundational literary works:

**Kojiki (712)**: The "Record of Ancient Matters" — Japan's oldest surviving book, containing creation myths, genealogies of the imperial family, and narratives of the gods. The text establishes the mythological framework of Japanese identity.

**Nihon Shoki (720)**: The "Chronicles of Japan" — a more systematic and Chinese-influenced historical record, providing the official narrative of Japanese history from creation to the 7th century.

**Manyoshu (compiled c. 759)**: The "Collection of Ten Thousand Leaves" — Japan's oldest and greatest poetry anthology, containing approximately 4,500 poems by authors ranging from emperors to soldiers to anonymous commoners. The Manyoshu is written in man'yogana — Chinese characters used phonetically to represent Japanese words — and its poetry expresses the full range of human emotion: love, grief, homesickness, the beauty of nature, the sorrow of separation.

The Manyoshu's connections to Nara are direct: many poems reference the city, its landscape, its people, and its seasonal beauty. Reading Manyoshu poems while in Nara — about deer, about autumn in the park, about the mountains visible from the capital — collapses the distance between the 8th century and the present.

### Art and Architecture

The Nara period represents the peak of Japanese Buddhist art's first great flowering:

**Sculpture**: Bronze, dry lacquer, clay, and wood sculptures of unprecedented quality. The Great Buddha, the Ashura, the Yakushi Trinity, the guardian figures at Todai-ji and Shin-Yakushi-ji — these works define the standard against which all subsequent Japanese sculpture is measured.

**Architecture**: Temple buildings of grand scale and refined proportion. Toshodai-ji's main hall — the only original Nara-period main hall surviving — demonstrates the architectural vocabulary of the era: Tang-Chinese influences adapted to Japanese materials and aesthetic sensibilities.

**Decorative arts**: Textiles, metalwork, lacquerware, and glass of extraordinary quality — many preserved in the Shoso-in treasury, providing the most complete picture of 8th-century material culture available anywhere in the world.

### Religion

The Nara period established Buddhism as Japan's primary intellectual and cultural institution:

**Six Schools**: Six Buddhist philosophical schools (Nanto Rokushu) were officially recognised and supported — each based at a major Nara temple. These schools represented different interpretations of Buddhist doctrine, and their presence made Nara a centre of religious scholarship.

**State Buddhism**: The government supported Buddhism as an instrument of national protection. The Great Buddha was explicitly commissioned to protect the nation from plague and disaster. The provincial temple system extended Buddhist presence to every province.

**Shinto Coexistence**: The indigenous Shinto tradition continued alongside Buddhism — the two were not competing religions but complementary systems. Kasuga Taisha's protection of the capital and the deer as divine messengers demonstrate this coexistence.

## International Connections

### The Tang Dynasty Relationship

The Nara period was the peak of Japanese engagement with Tang-dynasty China:

**Embassies**: Japan sent multiple diplomatic missions (kentoshi) to the Tang court. These missions lasted years and involved dangerous sea crossings. The diplomats, monks, and scholars who participated brought back knowledge, texts, art, technologies, and ideas that shaped every aspect of Nara-period civilisation.

**Cultural imports**: Writing systems, legal codes, Buddhist doctrines, architectural forms, sculptural techniques, musical traditions, medical knowledge, and administrative practices — all were absorbed from China and adapted to Japanese conditions.

**The Silk Road connection**: Through China, Japan received influences from across Asia — Persian glass, Central Asian textiles, Indian Buddhist iconography, Greco-Buddhist sculptural motifs. The Shoso-in's treasures document these connections physically.

### Ganjin

The Chinese monk Ganjin's arrival in 753, after twelve years of failed attempts, brought authentic Buddhist ordination practice to Japan and demonstrated the extraordinary commitment to knowledge transmission that characterised the era.

## The End of the Period

### Why the Capital Moved

In 784, Emperor Kanmu moved the capital from Heijo-kyo, first briefly to Nagaoka-kyo, then permanently to Heian-kyo (Kyoto) in 794. The reasons were multiple:

- The powerful Nara Buddhist institutions had accumulated political influence that the court wished to escape
- The Fujiwara clan's dominance, intertwined with the Nara temples, constrained imperial independence
- Heian-kyo offered a fresh start, with new temples and new institutional relationships

### What Remained

When the court departed, Nara's temples remained. Unlike the palace (which was dismantled), the great temples — Todai-ji, Kofuku-ji, Kasuga Taisha, Yakushi-ji, Toshodai-ji, and others — continued as active religious institutions. Their survival was ensured by their religious function, their landholdings, and the devotion of their communities.

It is this survival — the continuity of religious institutions through 1,200 years of political change — that gives modern Nara its extraordinary character. The city is not a reconstruction or an archaeological site — it is a living continuation of the Nara period, maintained by the same institutions that were founded in the 8th century.

## Seeing the Nara Period Today

For visitors, the Nara period is not abstract history — it is visible and tangible:

- **Todai-ji**: The Great Buddha, the Sangatsu-do sculptures, the Kaidan-in guardians — direct products of Shomu's vision
- **Kofuku-ji**: The Ashura and other Nara-period sculptures — the Fujiwara clan's artistic legacy
- **Yakushi-ji**: The Yakushi Trinity — the peak of Nara-period bronze
- **Toshodai-ji**: Ganjin's temple — the original main hall, the Tang-Chinese architectural influence
- **Heijo Palace**: The foundations and reconstructions of the imperial capital
- **Kasugayama Forest**: Protected since the Nara period — 1,180+ years of unbroken conservation
- **Shoso-in**: The treasury of Nara-period objects (Nara National Museum exhibition each autumn)

Properties like Kanoya in Naramachi provide accommodation in the landscape that the Nara period created — a stay that places guests in direct relationship with 1,300 years of continuous cultural history.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long was the Nara period?

84 years (710–794) — brief but extraordinarily consequential.

### What came before the Nara period?

The Asuka period (538–710) — the era of Buddhism's arrival and initial adoption, based at Asuka (south of Nara). Asuka is accessible as a day trip from Nara.

### What came after?

The Heian period (794–1185), based in Kyoto — the era of aristocratic court culture, the Tale of Genji, and the refinement of distinctively Japanese aesthetics.

### Why is the Nara period important?

It was Japan's foundational era — the period in which the country established centralised government, state Buddhism, its first great literature, and an artistic culture that influenced all subsequent Japanese civilisation.
