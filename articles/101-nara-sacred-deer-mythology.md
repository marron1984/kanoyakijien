# Nara's Sacred Deer: The Mythology Behind the Messengers of the Gods

The deer of Nara are not merely wildlife that happen to inhabit a city park. They are, according to Shinto belief, divine messengers — shinshi (神使) — of the deity enshrined at Kasuga Taisha. This status, maintained for over 1,300 years, has shaped the deer's relationship with the city, its people, and its visitors in ways that no other animal-human relationship in the world quite parallels. Understanding the mythology behind the deer transforms the experience of encountering them from a charming animal interaction into a meeting with living embodiments of the city's spiritual heritage.

## The Foundation Legend

### Takemikazuchi's Arrival

According to Shinto tradition, in 768 CE the deity Takemikazuchi no Mikoto was invited from the distant Kashima Shrine in eastern Japan (present-day Ibaraki Prefecture) to become the guardian deity of the newly established Kasuga Taisha in Nara. The god is said to have arrived riding on a white deer — a journey of hundreds of kilometres that carried divine protection to the new capital.

This founding legend established the deer as sacred intermediaries between the divine and human worlds. If the god chose a deer as his mount, the deer themselves must be worthy of reverence. From this moment, the deer of the Kasuga forest were declared divine messengers, and harming them became not merely a crime but a sacrilege.

### The Four Deities

Kasuga Taisha enshrines four deities, each associated with different aspects of protection and blessing:

- **Takemikazuchi no Mikoto**: The deity who arrived on the white deer, associated with martial prowess and protection
- **Futsunushi no Mikoto**: Associated with swordsmanship and order
- **Amenokoyane no Mikoto**: The ancestral deity of the Fujiwara clan, associated with wisdom and ritual
- **Himegami**: The consort deity, associated with prosperity and harmony

The deer serve as messengers for all four deities, giving them a breadth of sacred function that extends beyond any single spiritual role.

## Historical Protection

### Nara Period to Edo Period

The sacred status of the deer was enforced with remarkable consistency across centuries and political systems:

**Nara period (710–784 CE)**: The deer's protected status was established alongside the founding of Kasuga Taisha. As the shrine served the Fujiwara clan — the most powerful political family in Japan — the deer enjoyed the protection of the most influential patrons in the country.

**Medieval period**: Despite civil wars, political upheaval, and the decline of central authority, the deer's protected status in Nara was maintained by local custom and shrine authority.

**Edo period (1600–1868)**: Under the Tokugawa shogunate, killing a deer in Nara was punishable by death. Historical records document executions carried out for this offence. A famous story tells of a boy executed for accidentally killing a deer — the severity of the punishment reflecting the absolute nature of the prohibition.

The deer's protection was not merely symbolic. It was enforced by real power, with real consequences, for over a millennium.

### Modern Protection

In 1957, the deer of Nara were designated a Natural Monument of Japan. They are protected by both national wildlife laws and specific municipal ordinances. The Nara Deer Preservation Foundation (founded 1890) manages the deer population's health and welfare, including:

- Annual health surveys
- Veterinary care for injured deer
- Supplementary winter feeding
- The annual antler-cutting ceremony (Shika-no-Tsunokiri)
- Education programmes about deer behaviour and welfare

The current population is approximately 1,200 deer in the park area — a number that has remained relatively stable through active management.

## The Deer in Japanese Art and Literature

### Visual Arts

The image of the deer — particularly the deer of Nara — appears throughout Japanese art history:

- **Kasuga Mandala paintings**: Medieval paintings depicting Kasuga Taisha invariably include deer, often shown in sacred contexts alongside deities
- **Kasuga Deer Mandala** (鹿曼荼羅): A specific genre of devotional art showing a deer carrying a sacred tree on its back, with Buddhist and Shinto deities above — one of the most distinctive images in Japanese religious art
- **Nara-e**: The deer motif in Akahada-yaki pottery and other Nara crafts directly references the sacred deer tradition
- **Screen paintings**: Edo-period screens depicting Nara prominently feature deer in the landscape

### Poetry

The Manyoshu, compiled during the Nara period, contains poems referencing the deer of the Kasuga hills — establishing a literary tradition that has continued for thirteen centuries. The autumn cry of the deer (shika no nakigoe) became one of Japanese poetry's standard seasonal references, evoking both the beauty of autumn and the poignancy of longing.

### Contemporary Culture

The deer remain central to Nara's identity. They appear on the city's official materials, in local business branding, in souvenir design, and in the daily conversation of residents. The relationship between city and deer is not a tourist attraction imposed on the city but an integral part of its character — as fundamental to Nara's identity as the Seine is to Paris or the canals to Venice.

## The Deer and Shinto Belief

### Shinshi: Divine Messengers

In Shinto theology, certain animals serve as shinshi — messengers or servants of particular deities. Foxes serve Inari shrines, monkeys serve certain mountain shrines, and deer serve Kasuga Taisha. The concept is not that the animals are gods themselves but that they are vehicles of divine communication — their presence signals the deity's attention, and their welfare reflects the health of the spiritual relationship between the shrine and the community.

This belief has practical implications. When you encounter a deer in Nara Park, Shinto tradition holds that you are in the presence of a being connected to the divine — not a pet, not a wild animal in the usual sense, but a creature with sacred function. The bow that Nara's deer famously perform (actually a feeding-related behaviour reinforced by cracker rewards) is popularly interpreted through this lens — the divine messengers greeting the visitor.

### The Sacred Forest

The deer's habitat — the Kasugayama Primeval Forest behind Kasuga Taisha — has been protected from logging since 841 CE specifically because it is the deer's home and the deity's domain. This makes it one of the oldest nature conservation measures in world history, predating the modern conservation movement by over a millennium. The forest's survival — ancient trees, unchanged ecology, pristine condition within a major city — is a direct consequence of the deer's sacred status.

## Encountering the Sacred

### For Visitors

Understanding the deer's sacred mythology does not require accepting Shinto belief. It requires recognising that for the people of Nara — and for the continuous cultural tradition that stretches back thirteen centuries — these are not ordinary animals. They are participants in a spiritual relationship between a community and its sacred landscape.

This recognition changes the quality of the encounter. Feeding a deer becomes something more than a tourist activity. Walking among them in the morning mist becomes something more than a pleasant stroll. The deer's presence — calm, ancient, unafraid — becomes a living expression of the city's most fundamental identity.

### Respectful Engagement

The deer's sacred status carries an ethical dimension for visitors:

- **Feed only shika-senbei**: These crackers are nutritionally appropriate. Human food is not.
- **Do not chase or harass**: These are protected animals with sacred status.
- **Give space to mothers with fawns and stags in rut**: Approach calmly and retreat if the animal shows stress.
- **Pick up litter**: Deer eat plastic and paper, which can be fatal. Keeping the park clean is a form of respect.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are the deer really sacred?

In Shinto belief, yes — they are divine messengers of Kasuga Taisha. In legal terms, they are a designated Natural Monument. In cultural terms, they are central to Nara's identity.

### How long have the deer been in Nara?

The deer population in the Kasuga area predates the founding of the capital. Their sacred status dates from 768 CE — over 1,250 years.

### Are the deer wild?

The deer are wild in the sense that they are not domesticated, owned, or confined. They roam freely and breed naturally. However, their long coexistence with humans has made them unusually comfortable with human presence.

### Was it really a death penalty to kill a deer?

During the Edo period, yes. Historical records document executions for killing Nara's deer. The penalty reflected the absolute nature of their sacred protection.
