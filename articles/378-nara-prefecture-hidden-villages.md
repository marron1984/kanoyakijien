# Nara Prefecture's Hidden Villages: Rural Japan Off the Tourist Trail

Most visitors to Nara Prefecture never leave the northern basin. They see Todai-ji, feed the deer, walk through Naramachi, and perhaps venture south to Asuka or Yoshino. Yet the prefecture extends deep into the mountainous interior of the Kii Peninsula, encompassing a vast, sparsely populated landscape of forested gorges, highland plateaus, hidden valleys, and villages that have maintained their traditional character largely because the modern world has had little reason to disturb them. This is rural Japan at its most authentic — not curated, not commodified, and not easy to reach, but profoundly rewarding for travellers willing to make the effort.

Nara Prefecture's southern and eastern mountains cover an area larger than some entire prefectures, yet they receive a fraction of the tourism that flows through the Kansai region. For visitors based in Nara city, these hidden villages offer day trips and overnight excursions that reveal a dimension of Japan invisible from the Shinkansen window.

## Totsukawa Village

Totsukawa is Japan's largest village by area — covering over 672 square kilometres — yet its population numbers barely three thousand. Located in the southernmost reaches of Nara Prefecture, wedged between the mountains of the Kii Peninsula, Totsukawa is a place of steep-sided valleys, suspension bridges, thermal springs, and a fierce tradition of independence that dates back centuries.

The village is famous for its onsen. Totsukawa Onsen, Kamiyu Onsen, and Tosenji Onsen are all fed by natural hot springs that emerge from the volcanic geology of the region. Tosenji Onsen, in particular, is celebrated for its open-air baths overlooking the river gorge — an experience of soaking in mineral-rich water while gazing into a forested ravine that feels timelessly Japanese.

Totsukawa's suspension bridges are another distinctive feature. The village maintains several pedestrian suspension bridges spanning its deep gorges, the most famous being the Tanize Suspension Bridge, which stretches fifty-four metres above the Totsukawa River at a length of nearly three hundred metres. Crossing it is not for the faint-hearted, but the views are unforgettable.

Getting to Totsukawa requires commitment. By car from Nara, the journey takes approximately three hours via Route 169 and Route 168 through the mountains. Public buses run from Gojo (reachable by JR from Nara in about an hour) to Totsukawa, but the bus ride itself takes over three hours. An overnight stay — the village has several onsen ryokan and minshuku — is strongly recommended.

## Soni Plateau

In the northeastern corner of Nara Prefecture, the Soni Plateau (Soni Kogen) offers a landscape utterly unlike the rest of the region. At approximately 700 metres elevation, this broad, undulating grassland is covered in Japanese pampas grass (susuki) that turns golden in autumn, creating vast, wave-like expanses that ripple in the mountain breeze. The effect, particularly in late October and November when the grass catches the low autumn light, is mesmerising.

Soni is one of the few places in the Kansai region where the landscape opens up to such wide horizons. The plateau is crisscrossed by walking trails that wind through the grass and offer panoramic views of the surrounding mountains. The Okameyama trail, a gentle loop of about two hours, is the most popular route and provides the best viewpoints.

The village below the plateau maintains a quiet, agricultural character. Several farms offer experiences such as rice planting, vegetable harvesting, and traditional craft workshops. The Soni area is also known for its sake rice cultivation and its connection to the Muro-ji temple tradition in nearby Uda.

Getting to Soni from Nara takes approximately ninety minutes by car. Public transport involves taking the Kintetsu Line to Nabari (Mie Prefecture) and then a local bus, making a car the significantly more practical option.

## Kurotaki Village

Tucked into a narrow valley in the mountains south of Yoshino, Kurotaki is a village of approximately six hundred residents that has attracted attention in recent years for an unusual demographic distinction: it has one of the highest concentrations of non-Japanese residents in rural Japan. A community of practitioners drawn to the area's Shugendo (mountain asceticism) traditions has settled here, joining the existing population in maintaining the village's temples, forest management practices, and communal rituals.

Kurotaki's appeal lies in its combination of spiritual intensity and rural simplicity. The village is surrounded by forests managed using traditional techniques, and the sound of mountain water accompanies every walk. The Kurotaki River, running through the valley centre, is renowned for its clarity. The village's temples, small and unpretentious, maintain connections to the Omine pilgrimage route and the broader Shugendo tradition.

Reaching Kurotaki requires a car (about ninety minutes from Nara) or a combination of trains and infrequent buses via Shimoichiguchi Station. Like most of these hidden villages, it rewards visitors who have time and flexibility.

## Higashi-Yoshino Village

East of Yoshino, along the upper reaches of the Takami River, Higashi-Yoshino is a village of approximately 1,500 people known for its papermaking tradition. Yoshino washi (handmade paper) has been produced here for over 1,300 years, and several workshops continue the craft, using hand-beaten kozo (mulberry bark) fibre and cold mountain water in a process that has changed little since the Nara period.

Visitors can watch craftsmen at work and, in some workshops, try making paper themselves. The resulting sheets — translucent, strong, and subtly textured — are used in calligraphy, bookbinding, sliding door panels, and conservation work at museums worldwide. Understanding that this delicate material begins in a mountain village with mulberry trees and river water adds a dimension to every encounter with traditional Japanese paper culture.

The village's forests are also notable. Yoshino sugi (cedar) and hinoki (cypress), cultivated using methods developed over five centuries, are among the most prized timber varieties in Japan, used in temple construction, tea houses, and fine woodworking.

## Tsukigase and the Plum Groves

Northeast of Nara city, the small community of Tsukigase (now part of Nara city following a merger, but retaining its village character) is famous for its plum groves. Over 10,000 plum trees line the Nabari River valley, and their blossoming in late February and early March creates one of the earliest and most beautiful flower-viewing experiences in the Kansai region. The Tsukigase Bairin (plum grove) has been celebrated in Japanese poetry since the Edo period, and the combination of white and pink blossoms reflected in the river, set against the still-winter mountains, is genuinely enchanting.

Tsukigase is reachable in about forty-five minutes by car from Nara, or by bus from Kintetsu Nara Station during the plum blossom season. Outside the flowering period, the village is quiet but pleasant for walks along the river.

## Mitsue Village

On the border with Mie Prefecture, Mitsue Village centres on the worship of Mitsue Shrine, dedicated to a local deity associated with water and agriculture. The village is surrounded by primeval forest, and several hiking trails lead into the mountains. The Mitsue area is known for its mushroom cultivation and mountain vegetable cuisine, and the village onsen (Himeshi no Yu) offers hot spring bathing in a peaceful mountain setting.

## Practical Considerations for Rural Exploration

**Transport**: A rental car is effectively essential for most of these villages. Public transport exists but is infrequent, sometimes limited to two or three buses per day. Nara city has several car rental agencies near both JR and Kintetsu stations.

**Language**: English signage and English-speaking staff are rare in these villages. Basic Japanese phrases, a translation app, and a spirit of patient goodwill will serve you well.

**Accommodation**: Most villages offer minshuku (family-run guesthouses), and some have small ryokan or onsen facilities. Advance reservation is essential, particularly in smaller communities.

**Seasons**: Each village has its optimal season. Soni is best in autumn, Tsukigase in late winter, and the mountain villages (Totsukawa, Kurotaki, Higashi-Yoshino) are most accessible from spring through autumn.

**Etiquette**: These are living communities, not tourist attractions. Visitors are welcomed but should be respectful of private property, local customs, and the quiet rhythms of village life.

## The Value of Contrast

Part of the pleasure of exploring Nara Prefecture's hidden villages is the return to Nara city itself. After a day or two in the mountains — the narrow roads, the limited dining options, the sometimes challenging communication — the comforts of the city feel newly appreciated. Guests at Kanoya ryokan, returning from the remote reaches of the prefecture, often report that the contrast intensifies both experiences: the wildness of the mountains makes the ryokan's refinement more vivid, and the memory of rural simplicity enriches the subsequent days of temple-visiting and cultural exploration.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the best hidden villages to visit in Nara Prefecture?

Totsukawa Village (onsen and suspension bridges), Soni Plateau (autumn pampas grass), Higashi-Yoshino (traditional papermaking), and Kurotaki (mountain asceticism traditions) are among the most rewarding destinations for travellers seeking rural Japan off the tourist trail.

### Do I need a car to visit these villages?

A rental car is strongly recommended for most of these destinations. Public transport exists but is infrequent, and a car provides the flexibility needed to explore remote areas comfortably.

### Is it possible to visit these villages as day trips from Nara?

Soni Plateau and Tsukigase are accessible as day trips (sixty to ninety minutes by car). More distant villages like Totsukawa require three or more hours of driving each way and are better suited to overnight visits.

### What is Soni Plateau known for?

Soni Plateau, in northeastern Nara Prefecture, is a highland grassland famous for its vast fields of Japanese pampas grass (susuki) that turn golden in autumn, creating one of the most distinctive landscapes in the Kansai region.
