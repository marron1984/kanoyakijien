# New Year in Nara: Hatsumode, Temple Bells, and Celebrating Shogatsu

New Year (Shogatsu) is Japan's most important holiday — a period of renewal, reflection, and ritual that transforms the country's character more profoundly than any other celebration. For three days (and in practice, the entire first week of January), Japan slows, gathers, and marks the transition from old to new with a combination of shrine visits, temple rituals, family meals, and a collective sense of beginning that is both solemn and joyful.

In Nara, New Year carries additional weight. The ancient capital's temples and shrines — institutions that have been marking the turning of the year for over a millennium — conduct New Year ceremonies with a depth of tradition that newer cities cannot match. The joya-no-kane (bells tolling 108 times at midnight on December 31) ring from temple bells that have been casting their sound across the Nara basin for centuries. The hatsumode (first shrine visit) at Kasuga Taisha draws pilgrims to a shrine that has welcomed the new year since its founding in 768.

For visitors who happen to be in Nara during the New Year period — or who plan their trip specifically for the occasion — the experience is one of the most atmospheric and culturally immersive available in Japan.

## The New Year Calendar

### December 31 (Omisoka)

**Evening**: The mood shifts. Shops close early. The streets quiet. The focus turns inward — families gather, meals are prepared, and the evening builds toward midnight.

**11:00pm onwards**: Visitors begin walking toward the temples and shrines for the midnight ceremonies. The park paths are lit. The atmosphere is expectant.

**Midnight**: The joya-no-kane — temple bells across Nara ring 108 times, each stroke representing one of the 108 earthly desires (bonno) that Buddhist teaching identifies as the source of human suffering. The tolling purifies the old year's accumulated spiritual burdens and welcomes the new year in a state of cleansed readiness.

**Where to hear the bells**: Todai-ji, Kofuku-ji, and other major temples toll their bells. Standing in the park and hearing the bells from multiple temples simultaneously — each at slightly different pitch, slightly different rhythm — is an extraordinary acoustic experience.

**At some temples**: Visitors can participate — queueing to strike the bell themselves. The physical act of pulling the wooden beam and releasing it against the bell's surface, feeling the vibration, hearing the deep resonance — is unforgettable.

### January 1 (Gantan)

**Hatsumode**: The first shrine visit of the new year. Millions of Japanese visit their local shrine on January 1 (or during the first three days) to pray for health, prosperity, and good fortune in the coming year.

**At Kasuga Taisha**: The shrine is thronged with visitors from early morning. The approach through the deer park, the lantern-lined path, the vermilion shrine buildings — all are enhanced by the New Year atmosphere: special decorations (shimekazari — straw rope ornaments), kadomatsu (pine and bamboo arrangements at entrances), and the collective energy of shared celebration.

**At the shrine**: The hatsumode ritual includes:
1. Purification at the water basin (temizu)
2. Approaching the main hall
3. Making an offering (saisen — coins into the offering box)
4. Praying for the new year
5. Purchasing omamori (protective charms) and omikuji (fortune slips) for the year ahead

### January 1–3 (San-ga-nichi)

The three-day core of the New Year celebration:

**Special events**: Temples and shrines conduct New Year ceremonies, special prayers, and cultural performances. Check with the Nara Visitor Centre for the specific schedule.

**Special food**: New Year cuisine (osechi ryori) — elaborately prepared dishes in multi-layered lacquer boxes, each food symbolising a wish for the new year (prawns for longevity, herring roe for fertility, sweet black beans for health).

**Atmosphere**: The city is quieter than usual — many shops and restaurants close for the first three days. The streets have a calm, reflective quality. The park is populated with families making hatsumode visits, children in their best clothes, and couples walking the lantern-lined paths.

## New Year at Nara's Temples and Shrines

### Kasuga Taisha

The primary hatsumode destination. The shrine's New Year programme includes:

- **Special prayers and rituals** conducted by the head priest
- **Miko (shrine maiden) dances** — ceremonial performances
- **Omamori and omikuji sales** — protective charms and fortune slips for the new year
- **The lantern approach** — the 3,000 stone and bronze lanterns, some lit for the New Year period

**Crowds**: Kasuga Taisha is very popular for hatsumode. January 1 morning sees the heaviest crowds. For a less congested experience, visit on January 2 or 3, or in the late afternoon of January 1.

### Todai-ji

**Gantan-e (January 1)**: The New Year ceremony at the Great Buddha Hall — monks chant before the Great Buddha, and the large front windows of the hall are opened, allowing the Buddha's face to be seen from outside. This opening occurs only on specific occasions (New Year, Obon in August, and during the Omizutori period) and is a significant moment.

### Kofuku-ji

New Year ceremonies at the pagoda and main halls. The five-storey pagoda against the winter sky — clear, cold, with the first light of the new year — is one of Nara's defining New Year images.

### Gangō-ji

A quieter hatsumode option — the Naramachi temple's New Year celebrations are less crowded than Kasuga Taisha's, providing a more intimate experience.

## New Year Food

### Osechi Ryori

The traditional New Year cuisine — prepared in advance (as cooking on New Year's Day is traditionally avoided) and served in tiered lacquer boxes (jubako):

- **Kuromame** (sweet black beans): Health
- **Kazunoko** (herring roe): Fertility and prosperity
- **Tazukuri** (candied sardines): Abundant harvest
- **Datemaki** (sweet rolled omelette): Scholarship
- **Kamaboko** (fish cake, red and white): Celebration
- **Kuri-kinton** (sweet chestnut paste): Financial fortune
- **Ebi** (prawns): Longevity (curved like an elderly person's back)

**Where to experience**: Ryokan that operate over the New Year period serve osechi ryori to guests — often as part of a special New Year menu that combines osechi with fresh seasonal dishes.

### Ozoni

A soup containing mochi (rice cake) — the essential New Year's Day dish. Regional variations exist; the Kansai (including Nara) style typically uses white miso broth with round mochi.

### Toshikoshi Soba

Eaten on December 31 — "year-crossing noodles" symbolising longevity and the cutting of the old year's misfortunes. Soba restaurants that remain open on New Year's Eve serve this traditional dish.

## Practical Considerations

### Accommodation

Book well in advance — the New Year period is one of Japan's peak domestic travel seasons. Ryokan rates may be higher than standard, and New Year meal packages may be required.

### What's Open

- **Temples and shrines**: Open and active — the busiest time of year for religious institutions
- **Convenience stores**: Open 24 hours, as always
- **Restaurants**: Many close December 31–January 2. Some reopen January 3. Ryokan meals and convenience stores fill the gap.
- **Shops**: Most close December 31–January 2 or 3. Major department stores may reopen January 2 with New Year sales (hatsu-uri).
- **Museums**: The Nara National Museum typically closes December 28–January 1 but check specific dates.

### Weather

Late December and early January in Nara: cold (0–8°C during the day, below freezing at night). Clear skies are common. Dress warmly for outdoor events — the midnight bell ceremony and hatsumode visits involve extended time outdoors in winter conditions.

### Transport

Trains run on holiday schedules — less frequent than weekdays but operational. The first trains of January 1 (hatsu-densha) are sometimes decorated or carry special significance.

## The New Year Atmosphere

New Year in Nara has a quality that no other season achieves: the combination of winter clarity (cold air, sharp light, bare branches), spiritual renewal (the bells, the prayers, the fresh start), and cultural depth (traditions maintained for centuries, performed in their original settings). The city feels both ancient and new — the oldest capital celebrating the newest year.

The deer in the park during New Year are particularly calm — the reduced visitor numbers and the winter quiet create conditions for the gentlest deer encounters of the year.

Properties like Kanoya in Naramachi provide the ideal New Year base — the warmth of the ryokan to return to between outdoor celebrations, the special New Year meals that mark the occasion, and the Naramachi atmosphere of quiet festivity.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Nara a good place for New Year?

Excellent — the ancient temples, historic shrine, and atmospheric setting make it one of the finest New Year destinations in Japan. Less crowded than Kyoto's major shrines.

### Do I need to be Buddhist or Shinto to participate?

No. New Year celebrations are cultural as much as religious. Visitors of all backgrounds are welcome at both temple and shrine events.

### Should I book a ryokan for New Year?

Strongly recommended — the special New Year meals, the warm base for midnight activities, and the traditional atmosphere are ideal. Book months in advance.

### What time should I arrive for the midnight bells?

Arrive at your chosen temple by 11:00–11:30pm. If you wish to strike the bell yourself, arrive earlier — queues can be long at popular temples.
