# Nara for History Buffs: A Chronological Walking Tour

Nara's appeal for the casually curious and the deeply devoted historian is the same: everything is still here. Unlike many ancient cities where development has buried the past, Nara has preserved its historical layers with remarkable fidelity. Within a day's walking, you can move from the ruins of an 8th-century imperial palace to temples built in the same century that still function today, through medieval merchant quarters and Edo-period streetscapes, into a modern city that remains quietly shaped by its ancient identity.

This walking tour is organised chronologically rather than geographically. It requires some back-and-forth across the city, and completing every stop in a single day would be exhausting. Consider it a menu rather than a mandate — select the periods that interest you most, or spread the full itinerary across two days. Those staying overnight in Nara are at an advantage here: an evening walk through Naramachi and an early morning at Heijo Palace are experiences that day-trippers from Kyoto or Osaka simply cannot replicate.

## Before Nara: The Kofun Period (3rd–7th Century)

### Unebi and the Burial Mounds

Nara Prefecture contains hundreds of kofun — keyhole-shaped burial mounds from Japan's proto-historical period. While the largest and most famous kofun are in Osaka (the Mozu-Furuichi cluster), several are accessible within Nara city or on short excursions.

The tomb traditionally attributed to Emperor Jimmu, Japan's mythological first emperor, is located near Unebi in southern Nara Prefecture. While the attribution is legendry rather than archaeology, the tomb and its surrounding parkland are maintained with imperial reverence and offer an atmospheric starting point for understanding the deep roots of Japanese rulership in the Yamato region — the plain on which Nara sits.

Within Nara city, the Saki Kofun Cluster north of Heijo Palace includes several large keyhole-shaped mounds visible from the road, though most imperial tombs cannot be entered. Even from outside, the scale communicates power — these were the tombs of rulers who preceded the historical emperors of the Nara Period.

## The Founding: Nara Period (710–784 CE)

### Heijo Palace Ruins

The chronological tour begins properly at Heijo-kyu, the site of the imperial palace established in 710. The reconstructed Suzaku Gate and Daigokuden (Great Audience Hall) give physical form to the ambition of the Nara state: a capital modelled on Tang Dynasty Chang'an, with a palace complex at its heart designed to project centralised authority.

Allow 90 minutes to two hours. The site is vast and flat — arrive early to avoid midday heat in summer. The on-site museum contextualises the palace within the broader story of Nara Period administration.

### Yakushi-ji

From Heijo Palace, head southwest to Yakushi-ji, one of the oldest temple complexes in Japan. Founded in 680 — predating the move to Nara — and relocated to its current site in 718, Yakushi-ji's East Pagoda is the sole surviving original structure from the Nara Period on this site. The three-storey pagoda (which appears to be six stories due to its decorative pent roofs) has stood for over 1,300 years and is considered one of the finest examples of early Japanese architecture.

The West Pagoda, reconstructed in 1981, provides an instructive contrast — its fresh vermillion paint and sharp lines show what the East Pagoda looked like when new, before centuries of weathering softened its appearance.

### Toshodai-ji

Ten minutes' walk north from Yakushi-ji, Toshodai-ji was founded in 759 by the Chinese monk Ganjin (Jianzhen), who endured five failed attempts to cross the sea from China to Japan, losing his eyesight in the process. The temple's Golden Hall (kondo) is the largest surviving Nara Period timber structure and a masterpiece of restrained elegance. The lacquer statue of Ganjin in the Founder's Hall is one of the most moving portrait sculptures in Japanese art.

### Todai-ji

Heading east across the city to Nara Park, Todai-ji represents the apex of Nara Period ambition. Founded in 752, the Great Buddha Hall (Daibutsuden) houses the 15-metre bronze Vairocana Buddha — a project so enormous it nearly bankrupted the state. The current hall, rebuilt in 1709, is itself a marvel, though it is actually smaller than the original.

Do not rush Todai-ji. The approach through the Nandaimon Gate — with its fierce 13th-century guardian statues by the sculptor Unkei — the scale of the hall itself, and the quiet chapels and sub-temples around the perimeter all reward slow attention.

### Kasuga Taisha

From Todai-ji, the path eastward through the primeval forest leads to Kasuga Taisha, founded in 768 as the shrine of the Fujiwara clan, the most powerful aristocratic family of the Nara Period. The shrine's 3,000 stone and bronze lanterns, donated over centuries, line the approach and fill the grounds. The architectural style — vivid vermillion paint, curved roofs, intimate scale — is distinctly different from the monumental Buddhist temples nearby.

## Medieval Nara (12th–16th Century)

### Kofuku-ji

Return to the western edge of Nara Park for Kofuku-ji, whose history spans the transition from ancient to medieval Japan. Originally the Fujiwara clan temple, Kofuku-ji was repeatedly destroyed and rebuilt — the current five-storey pagoda dates to 1426, making it a Muromachi Period reconstruction of a Nara Period original. The temple's collection of Buddhist sculpture, housed in the National Treasure Hall, includes the haunting three-headed Ashura statue from 734 — an 8th-century work that remains one of the most famous sculptures in Japan.

Kofuku-ji's warrior monks (sohei) were a significant military force in medieval Japan, and the temple's political influence persisted for centuries after the imperial court left Nara. The current grounds, overlooking Sarusawa Pond, hint at a complex that once rivalled Todai-ji in scale.

### Gangou-ji

South of Kofuku-ji, in the heart of what is now Naramachi, Gangou-ji preserves elements from the Asuka Period (6th–7th century) — roof tiles from the original Asuka-dera, Japan's first full-scale Buddhist temple, were reused when the temple was relocated to Nara in 718. The temple is small and easily overlooked, but for the historically attentive, it represents the oldest physical layer of Buddhism in Japan.

## Edo Period (1603–1868)

### Naramachi

The merchant quarter of Naramachi took its present form during the Edo Period, when Nara's political significance had long faded but its economic and cultural life continued. The narrow streets, machiya townhouses with their characteristic deep, narrow floor plans, and small neighbourhood temples create an atmosphere of domestic history quite different from the monumental scale of the temple circuit.

Walk along Naramachi's main streets and side alleys. Several machiya have been preserved as museums or public spaces, allowing you to enter and see the merchant-class lifestyle: the shop front facing the street, the living quarters behind, the storehouse (kura) at the rear, and the small courtyard garden that brought light into the deep interior.

The Naramachi Koshi-no-Ie and the Naramachi Mechanical Toy Museum offer accessible glimpses into Edo-period domestic life. The red cloth monkey charms (migawari-zaru) hanging from house eaves are a surviving Edo-period folk tradition — each monkey represents a household member, absorbing misfortune on their behalf.

## Meiji to Modern (1868–Present)

### Nara National Museum

Founded in 1895, the Nara National Museum represents the Meiji-era impulse to preserve and present Japan's cultural heritage through Western museum models. The original building, designed by Katayama Tokuma in a French Renaissance style, is itself an artefact of Japan's rapid Westernisation. Inside, the Buddhist art collection is among the finest in the world.

### Nara Hotel

The Nara Hotel, opened in 1909, is another Meiji-era landmark. Built to accommodate foreign dignitaries visiting Nara's historical sites, its Western-style architecture with Japanese decorative elements represents the cultural fusion of the period. Even if you are not staying there, the lobby and grounds are worth a brief visit.

### Modern Nara

Contemporary Nara is a city that has made a deliberate choice to preserve rather than develop. The low skyline, the absence of heavy industry near the historical core, and the continued presence of deer on city streets are all expressions of a civic identity rooted in heritage. Walking from the ancient palace ruins through the temple district and into the modern city, you experience a continuum rather than a rupture — each era visible, each layer accessible.

## Planning Your Walk

The full chronological route covers approximately 12 to 15 kilometres and takes a full day at a brisk pace, or two comfortable days with time to enter sites and museums. Comfortable walking shoes are essential. Bicycles can shorten transit between western Nara (Heijo Palace, Yakushi-ji, Toshodai-ji) and eastern Nara (Todai-ji, Kasuga Taisha, Naramachi).

For those staying at a centrally located accommodation such as Kanoya ryokan, the eastern Nara sites are within easy walking distance, and the western sites can be reached by a short bus or bicycle ride. Starting early and returning to the ryokan for a midday rest before an afternoon of further exploration is a civilised way to manage the distance.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I complete this walking tour in one day?

It is possible but demanding. The full route is 12 to 15 kilometres with significant time at each stop. Spreading it over two days — western Nara on one day, eastern Nara on the other — is more comfortable and allows deeper engagement.

### Which stops are most important for a half-day visit?

For a focused half-day, prioritise Todai-ji, Kasuga Taisha, and Naramachi. This covers the Nara Period's religious pinnacle, its Shinto heritage, and its Edo-period domestic character in a manageable loop.

### Do I need a guide for this tour?

A guide adds depth, particularly for the archaeological and architectural details. However, the route is well-signed and navigable independently. English signage exists at all major sites.

### Are the sites accessible for wheelchair users?

Most major temples and shrines have accessible paths, though some older buildings require navigating steps. Heijo Palace is entirely flat. Naramachi's narrow streets are walkable but uneven in places.

### What is the best season for this walk?

Autumn (November) offers comfortable temperatures and spectacular foliage. Spring (late March to April) provides cherry blossoms and mild weather. Winter is cold but uncrowded. Summer is hot and humid — start early and carry water.
