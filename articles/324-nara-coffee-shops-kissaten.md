# Nara's Best Coffee Shops and Kissaten: A Café Culture Guide

There is a particular kind of café that exists in Japanese cities — called a kissaten — that has no real equivalent anywhere else. It is not a coffee shop in the Western sense. It is not a place to work on a laptop or meet for a quick espresso. A kissaten is a space for sitting, for slowness, for the ritual of a carefully prepared cup of coffee served on a saucer with a small dish of cream, in an interior that has not changed in thirty or forty years.

Nara has both: the traditional kissaten, dark-wooded and unhurried, and the newer wave of specialty coffee roasters and contemporary cafés that have opened in renovated machiya and modern minimalist spaces. Together they form a café landscape that is remarkably rich for a city of Nara's size — a landscape shaped by the same values that define the city itself: respect for craft, appreciation for quiet, and an instinct for beauty in everyday things.

## The Kissaten Tradition

### What Is a Kissaten?

Kissaten (喫茶店) literally means "tea-drinking shop," though coffee has long been the primary draw. These establishments emerged in Japan during the early 20th century, peaked in popularity during the Showa era (1950s through 1980s), and have been gradually declining as chain coffee shops and specialty roasters have expanded.

What makes a kissaten distinctive is not just the coffee but the entire atmosphere. Key characteristics include:

- **Hand-dripped coffee**: Kissaten masters typically use nel drip (flannel filter) or paper pour-over methods, preparing each cup individually. The coffee is often a dark-roasted house blend — rich, slightly bitter, and full-bodied.
- **Showa-era interiors**: Dark wood panelling, leather or velvet seats, dim lighting, jazz or classical music playing at low volume. Many kissaten have not been redecorated since they opened.
- **Food menu**: Kissaten typically serve a limited food menu that might include Napolitan spaghetti (a ketchup-based pasta unique to Japan), sandwiches with the crusts removed, cream soda (melon soda with a scoop of vanilla ice cream), and hand-cut toast with butter.
- **Morning service (morning setto)**: Many kissaten offer a morning set between opening time and 10:00 or 11:00 — a cup of coffee with thick toast, a hard-boiled egg, and sometimes a small salad, all for the price of a coffee alone (typically 400 to 600 yen).
- **No Wi-Fi, no laptops**: This is implied rather than stated. Kissaten are spaces for reading a newspaper, having a quiet conversation, or simply sitting with a cup of coffee. The etiquette is understood by regular customers.

### Nara's Kissaten Culture

Nara's kissaten have a particular character shaped by the city's identity. They tend to be quieter and less eclectic than their Osaka counterparts, reflecting the city's contemplative atmosphere. Several have been operating for decades, maintained by ageing proprietors who roast their own beans and know their regular customers by name.

The areas around Kintetsu Nara Station, Sanjo-dori, and the edges of Naramachi contain the highest concentration of traditional kissaten. Some are hidden on second floors above shops, announced only by a small sign and a narrow staircase — the kind of places you would never find without knowing they exist.

**What to order**: The house blend (burendo) is the standard choice and will reveal the kissaten master's particular taste in coffee. If available, try the iced coffee (aisu kohii), which in a good kissaten is made by dripping hot coffee over ice in a single glass, producing a coffee of remarkable clarity and intensity.

**Pricing**: Coffee at a traditional kissaten typically costs 400 to 700 yen. The morning set — coffee plus toast and egg — is usually the best value at 500 to 700 yen.

## Third-Wave and Specialty Coffee in Nara

### The New Wave

Over the past decade, Nara has seen an influx of specialty coffee shops run by a new generation of roasters and baristas. These establishments represent a fundamentally different approach to coffee: single-origin beans, light to medium roasts, precise extraction methods (V60, Aeropress, Chemex), and a focus on flavour notes and traceability that would be familiar to specialty coffee enthusiasts worldwide.

What distinguishes Nara's specialty coffee scene from those in larger cities is the setting. Many of these new shops have opened in renovated machiya — traditional wooden townhouses in Naramachi and surrounding areas — creating spaces where contemporary coffee culture meets historical architecture. The combination is striking: a perfectly extracted Ethiopian pourover served on a ceramic plate made by a local potter, in a room with 150-year-old timber beams overhead and a garden visible through sliding doors.

### Naramachi's Coffee Corridor

Naramachi has become the epicentre of Nara's new coffee culture. Within a fifteen-minute walk, you can visit half a dozen specialty roasters and contemporary cafés, each with a distinct personality. Some focus exclusively on coffee; others combine coffee with gallery space, bookshops, or curated selections of local crafts.

The renovated machiya setting is not mere aesthetic choice — it reflects a deliberate philosophy shared by many of Nara's new coffee entrepreneurs: that serving excellent coffee is a way of participating in the city's tradition of craft and care, connecting the precision of modern extraction techniques to the broader Japanese commitment to shokunin (artisan) values.

### Roaster-Cafés

Several Nara coffee shops roast their own beans on-site, allowing them to control every stage of the process from green bean selection to final cup. These roaster-cafés tend to offer a rotating selection of single-origin coffees alongside a house blend, with staff who can explain the flavour profile and brewing method for each option.

Expect to pay 500 to 800 yen for a single-origin pourover at a specialty shop, and 400 to 600 yen for a house blend or espresso drink. Some shops offer tasting flights of two or three coffees, which is an excellent way to explore different flavour profiles.

## Japanese Tea at Coffee Shops

While coffee dominates, many of Nara's cafés also serve Japanese tea — and given Nara's proximity to the Yamato Plateau tea-growing regions, the quality can be exceptional. Matcha lattes, hojicha (roasted green tea), and cold-brewed sencha are increasingly common on café menus.

Some shops have positioned themselves at the intersection of coffee and tea culture, offering both with equal seriousness. This reflects Nara's particular identity as a city where tea ceremony traditions run deep, and where the idea of carefully preparing a hot beverage for a seated guest has centuries of context behind it.

## Café Food Culture in Nara

### Kissaten Classics

The food at traditional kissaten follows a distinct canon:

- **Thick toast (atsugiri tosuto)**: White bread, cut thick, toasted until golden, served with butter. Simple and excellent.
- **Napolitan spaghetti**: Spaghetti sautéed in ketchup-based sauce with onions, bell peppers, and ham. A purely Japanese invention that tastes better than it sounds.
- **Tamago sando**: Egg salad sandwich on crustless white bread. The definitive kissaten snack.
- **Cream soda**: Bright green melon soda with a float of vanilla ice cream. Nostalgic and photogenic.
- **Pudding (purin)**: Firm, caramel-topped custard. The standard kissaten dessert.

### Contemporary Café Food

Newer cafés in Nara tend toward a more health-conscious and aesthetically refined food offering: artisanal baked goods, seasonal fruit tarts, handmade granola, and lunch plates featuring local vegetables. Several have developed signature sweets that incorporate Nara ingredients — kuzu (arrowroot from Yoshino), local honey, or matcha from nearby growing regions.

## Practical Guide to Nara's Café Scene

### Best Areas for Café-Hopping

1. **Naramachi**: The highest concentration of both traditional kissaten and new specialty shops. Walk through the narrow lanes and discover something every few hundred metres.
2. **Kintetsu Nara Station surroundings**: Several established kissaten on the upper floors of buildings along Sanjo-dori and Higashimuki arcade.
3. **Nara Park periphery**: A handful of cafés positioned for post-temple-visit coffee, with views toward the park or surrounding gardens.
4. **Tomigaoka / residential areas**: Smaller neighbourhood cafés frequented by local residents rather than tourists, offering a window into everyday Nara life.

### Timing

- **Morning (7:00-10:00)**: Kissaten morning sets are the best way to start the day. Arrive early, eat toast and egg with coffee, and read or sit in silence as the city wakes up.
- **Mid-morning (10:00-11:30)**: Specialty coffee shops open, and this is the ideal window for exploring before lunch crowds arrive.
- **Afternoon (14:00-16:00)**: The traditional coffee break hour. Both kissaten and modern cafés are at their most atmospheric during this lull between lunch and dinner.

### Etiquette

- Seat yourself at most cafés and kissaten, unless directed otherwise.
- Order at least one drink per person — cafés are not free seating.
- Keep conversations at a moderate volume, especially in kissaten.
- At traditional kissaten, put away your laptop. At modern cafés, check whether Wi-Fi is offered as a signal that working is welcome.

For visitors staying at a ryokan like Kanoya, where mornings begin with a traditional Japanese breakfast, the afternoon provides the natural window for exploring Nara's café culture. A slow walk through Naramachi after visiting the morning temples, with a stop at a kissaten or specialty roaster, is one of the quiet pleasures that make Nara worth more than a day trip.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between a kissaten and a café in Japan?
A kissaten is a traditional Japanese coffee house, typically featuring dark Showa-era interiors, hand-dripped dark-roast coffee, and a nostalgic food menu. A café (kafe) in modern Japanese usage tends to refer to newer establishments with brighter interiors, specialty coffee, and a contemporary aesthetic.

### Do Nara coffee shops have Wi-Fi?
Modern specialty coffee shops increasingly offer Wi-Fi, though it is not universal. Traditional kissaten generally do not. Chain coffee shops (Starbucks, Tully's) reliably have Wi-Fi.

### What is a morning set (morning setto)?
*A morning set is a breakfast deal offered by kissaten and some cafés in Japan, typically available until 10:00 or 11:00, consisting of a cup of coffee with thick buttered toast and a boiled egg — all for the price of a coffee alone, usually 400 to 600 yen — making it one of the best breakfast values in Japan.*

### Can I work from a café in Nara?
Some modern cafés welcome laptop use, particularly those with Wi-Fi and power outlets. Traditional kissaten are not suitable for working. When in doubt, observe what other customers are doing.

### How much does coffee cost in Nara?
Traditional kissaten charge 400 to 700 yen per cup. Specialty coffee shops charge 500 to 800 yen for single-origin pourover. Chain coffee shops are slightly cheaper at 350 to 500 yen.
