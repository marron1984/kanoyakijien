# Murō-ji: The Women's Kōyasan Hidden in the Mountains

Deep in the mountains of eastern Nara Prefecture, up a narrow valley where the Murō River cuts through cedar forests, a temple exists that most visitors to Nara never hear about. Murō-ji has been here since the late 8th century, its buildings distributed across a steep, forested hillside that is breathtakingly beautiful in every season. But its historical significance rests on a single, radical fact: when the great monastic complex of Kōyasan in Wakayama Prefecture banned women from its precincts — a prohibition that lasted from the 11th century until 1872 — Murō-ji welcomed them. It became known as "Women's Kōyasan" (女人高野, nyonin kōya), a place where female devotees could practice Buddhism without restriction.

This is a temple that rewards the effort of reaching it. The journey from Nara is not brief, and the mountain setting is remote by Japanese standards. But the combination of exceptional architecture, important sculpture, ancient forest, and profound historical significance makes Murō-ji one of the most genuinely moving places in the Nara region.

## History

### Origins in the Mountains

Murō-ji's founding is attributed to the monk En no Gyōja (the legendary founder of Shugendō mountain asceticism) in the 7th century, though the temple as it exists today was established in the late 8th century under the patronage of the imperial court. The mountains around Murō had long been considered sacred — a place where dragons dwelled and rain could be summoned through ritual. The temple was originally associated with esoteric Buddhism, and its mountain setting reflects the Shingon Buddhist belief that wilderness is the ideal environment for spiritual practice.

### Why Women Were Welcomed

Mount Kōya, the headquarters of Shingon Buddhism founded by Kūkai in 816 CE, enforced a strict prohibition against women entering its sacred precincts. This ban, known as nyonin kekkai, was common at major monastic centres in medieval Japan and reflected broader religious attitudes that viewed women as spiritually impure. The prohibition was absolute — even empresses were turned away at the mountain's gates.

Murō-ji, as a Shingon temple in a mountain setting that echoed Kōyasan's, became the natural alternative. Women seeking to venerate Shingon Buddhism's principal deities, study its teachings, and experience its mountain ascetic practices came to Murō-ji instead. The temple did not merely tolerate female worshippers — it actively welcomed them, and its "Women's Kōyasan" identity became central to its character and its survival.

This history gives Murō-ji a significance that extends beyond art and architecture. It is a monument to the women who refused to accept exclusion from the spiritual life they valued, and to the temple that chose inclusion over convention.

## The Five-Storey Pagoda

### The Smallest Outdoor Pagoda in Japan

Murō-ji's five-storey pagoda, standing just 16.1 metres tall, is the smallest outdoor five-storey pagoda in Japan. What it lacks in height, it compensates for in setting. The pagoda stands on a steep hillside among towering cedars and Japanese cypress trees, its vermilion-painted wood glowing against the dark green forest. The contrast between the pagoda's geometric precision and the organic wildness of the surrounding forest is one of the most photographed compositions in Japanese architecture.

The pagoda dates from the late 8th or early 9th century — its exact construction date is debated — and was built using Japanese cypress (hinoki). In 1998, Typhoon No. 7 toppled a large cedar tree onto the pagoda, severely damaging the structure. The subsequent three-year restoration used traditional techniques and materials, and the rebuilt pagoda is now considered faithful to its original appearance. The incident was a reminder of the vulnerability of wooden architecture in a mountainous, forested environment — and of the commitment required to preserve it.

## The Main Hall and Mirokudō

### Nara-Period Sculpture in a Mountain Setting

The Kondō (Main Hall) houses a collection of Buddhist sculptures that includes a Shaka Nyorai triad and standing images of the Eleven-Headed Kannon and Jizō Bosatsu. These figures, dating from the Heian period, display the rounded, approachable style that characterised Japanese Buddhist sculpture after it began to move away from the more formal continental models of the Nara period.

The Mirokudō (Maitreya Hall), higher up the hillside, contains a seated Miroku Bosatsu (the Future Buddha) that is one of the temple's most important images. The climb to the Mirokudō through the forest is steep but short, and the hall's isolated position among the trees gives it an atmosphere of deep seclusion.

### The Inner Sanctum

Beyond the Mirokudō, a long stone stairway ascends steeply to the Okunoin (Inner Sanctum) at the highest point of the temple complex. The stairway, cut into the mountainside and flanked by moss-covered stone walls and towering cedars, is physically demanding — approximately 700 steps — but the journey through the forest is the point, not merely the destination. The Okunoin itself is a small hall in a clearing at the summit, surrounded by ancient trees. The silence here is absolute.

## The Natural Setting

### The Forest Cathedral

Murō-ji's forest is not merely a backdrop — it is integral to the temple's character and its religious function. The trees, some of them centuries old, create a canopy that filters light into green-gold columns. Moss covers the stone walls, steps, and lanterns with a thick, velvety layer that speaks to the constant moisture of the mountain environment. The sound of the Murō River, audible throughout the lower temple grounds, provides a continuous natural soundtrack.

In autumn (typically mid to late November), the maples scattered among the evergreen cedars create a colour contrast that is among the most beautiful in Nara Prefecture. The vermilion pagoda, ringed by golden and crimson foliage against dark green conifers, has appeared on more Japanese calendars and postcards than almost any other temple scene.

In spring, rhododendrons (shakunage) bloom throughout the hillside in late April to mid-May, adding purple and pink to the green forest palette. Murō-ji is particularly known for its wild rhododendron displays, which are less manicured than those at lowland gardens and have a natural exuberance suited to the mountain setting.

### Wildlife

The mountain forests around Murō-ji are home to Japanese giant salamanders (ōsanshōuo) in the river, wild boar in the upper forests, and a variety of bird species. The giant salamanders, which can reach over a metre in length, are occasionally spotted in the clear river waters — they are a nationally protected species and a reminder of the ecological richness that surrounds the temple.

## Getting There

### From Nara

The journey to Murō-ji requires some commitment. Take the Kintetsu Osaka Line from Kintetsu Nara Station to Murōguchi-Ōno Station (approximately 50 minutes with a transfer at Yamato-Saidaiji). From the station, a Nara Kōtsū bus runs to Murō-ji (approximately 15 minutes, ¥440). Buses run roughly hourly, so check the schedule before departing.

### Combining with Hase-dera

Murō-ji combines naturally with Hase-dera for a full-day temple excursion. Visit Hase-dera in the morning, take the bus from the Hasedera Station area to Murōguchi-Ōno, then continue by bus to Murō-ji. Return to Nara in the late afternoon. This requires an early start (8:00am from Nara) but creates one of the most rewarding day trips in the Kansai region.

### Recommended Timing

- **Time at the temple**: 1.5–2 hours (including the climb to Okunoin; 1 hour without)
- **Total day trip time**: 5–6 hours from Nara including travel
- **Best seasons**: Autumn foliage (mid-November), rhododendrons (late April–mid-May), fresh green (June)

Guests at Kanoya ryokan planning a Murō-ji visit should consider staying two nights in Nara, using one full day for the mountain temple excursion and the other for central Nara's sites.

## Practical Information

- **Hours**: 8:30am–5:00pm (April–November); 9:00am–4:00pm (December–March)
- **Admission**: ¥600 (adults)
- **Access**: Nara Kōtsū bus from Murōguchi-Ōno Station (Kintetsu line, 50 minutes from Nara); bus ride 15 minutes, ¥440
- **Physical demands**: The temple involves significant uphill walking; the climb to Okunoin is steep (700 steps)
- **Facilities**: Small shop and rest area near the entrance; no facilities on the upper hillside

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is Murō-ji called "Women's Kōyasan"?

When Mount Kōya banned women from its precincts (11th century–1872), Murō-ji welcomed female devotees as an alternative Shingon Buddhist mountain temple. It became known as nyonin kōya — "Women's Kōyasan" — a place of spiritual practice without gender exclusion.

### How difficult is the climb at Murō-ji?

The main temple buildings are reached by moderate hillside paths. The climb to the Okunoin (Inner Sanctum) involves approximately 700 steep stone steps through the forest and is physically demanding. The pagoda and main hall can be visited without this climb.

### When is the best time to visit Murō-ji?

Autumn foliage (mid to late November) is the most popular season. Rhododendron season (late April to mid-May) is equally beautiful and less crowded. The fresh green of early summer (June) offers the most atmospheric forest experience.

### Can I combine Murō-ji with Hase-dera?

Yes. Visit Hase-dera in the morning, then take the bus to Murō-ji via Murōguchi-Ōno Station. This full-day itinerary requires an early start from Nara (approximately 8:00am).
