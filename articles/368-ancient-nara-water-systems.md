# Ancient Nara's Water Systems: Engineering and Sacred Springs

Water shaped Nara in ways that are not immediately obvious to the modern visitor. The temples draw attention; the deer are photogenic; the park is beautiful. But beneath and around these visible attractions lies a story about water — how it was managed, channelled, revered, and used — that reveals a different dimension of the ancient capital. In Nara, water was simultaneously a practical engineering challenge and a sacred substance, and understanding both aspects enriches a visit to the city.

## Water and the Ancient Capital

When Empress Genmei chose the site for Heijō-kyō in 710 CE, water was a primary consideration. The Nara Basin offered several advantages: the Saho River to the north and east, the Akishino River to the west, and numerous streams and springs fed by rainfall on the surrounding hills. But a capital of 100,000 to 200,000 people required more than natural watercourses — it required engineered systems to supply drinking water, remove waste, irrigate gardens, and fill the moats and ponds that were both defensive features and aesthetic elements of the city.

### The Canal System

Archaeological excavations at the Heijō Palace Site have revealed a sophisticated network of canals and drainage channels running beneath the streets of the ancient capital. These channels were constructed from wood and stone, aligned with the city's grid plan, and designed to carry water from the eastern hills through the residential and commercial districts.

The main canal ran along the central boulevard (Suzaku-ōji) and branched into smaller channels serving individual city blocks. The system served multiple purposes:

- **Drinking water supply**: Spring water was channelled from the hills to public and private wells throughout the city
- **Waste removal**: Drainage channels carried wastewater and sewage away from residential areas
- **Fire suppression**: In a city built almost entirely of wood, water access was a critical safety feature
- **Aesthetic function**: Canals and ponds beautified the palace grounds and the residences of the aristocracy

### Wells and Water Supply

Excavations have uncovered hundreds of wells from the Nara period, ranging from simple wooden-frame structures in commoners' districts to elaborate stone-lined wells in the palace compound. Analysis of well construction shows that the techniques became more sophisticated over the seventy years of the capital's existence, suggesting ongoing investment in water infrastructure.

The quality of water varied across the city. The eastern districts, closer to the hills and the head of the spring-fed streams, generally had better water quality than the western districts, which relied more heavily on the canal system. This may help explain why the eastern side of the city was more densely populated and more desirable — a pattern visible in the concentration of temples and aristocratic residences on the eastern side.

### The Palace Water Gardens

The Heijō Palace complex included elaborate water features — ponds, streams, and garden waterways — that served both aesthetic and ceremonial functions. Archaeological work at the palace site's East Palace Garden (Tōin Teien) has revealed a landscape garden with a sinuously curving stream designed for the aristocratic pastime of kyokusui no en — a poetry game in which participants sat along a winding stream and composed poems before a cup of wine floated past them.

This garden has been partially reconstructed and is open to visitors at the Heijō Palace Site. It represents one of the earliest examples of Japanese garden design and demonstrates how water functioned as an integral element of courtly culture.

## Sacred Water in Nara's Temples and Shrines

If the civil engineering of water supply shows Nara's practical side, the sacred use of water reveals its spiritual dimension. Water in Buddhist and Shinto tradition is not merely a physical substance — it is a purifying agent, a sacred offering, and a medium of connection between the human and divine worlds.

### Kasuga Taisha's Sacred Springs

The springs on Mount Kasuga, behind Kasuga Taisha shrine, have been revered since before the Nara period. The primeval forest that covers the mountain has been protected for over a thousand years, and the springs that emerge from it are considered sacred.

Several named springs on the shrine grounds are accessible to visitors:

**Mizuya Jinja**: A small subsidiary shrine dedicated to water deities, located near one of the mountain's springs.

**The purification basins (temizu-sha)**: The water used in the ritual hand-washing basins at Kasuga Taisha is drawn from the sacred springs, maintaining a direct connection between the mountain's water and the act of purification that precedes worship.

The Kasuga springs are part of a broader pattern in Japanese religion in which mountains are seen as the source of life-giving water, and springs are treated as points where the sacred power of the mountain becomes accessible. This belief system, which predates Buddhism and blends Shinto nature worship with Buddhist cosmology, is particularly visible in Nara, where the eastern hills and their waters have been revered continuously since antiquity.

### The Omizutori Ceremony

The most dramatic expression of water's sacred role in Nara is the Omizutori ("Water-Drawing") ceremony at Tōdai-ji's Nigatsu-dō (Second Month Hall). Held annually in March as the climax of a two-week repentance ritual called Shuni-e, the ceremony has been performed without interruption since 752 CE — making it one of the longest-running annual rituals in the world.

The water-drawing takes place at approximately 2:00 AM on March 13th (by the traditional calendar, the 12th day of the second month). Monks descend to a well called the Wakasa-i, which is said to be connected underground to a spring in faraway Wakasa Province (present-day Fukui Prefecture). According to legend, the deity of Wakasa was late to a gathering of gods at Tōdai-ji and, in apology, caused a spring to emerge that sends sacred water from Wakasa to Nara.

The water drawn from the Wakasa-i well is considered to have healing and purifying properties. It is presented as an offering to the Kannon enshrined in the Nigatsu-dō and distributed in small quantities to worshippers.

The fire ceremony that accompanies Omizutori — in which monks carry enormous flaming torches along the balcony of the Nigatsu-dō — is the more visually spectacular aspect, but the water-drawing is the ritual's spiritual heart. Fire and water together, destruction and purification, are the complementary forces that the ceremony invokes.

### Temple Purification and Ritual Use

Water plays a role in daily temple life that visitors may observe without recognising its significance:

**Temizu (hand-washing)**: The ritual washing of hands and mouth before entering a shrine or temple is the most visible water ritual. The act is not about physical cleanliness but spiritual purification — removing the pollution (kegare) of the everyday world before entering sacred space.

**Sutra copying**: Water is used in the preparation of ink for sutra copying (shakyō), a meditative practice offered at several Nara temples. The mixing of water and ink is itself treated as a mindful, purifying act.

**Garden water features**: The streams, ponds, and waterfalls in temple gardens are not merely decorative. In Buddhist symbolism, flowing water represents the impermanence of all things, while still water represents the calm of an enlightened mind.

## Natural Springs in Modern Nara

Several natural springs in and around Nara remain accessible and carry echoes of their sacred and practical history:

**Sarusawa Pond**: This pond near Kōfuku-ji has been a central feature of the Nara landscape since the 8th century. Originally a reservoir within the capital's water system, it became a site of legend and literature. Its surface, reflecting Kōfuku-ji's five-storey pagoda, is one of Nara's most recognisable views.

**Yoshiki-en Garden**: This garden near Isuien uses water from the Yoshiki River in a traditional landscape design. The garden's streams and ponds demonstrate how water was integrated into Japanese garden aesthetics — principles that originated in the Nara period.

**The springs of Nara Park**: Several small springs emerge in Nara Park, particularly along the eastern edge near the base of Mount Wakakusa. These are the same geological features that made the site attractive for the ancient capital — the reliable, clean water that flows from the forested hills.

## Practical Information for Visitors

Travellers interested in Nara's water heritage can follow a route that connects several key sites:

**Morning**: Begin at the Heijō Palace Site, where the reconstructed East Palace Garden shows the role of water in courtly culture. The garden is usually quiet in the morning.

**Midday**: Walk through Nara Park to Kasuga Taisha, noting the streams that cross the park and the purification basins at the shrine entrance. The walk along the approach to Kasuga passes through the ancient forest, whose springs feed the park's waterways.

**Afternoon**: Visit Nigatsu-dō at Tōdai-ji, where the Omizutori water-drawing takes place each March. The well itself is not visible, but the hall's hilltop location — with its commanding view over Nara — conveys the spiritual significance of the site.

**Evening**: End at Sarusawa Pond, where the still water reflects the five-storey pagoda and the evening sky.

For those visiting in March, the Omizutori ceremony (March 1–14) is one of Nara's most powerful experiences. The fire ceremony on the Nigatsu-dō balcony draws large crowds, but the entire two-week ritual period has a contemplative intensity that rewards an extended stay. Kanoya ryokan provides a quiet retreat during this period, close enough to Tōdai-ji for late-night visits to the ceremony but far enough from the crowds for rest between them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Did ancient Nara have a water supply system?

Yes, archaeological excavations have revealed a network of canals, drainage channels, and wells that supplied the capital with drinking water, removed waste, and served aesthetic functions. The system was aligned with the city's grid plan and supplied by springs from the eastern hills.

### What is the Omizutori ceremony?

Omizutori ("Water-Drawing") is a ritual at Tōdai-ji's Nigatsu-dō held annually in March since 752 CE. Monks draw sacred water from a special well believed to be connected to a spring in distant Wakasa Province. The ceremony includes a dramatic fire ritual with large torches.

### Can I see ancient water features in Nara today?

Yes. The reconstructed East Palace Garden at the Heijō Palace Site includes a Nara-period stream garden. Sarusawa Pond dates from the 8th century. The springs and streams of Nara Park and Kasuga Taisha continue to flow from the same sources that supplied the ancient capital.

### Why was water sacred in Nara?

In both Buddhist and Shinto tradition, water is a purifying substance that connects the human and divine worlds. Springs were seen as points where sacred mountain power became accessible, and water rituals — from simple hand-washing to the elaborate Omizutori ceremony — expressed this belief.
