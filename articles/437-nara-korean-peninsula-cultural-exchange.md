# Nara's Connection to Korean Peninsula: Cultural Exchange History

There is a truth about Nara that is visible everywhere but rarely stated plainly: this city, the cradle of Japanese civilization, was built with Korean hands and Korean knowledge. The Great Buddha at Todai-ji was cast using techniques transmitted from the Korean Peninsula. The temple architecture that defines Nara's skyline derives from Korean prototypes. The Buddhist scriptures that gave Nara its spiritual identity arrived through Korean monks. The very system of government that made Nara a capital was modeled on institutions that Japan learned, in significant part, through its close relationship with the kingdoms of Korea.

This is not a controversial claim among historians, though it is one that the complexities of modern Japanese-Korean relations sometimes make uncomfortable to discuss openly. The historical record is clear: the cultural exchange between the Korean Peninsula and the Yamato court — centered in the Nara region — was one of the most consequential transfers of knowledge, technology, and belief in East Asian history.

## The Three Kingdoms and Yamato

### Baekje: Japan's Closest Ally

Of the three kingdoms that dominated the Korean Peninsula in the ancient period — Goguryeo in the north, Silla in the southeast, and Baekje in the southwest — it was Baekje that maintained the closest relationship with Japan. The connection was political, military, cultural, and, according to some historians, familial. The Nihon Shoki, Japan's eighth-century chronicle compiled in Nara, records extensive interactions between the Yamato court and Baekje, including military alliances, diplomatic marriages, and the exchange of scholars and artisans.

Baekje's most consequential gift to Japan was Buddhism. In 538 or 552 CE (scholars debate the exact year), King Seong of Baekje sent a golden Buddha image, sutras, and a letter recommending the Buddhist faith to the Yamato court. This diplomatic-religious gesture initiated the transformation of Japanese culture that would culminate in the establishment of Nara as a Buddhist capital in 710 CE.

But Buddhism did not arrive as an abstract philosophy. It came with the material culture necessary to practice it: temple architecture, sculpture, painting, metalwork, textile production, and the literate bureaucratic systems that Buddhist monasteries required. Baekje sent not only scriptures but also architects, sculptors, tile makers, and monks — the people who could build the physical infrastructure of a Buddhist civilization.

### Goguryeo's Contributions

Goguryeo, the northern Korean kingdom, also contributed significantly to Japanese culture. The monk Damjing (known in Japan as Doncho) traveled from Goguryeo to Japan in 610 CE, bringing knowledge of papermaking, ink production, and pigment preparation. The wall paintings in Japan's earliest temples — including the famous murals of Horyuji's Kondo (tragically destroyed by fire in 1949) — showed strong stylistic connections to Goguryeo mural painting traditions.

Goguryeo's influence is particularly visible in Nara's earliest artistic heritage. The facial features of certain Asuka and early Nara-period Buddhist statues, the compositional conventions of early temple paintings, and specific iconographic details all point to Goguryeo as a source alongside the more frequently cited Baekje.

### Silla and the Unification Period

After Silla unified the Korean Peninsula in 668 CE with Tang Chinese support, the relationship between Korea and Japan changed character but did not diminish. Silla sent embassies to the Nara court, and Japanese envoys traveled to the Silla capital of Gyeongju. The exchanges during this period were more formal and diplomatic than the earlier Baekje relationship, but they continued to transmit cultural and technological knowledge.

Gyeongju, Silla's capital, was itself a magnificent planned city, and its influence on the design of Nara — Heijokyo, established in 710 — is a subject of ongoing scholarly discussion. Both cities were laid out on grid plans derived from the Chinese capital of Chang'an, but certain features of Nara's design show closer parallels to Gyeongju than to any Chinese model, suggesting that Japanese planners studied the Korean capital as well as the Chinese one.

## Korean Artisans in Nara

### The Builders

The construction of Nara's great temples required skills that were not yet fully developed among Japanese craftsmen in the seventh and eighth centuries. Korean builders — particularly those from Baekje and its successor communities in Japan — provided essential expertise. The technique of constructing large-scale timber-frame buildings with complex bracket systems, the methods of firing and laying ceramic roof tiles, and the engineering knowledge required to build multi-storey pagodas all came to Japan primarily through Korean transmission.

The families of Korean artisans who settled in the Yamato region became integrated into Japanese society over generations, but their origins were recorded and remembered. The Shinsen Shojiroku, a ninth-century genealogical register compiled shortly after the Nara period, lists numerous prominent families of Korean descent, including many whose ancestors had been artisans, scholars, or monks.

### The Sculptors

Tori Busshi, the sculptor credited with some of the earliest and most important Buddhist statues in Japan — including works at Horyuji — was a descendant of a family that had emigrated from Baekje. His artistic style, known as the Tori style, shows clear connections to Korean and Chinese Buddhist sculpture of the sixth century. Through Tori and his workshop, Korean sculptural traditions became the foundation of Japanese Buddhist art.

In Nara itself, the massive bronze Buddha at Todai-ji — cast in 752 CE — required bronze-casting expertise on a scale unprecedented in Japan. The project drew on knowledge that had been accumulating through Korean-mediated technology transfer for two centuries. Historical records suggest that craftsmen of Korean descent participated directly in the casting.

### The Tile Makers

One of the most tangible Korean legacies in Nara is visible at Gangoji temple in Naramachi. The temple's roof includes tiles dating to the Asuka period (sixth-seventh centuries) — among the oldest roof tiles in Japan. These tiles were made using techniques brought from Baekje, and their style is recognizably Korean. They have survived for approximately 1,400 years, a testament to the quality of the technology that crossed the sea.

The production of ceramic roof tiles was a Korean introduction to Japan. Before Buddhism arrived, Japanese buildings used thatched roofs. The tiled roofs that define Japanese temple architecture — and that spread to secular buildings over the following centuries — are a Korean gift that fundamentally changed the appearance of Japanese architecture.

## Buddhism's Korean Pathway

### Transmission and Translation

Buddhism originated in India, matured philosophically in China, and arrived in Japan primarily through Korea. This three-stage transmission was not merely a relay — each culture that received Buddhism added interpretive layers, artistic traditions, and organizational structures. The Buddhism that reached Japan via Baekje was already a Korean Buddhism, shaped by Korean scholars' engagement with Chinese texts and Korean artists' interpretations of Buddhist iconography.

The sutras that formed the theological foundation of Nara Buddhism — the Kegon Sutra that inspired Todai-ji, the Hosso teachings that defined Kofuku-ji — arrived through Korean scholarly networks. Korean monks served as teachers and translators, making Chinese Buddhist texts accessible to Japanese students who were still developing their own capacity for reading classical Chinese.

### Korean Monks in Nara

Throughout the Nara period, Korean monks lived, studied, and taught in Nara's temples. They participated in the great Buddhist ceremonies, contributed to theological debates, and helped train the Japanese monastic community that would eventually sustain Buddhist practice independently. Their presence was not exceptional — it was a normal part of the cosmopolitan culture of eighth-century Nara, which also included Chinese monks, Central Asian traders, and envoys from Southeast Asia.

The Shoso-in, the imperial repository at Todai-ji, contains artifacts that document this cosmopolitan character. Musical instruments, textiles, medicines, and decorative objects from across Asia were collected at the Nara court, reflecting a culture that was actively engaged with the broader world through networks that often passed through or originated in Korea.

## Visible Korean Connections in Nara Today

### Architecture

The bracket systems (tokyou) that support the roofs of Nara's oldest temples — Horyuji, Toshodai-ji, the surviving structures of Yakushi-ji — are variations on construction methods that arrived from the Korean Peninsula. The basic principle — using interlocking wooden brackets to extend the eaves far beyond the supporting columns — is a technology that Japan received from Korea and China and then developed into distinctive Japanese forms.

### Sculpture

The Buddhist statues in Nara's museums and temples trace a stylistic lineage that passes through Korea. The gentle, archaic smiles of early Japanese Buddhist figures echo Korean prototypes from Baekje and Silla. As Japanese sculptors developed their own styles during the Nara and Heian periods, they moved away from Korean models, but the foundation remained visible.

### The Shoso-in Treasures

The Shoso-in annual exhibition at the Nara National Museum (typically held in late October and November) displays artifacts from the eighth-century imperial collection that include objects of Korean origin or showing Korean stylistic influence. These exhibitions provide direct evidence of the material culture that flowed between Korea and Nara.

## Experiencing This History

Understanding the Korean connection enriches every temple visit in Nara. When you look at the roof tiles of Gangoji, you are looking at Korean craftsmanship. When you stand before the Great Buddha, you are seeing the culmination of a technology transfer that began with Baekje artisans. When you walk through Horyuji, you are walking through a building that could not have existed without Korean knowledge.

Visitors who stay in Nara long enough to visit multiple temples — using a base like Kanoya ryokan in Naramachi, within walking distance of Gangoji and its ancient Korean-made tiles — can trace this story across the city, from the earliest Asuka-period artifacts to the mature Nara-period masterpieces that represent the full flowering of a tradition that began across the sea.

The story of Nara and Korea is ultimately a story about how civilizations are built: not in isolation, but through exchange, borrowing, adaptation, and the generous movement of knowledge across borders. Nara is not less Japanese for its Korean connections. It is more fully understood.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Did Korea introduce Buddhism to Japan?

Yes. Buddhism arrived in Japan from the Korean kingdom of Baekje in the mid-sixth century (538 or 552 CE), when King Seong sent Buddhist scriptures and a golden Buddha image to the Yamato court.

### Are there Korean-made artifacts still visible in Nara?

Yes. The most notable are the Asuka-period roof tiles at Gangoji temple, which were made using techniques brought from Baekje. The Shoso-in repository at Todai-ji also contains objects of Korean origin or influence.

### How did Korean artisans end up in Japan?

Korean artisans came to Japan through several channels: as members of diplomatic missions, as refugees from conflicts on the Korean Peninsula (particularly after the fall of Baekje in 660 CE), and as invited specialists brought to serve the Yamato court's building programs.

### What is the relationship between Gyeongju and Nara?

*Gyeongju, the capital of Korea's Silla kingdom, and Nara were contemporary capital cities that shared certain design principles derived from the Chinese capital of Chang'an. Both were planned cities on grid layouts, both were centers of Buddhist culture, and both maintained diplomatic relations. Today, the two cities are sister cities, reflecting a historical connection that spans more than 1,300 years.*
