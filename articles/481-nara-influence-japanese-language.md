# Nara's Influence on Japanese Language: Words Born in the Ancient Capital

Before Tokyo was a fishing village, before Kyoto laid its first stone, Nara was the place where the Japanese language found its written voice. The city that served as Japan's capital from 710 to 784 CE did more than build temples and cast bronze Buddhas. It produced the country's earliest substantial literary works, established the conventions for writing Japanese with Chinese characters, and minted words and phrases that remain embedded in everyday speech more than twelve centuries later.

For visitors with even a passing interest in language, Nara offers something no other Japanese city can: the physical landscape where a nation's literary tradition began, still largely intact and still resonant with the words that were first composed here.

## The Birth of Written Japanese

### Before Nara: An Oral Culture

Japanese existed as a spoken language for millennia before anyone attempted to write it down. The challenge was fundamental: Japanese and Chinese are unrelated languages with completely different grammatical structures, yet Chinese characters — kanji — were the only writing system available in East Asia. Imagine trying to write English using Arabic script, and you approach the scale of the problem that confronted Japan's early scholars.

The solution, developed gradually during the centuries before the Nara period, was to use Chinese characters in two ways simultaneously: for their meaning (using the Chinese concept but pronouncing it in Japanese) and for their sound (ignoring the meaning entirely and using the character purely as a phonetic symbol). This dual system, clumsy as it was, made it possible for the first time to record Japanese speech in writing.

It was during the Nara period that this system was refined, standardized, and used to produce works of lasting significance.

### Man'yogana: The Ancestor of Hiragana and Katakana

The phonetic use of Chinese characters — called man'yogana after the great poetry anthology that employed it most extensively — was Nara's crucial contribution to Japanese literacy. Each Chinese character was conscripted to represent a Japanese syllable. The character 安 (an, meaning "peace"), for example, was used simply for its sound "a." Over the following centuries, these borrowed characters were simplified through cursive writing into hiragana and through abbreviation into katakana — the two phonetic scripts that, alongside kanji, form the modern Japanese writing system.

Every time someone in Japan writes a text message, fills out a form, or reads a novel, they are using a system whose foundations were laid in Nara. The city's contribution to Japanese literacy is not historical trivia; it is the bedrock of daily communication.

## The Man'yoshu: Japan's First Great Anthology

### A Democratic Collection

The Man'yoshu — literally "Collection of Ten Thousand Leaves" — is Japan's oldest surviving poetry anthology, compiled sometime after 759 CE. Its 4,516 poems span more than four centuries, but its heart belongs to the Nara period. What makes the Man'yoshu extraordinary, and relevant to understanding Nara's linguistic legacy, is its remarkable breadth. Unlike later anthologies dominated by court aristocrats, the Man'yoshu includes poems attributed to emperors and empresses alongside verses by frontier soldiers, rural peasants, traveling officials, and anonymous commoners.

This democratic range preserved a snapshot of the Japanese language at a pivotal moment — before the heavy influence of Chinese literary conventions reshaped court poetry in the Heian period. The vocabulary is earthier, more direct, and more emotionally transparent than the elegant indirectness that would later define Japanese poetic diction.

### Words Preserved in Amber

The Man'yoshu preserves words and grammatical forms that had already become archaic by the Heian period. Linguists treat it as an invaluable window into Old Japanese, the language spoken during the Nara era. Some of these ancient forms survive in modern Japanese in fossilized expressions. The word "inishie" (古), meaning "the ancient past," appears in the Man'yoshu and remains in use today, carrying a weight of nostalgia that its modern synonym "mukashi" does not quite match.

Place names in the collection map directly onto Nara's geography. Mount Mikasa, the Sahoyama hills, the Yoshino River, the fields of Kasuga — these locations appear repeatedly in Man'yoshu poems, and visitors can stand in the same landscapes today. Reading even a single translated verse before visiting Kasuga Shrine or walking along the Sahogawa River transforms the experience from sightseeing into something closer to literary pilgrimage.

## Words That Were Born in Nara

### Administrative and Religious Vocabulary

The Nara period was when Japan built its first permanent capital modeled on Chinese principles, established a centralized bureaucracy, and adopted Buddhism as a state-supported religion. Each of these developments required new vocabulary. Words for government ranks, administrative districts, tax categories, and legal procedures were coined or adapted from Chinese during this era. Many remain in use: "todofuken" (都道府県), the modern term for Japan's prefectural system, traces its conceptual lineage to the provincial structure established in the Nara period.

Buddhist terminology flooded into Japanese during this era as sutras were translated, temples were built, and monks developed a Japanese vocabulary for concepts that had no native equivalent. Words like "gaman" (我慢, patience or endurance), "shujin" (主人, master or husband), and "danna" (旦那, patron or husband) all entered Japanese through Buddhist channels during or around the Nara period. These words have long since lost their religious connotations for most speakers, but their origins in Nara-period Buddhism remain traceable.

### Everyday Words with Ancient Roots

Some of the most common words in modern Japanese crystallized during the Nara era. "Sakura" (桜) for cherry blossom, "shika" (鹿) for deer, and "miyako" (都) for capital city all appear in Nara-period texts in forms recognizable to modern speakers. The word "nara" itself has been subject to centuries of folk etymology — some derive it from "narasu" (to flatten), referring to the level plain where the city was built, while others connect it to the Korean word "nara" meaning "country" or "kingdom," reflecting the deep Korean influence on Nara-period culture.

The counting system, the names of months in the old calendar, honorific verb forms, and many of the particles that give Japanese its characteristic grammatical texture — all were already in use and recorded in Nara-period documents. To study the Nara period linguistically is to encounter the DNA of the modern language.

## Nara Dialect: A Living Relic

### Kansai Roots

Modern Nara sits within the broader Kansai dialect region, sharing many features with the speech of Osaka and Kyoto. But the Nara dialect — Nara-ben — retains certain archaic features that reflect the area's deep history. Certain verb endings, intonation patterns, and vocabulary items found in Nara speech are closer to classical Japanese than the forms used in neighboring cities.

Visitors will notice Nara-ben immediately if they have studied standard Tokyo Japanese. The characteristic melodic intonation of Kansai speech is present, along with distinctive expressions. "Oideyasu" as a welcome greeting, "akan" for "no good," and "honma" for "really" are Kansai-wide features, but Nara speakers deploy them with their own rhythm and emphasis.

### Yoshino and Southern Dialects

Travel south into the mountainous districts of Yoshino and Totsukawa, and the dialect diverges further. The Totsukawa dialect, spoken in one of Japan's most isolated mountain communities, preserves linguistic features that some scholars have linked to the speech of the ancient Yamato court. The isolation of these mountain villages — Totsukawa was accessible only by foot until the twentieth century — created a linguistic time capsule that is only now being documented before its oldest speakers pass away.

## Literary Landmarks in Nara

### The Man'yoshu Botanical Garden

Nara's Kasuga Taisha shrine complex includes a botanical garden dedicated to the plants mentioned in the Man'yoshu. Each specimen is labeled with the relevant poem, creating a living anthology where literature and nature overlap. Walking through the garden in spring or autumn, when many of the referenced plants are in bloom, is one of Nara's most quietly powerful cultural experiences.

### Inscription Stones and Poetry Monuments

Throughout Nara city and the surrounding countryside, stone monuments inscribed with Man'yoshu poems mark the locations where particular verses were composed. These "kahi" (歌碑) are found near temples, along walking paths, beside rivers, and on mountain overlooks. They are easily overlooked by hurried visitors, but for those who pause to read them — even in translation — they connect the modern landscape to its literary past with startling directness.

### Todai-ji's Repository: The Shoso-in

The Shoso-in, the imperial repository at Todai-ji temple, houses documents from the Nara period that include some of the earliest examples of written Japanese. While the building itself is only open to public viewing briefly each autumn, and the documents are displayed in rotation at the Nara National Museum, seeing original Nara-period calligraphy — brush strokes laid down twelve hundred years ago — is a profound encounter with the physical origins of Japanese writing.

## Language as a Travel Lens

Understanding even fragments of Nara's linguistic history changes the experience of visiting the city. The deer in Nara Park become "shika," a word spoken in this same place for over a millennium. The shrine at Kasuga becomes the "Kasuga" of dozens of Man'yoshu poems. The evening light on Mount Wakakusa becomes the same light that inspired Yamanoue no Okura to compose verses about the brevity of human life.

Guests at Kanoya ryokan who have spent a day tracing these literary connections often find that the evening's kaiseki dinner, with its seasonal references and poetic dish names, resonates differently. Japanese cuisine names its dishes with the same attention to seasonal language that the Man'yoshu poets brought to their verses — and the tradition is continuous, linking the eighth-century court to the modern table.

## Practical Information for Language Enthusiasts

The Nara National Museum and the Nara Prefecture Complex of Man'yo Culture (Man'yo Bunkakan) in Asuka, south of Nara city, are the best starting points for exploring the literary heritage. The Man'yo Bunkakan offers interactive displays that bring the anthology to life even for visitors who do not read Japanese.

English translations of the Man'yoshu are available in several editions; the most accessible is the one-volume selection by Ian Hideo Levy. Reading even a handful of poems before visiting Nara rewards the traveler many times over.

Guided literary walks are occasionally organized by local cultural associations, particularly during the spring and autumn tourism seasons. The Nara City tourist information office at Kintetsu Nara Station can advise on current offerings.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How did Nara influence the Japanese language?
Nara served as the site where Japanese was first systematically written down, where the phonetic precursors to hiragana and katakana were developed, and where Japan's first major literary works were composed. Many modern Japanese words for administrative, religious, and everyday concepts were coined during the Nara period (710-784 CE).

### What is the Man'yoshu and why is it important?
The Man'yoshu is Japan's oldest surviving poetry anthology, containing 4,516 poems compiled after 759 CE. It preserves Old Japanese vocabulary and grammar, includes works by people of all social classes, and provides the earliest extensive written record of the Japanese language.

### Can I visit Man'yoshu-related sites in Nara?
Yes. The Man'yoshu Botanical Garden at Kasuga Taisha displays plants mentioned in the anthology alongside the relevant poems. Poetry inscription stones are scattered throughout the city and countryside. The Nara National Museum periodically displays original Nara-period documents.

### What is special about the Nara dialect?
*The Nara dialect (Nara-ben) belongs to the broader Kansai dialect family but retains certain archaic features that reflect the region's history as Japan's first permanent capital. Some verb forms and vocabulary items used in Nara speech are closer to classical Japanese than the standard Tokyo dialect, making the living dialect a subtle echo of the language spoken in the ancient capital over twelve centuries ago.*

### Are there English resources for exploring Nara's literary heritage?
Yes. Ian Hideo Levy's English translation of the Man'yoshu is widely available. The Man'yo Bunkakan museum in Asuka offers some English-language displays, and the Nara City tourist office can provide information on guided literary walks available in English.
