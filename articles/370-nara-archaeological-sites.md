# Nara's Archaeological Sites: Digging into the Ancient Capital

Beneath modern Nara lies another city. The ancient capital of Heijō-kyō — its palaces, residences, workshops, markets, and streets — is still there, buried under rice paddies, parking lots, and suburban houses. Over the past century, archaeological excavations have progressively revealed this hidden city, uncovering objects, structures, and information that transform our understanding of life in 8th-century Japan.

For visitors, Nara's archaeological heritage offers something different from its famous temples. The temples are magnificent, but they represent the survivors — the institutions grand enough and well-maintained enough to have lasted twelve centuries. Archaeology reveals the rest: the ordinary houses, the workshops, the kitchens, the drains, the discarded pottery, the wooden tablets on which officials scribbled their daily business. It reveals the city that the temples stood in.

## The Heijō Palace Site (Heijō-kyū Seki)

The most important and accessible archaeological site in Nara is the Heijō Palace Site, located in the northwestern part of the modern city. The palace was the political centre of Japan from 710 to 784 CE — the place where emperors held court, officials conducted government business, and ceremonies of state were performed.

### History of Excavation

After the capital moved to Kyoto in 784 CE, the palace was abandoned and gradually returned to agricultural use. By the Edo period, rice paddies covered the entire site, and the exact location of the palace was uncertain.

Systematic archaeological investigation began in 1959, and the site was designated a Special National Historic Site in 1952. Since then, the Nara National Research Institute for Cultural Properties (Nabunken) has conducted continuous excavations that have revealed the layout of the palace in remarkable detail.

### What Has Been Found

Excavations have uncovered:

**Building foundations**: The stone bases of major palace buildings, including the Former Imperial Audience Hall (Daigoku-den), the administrative offices (chōdō-in), and numerous smaller structures. The foundations reveal the scale and arrangement of the palace complex — a grid of buildings aligned on a north-south axis, with the emperor's audience hall at the northern end.

**Wooden tablets (mokkan)**: Over 350,000 mokkan have been recovered from the palace site — thin strips of wood used for administrative records, delivery tags, practice calligraphy, and personal notes. These tablets are among the most important primary sources for understanding Nara-period government, economy, and daily life. They record tax shipments from the provinces, requests for supplies, personnel lists, and even casual messages between officials.

**Ceramics and metalwork**: Thousands of pottery fragments, roof tiles, and metal objects document the material culture of the palace and the city. The roof tiles, stamped with identifying marks, help archaeologists track which buildings were constructed when and by which workshops.

**The East Palace Garden**: A landscape garden with a sinuously curving stream, designed for the aristocratic poetry game kyokusui no en, was discovered and has been partially reconstructed. It is one of the oldest garden designs found in Japan.

**Latrines and waste pits**: Less glamorous but highly informative, the contents of palace toilets and rubbish pits reveal details about diet (including fish species, grain types, and fruit seeds), health (intestinal parasites are identifiable), and craft production (workshop waste).

### What You Can See Today

The Heijō Palace Site is a vast open area — approximately 1 km by 1 km — with several reconstructed buildings and an excellent museum:

**The Former Imperial Audience Hall (Daigoku-den)**: Reconstructed in 2010 to mark the 1,300th anniversary of Nara's founding, this massive building gives a powerful sense of the palace's scale. The reconstruction is based on archaeological evidence and historical records, using traditional construction methods.

**The Suzaku Gate (Suzakumon)**: The reconstructed main gate of the palace, standing at the head of the great central boulevard (Suzaku-ōji) that divided the city in two.

**The East Palace Garden (Tōin Teien)**: The reconstructed stream garden, surrounded by restored plantings, offers a glimpse of courtly leisure.

**The Heijō Palace Site Museum**: An excellent modern facility displaying artefacts from the excavations, including mokkan, ceramics, and scale models of the palace complex. The museum provides essential context for understanding what the open site looked like when it was a functioning palace.

**Excavation areas**: Depending on the time of your visit, you may be able to observe ongoing archaeological work. Nabunken frequently has open excavation areas where archaeologists are actively working.

The site is free to enter and can be explored on foot. Allow at least two to three hours, and bring comfortable shoes — the distances are considerable.

## The Heijō-kyō City Site

Beyond the palace, the ancient city itself has been the subject of extensive archaeological investigation. The remains of Heijō-kyō extend well beyond the palace site, underlaying much of modern Nara's western suburbs.

### What Has Been Found

**Street grid**: The city's original grid plan — based on Chang'an, with streets dividing the city into rectangular blocks (bō) — has been confirmed by archaeological trenching. The main boulevard (Suzaku-ōji) was approximately 70 metres wide.

**Residential areas**: Excavations in various locations have uncovered the foundations of aristocratic residences (with gardens and elaborate drainage) and commoner dwellings (simpler structures with earthen floors). The difference in material culture between wealthy and ordinary households is stark and informative.

**Markets**: The east and west markets (Higashi-ichi and Nishi-ichi), where goods were bought and sold, have been partially investigated. Finds include weights, measures, and the debris of commercial activity.

**Temples**: The archaeological investigation of Nara's temples has supplemented what is known from their standing structures. Excavations at Kōfuku-ji, Gangō-ji, and other temples have revealed earlier building phases, underground deposits, and objects that clarify the temples' histories.

**Craft workshops**: Production sites for ceramics, metalwork, and textiles have been identified, showing how the capital's material needs were met. Tile kilns, bronze-casting facilities, and weaving workshops operated within and around the city.

### The Challenge of Urban Archaeology

Much of the ancient city lies beneath modern buildings, roads, and private properties, making comprehensive excavation impossible. Archaeological investigation happens opportunistically — when land is developed, road work is undertaken, or buildings are demolished, archaeologists have a window to investigate what lies beneath.

This means that knowledge of Heijō-kyō accumulates gradually, piece by piece. Each new excavation adds to a jigsaw puzzle that will never be complete but that becomes more detailed with every passing year.

## Asuka and the Pre-Nara Sites

South of Nara, the Asuka region preserves archaeological sites from the period immediately before the Nara capital was established. These sites provide context for understanding how the Nara state developed.

**Asuka-dera**: The site of Japan's first true Buddhist temple (established 596 CE), where excavations have revealed the original layout and the remains of the temple's earliest structures.

**Fujiwara-kyō**: The capital that preceded Nara (694–710 CE), located between Asuka and Nara. The palace site has been excavated and is open to visitors, though less extensively developed for tourism than the Heijō site.

**Ishibutai Kofun**: A massive 7th-century stone burial chamber, probably the tomb of Soga no Umako, that has been fully exposed by the removal of its original earth mound. The engineering of the stone chamber — using boulders weighing up to 77 tonnes — is impressive evidence of pre-Nara construction capability.

**Takamatsuzuka and Kitora Tombs**: Two decorated tomb chambers from the late 7th to early 8th century, whose wall paintings depicting astronomical charts and figures in Tang-style clothing are among the most important archaeological discoveries in Japanese history. The murals are displayed in controlled museum environments near their original sites.

A day trip from Nara to the Asuka sites is highly recommended for visitors interested in archaeology. The area is best explored by bicycle, with rental shops available near Asuka Station.

## The Nara National Research Institute for Cultural Properties (Nabunken)

The institutional backbone of Nara's archaeology is Nabunken, the research institute that has led excavations at the Heijō Palace Site since 1959. Nabunken's work includes not only excavation but conservation, analysis, and publication of results.

The institute operates the Heijō Palace Site Museum and provides public access to ongoing research through exhibitions, lectures, and open excavation days. Their website publishes excavation reports and databases that are valuable resources for anyone interested in Nara's archaeological heritage.

## Planning an Archaeological Visit

For travellers who want to engage with Nara's archaeological dimension:

**Heijō Palace Site**: Essential. Allow two to three hours for the museum, reconstructed buildings, and the open site. Morning visits avoid afternoon heat in summer.

**Nara National Museum**: The museum's collection includes archaeological finds alongside temple art, providing context for both the monumental and the everyday aspects of Nara-period culture.

**Asuka day trip**: A full day by bicycle through the Asuka archaeological sites makes an excellent complement to Nara's temples. Take the Kintetsu line south to Asuka Station.

**Gangō-ji**: This temple in Naramachi preserves architectural elements from the Asuka period — roof tiles from the original Asuka-dera, moved to Nara when the capital was established. The small museum on the temple grounds displays archaeological finds from the surrounding area.

Staying overnight in Nara makes it possible to visit both the Heijō Palace Site (in the west) and the central temples, and to add an Asuka day trip without the fatigue of commuting from Osaka or Kyoto. Kanoya ryokan is well positioned for both the palace site and the central Nara Park area, and the quiet of a ryokan evening is conducive to processing the layers of history that archaeology reveals.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I visit the Heijō Palace archaeological site?

Yes, the Heijō Palace Site is open to the public free of charge. It includes reconstructed buildings (the Former Imperial Audience Hall and Suzaku Gate), a museum, and the East Palace Garden. Allow two to three hours.

### What are mokkan?

Mokkan are thin wooden tablets used for administrative records in Nara-period Japan. Over 350,000 have been recovered from the Heijō Palace Site. They record tax shipments, personnel lists, and daily administrative matters, providing invaluable information about 8th-century government and society.

### Are there ongoing excavations in Nara?

Yes, the Nara National Research Institute for Cultural Properties (Nabunken) conducts continuous archaeological work in and around Nara. Depending on timing, visitors may be able to observe active excavation areas at the Heijō Palace Site.

### How do I visit the Asuka archaeological sites?

Take the Kintetsu line from Nara to Asuka Station (approximately one hour). Rent a bicycle at the station and cycle between sites including Asuka-dera, Ishibutai Kofun, Takamatsuzuka Tomb, and the Fujiwara Palace Site. A full day is recommended.
