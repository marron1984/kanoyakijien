# Nara Food Allergies Guide: Navigating Japanese Cuisine Safely

Travelling with food allergies requires planning in any country. In Japan, the challenge is particular: the cuisine relies heavily on a handful of ingredients — soy, wheat, fish, shellfish, eggs — that are among the world's most common allergens. These ingredients are not merely occasional additions; they are structural, woven into nearly every dish through sauces, broths, and preparations that are not always visible to the diner.

The good news is that Japan takes food allergies seriously. Labelling laws are strict, restaurant staff are generally conscientious, and the cultural disposition toward precision and care extends to accommodating dietary needs when they are clearly communicated. The key phrase is "clearly communicated" — the language barrier makes this harder than it needs to be, and preparation before your trip is essential.

This guide covers the specific allergen challenges you will face in Nara, practical strategies for dining safely, and the resources available to help you navigate Japanese cuisine with confidence.

## Common Allergens in Japanese Cuisine

### Soy (Daizu — 大豆)

Soy is the most pervasive allergen in Japanese cooking. It appears in:

- **Soy sauce (shoyu)**: Used in virtually every savoury dish, including dashi broth, marinades, glazes, dipping sauces, and dressings. Present in almost all restaurant food.
- **Miso**: Fermented soybean paste used in miso soup (served with nearly every meal), glazes, dressings, and marinades.
- **Tofu**: In all its forms — silken, firm, deep-fried (agedashi dofu, aburaage), dried (koya-dofu), and freeze-dried.
- **Edamame**: Whole soybeans, boiled and salted. A common izakaya starter.
- **Natto**: Fermented soybeans, served at breakfast.
- **Kinako**: Roasted soybean powder, used in desserts and dusted on mochi.

**Severity of challenge**: Extreme. A true soy allergy makes eating in Japan genuinely difficult. Soy sauce and miso are used so ubiquitously that avoiding them requires constant vigilance and clear communication with every restaurant.

**Strategy**: Carry a printed allergy card in Japanese (see below). Some restaurants can substitute tamari (wheat-free soy sauce) but not soy-free alternatives. Allergic reactions to soy lecithin (used in processed foods) may be distinct from reactions to soy protein — consult your allergist about your specific sensitivities before travelling.

### Wheat (Komugi — 小麦)

Wheat is the second most difficult allergen to avoid in Japanese cuisine:

- **Soy sauce**: Standard soy sauce (koikuchi shoyu) is brewed with wheat. Tamari soy sauce is traditionally wheat-free, but not all brands are — verify before consuming.
- **Udon noodles**: Made from wheat flour.
- **Ramen noodles**: Made from wheat flour.
- **Tempura batter**: Wheat flour-based.
- **Panko breadcrumbs**: Used in tonkatsu, korokke, and other fried foods.
- **Fu (wheat gluten)**: Used in some soups and stews.
- **Curry roux**: Standard Japanese curry roux is made with wheat flour.

**Strategy**: Rice-based dishes are the safest foundation. Soba (buckwheat noodles) may be wheat-free, but many soba noodles contain a percentage of wheat flour — ask specifically for juwari soba (100% buckwheat) if buckwheat is safe for you. Note that even juwari soba may be cooked in shared water with wheat noodles.

### Fish and Shellfish (Sakana / Kai — 魚 / 貝)

Fish and shellfish allergens are widespread in Japanese cuisine, often in forms that are not immediately obvious:

- **Dashi**: The fundamental stock of Japanese cooking, made from katsuobushi (dried bonito flakes) and kombu (kelp). Dashi is in miso soup, simmered dishes, noodle broths, and many sauces. Bonito-based dashi contains fish allergens.
- **Fish sauce**: Used in some Kansai-style preparations.
- **Dried shrimp / dried fish**: Used as flavouring in many dishes, sometimes ground to invisibility.
- **Ebi (shrimp), kani (crab)**: Common ingredients in tempura, sushi, and various preparations.

**Strategy**: Kombu dashi (kelp-only dashi) and shiitake dashi (mushroom-based) are fish-free alternatives used in shojin ryori (Buddhist vegetarian cuisine). Restaurants that serve shojin ryori or vegetarian kaiseki are the safest options for those with fish or shellfish allergies. Always specify whether the allergy includes all fish, only shellfish, or specific species.

### Eggs (Tamago — 卵)

Eggs appear in Japanese cuisine in multiple forms:

- **Tamagoyaki**: Rolled omelette, a standard breakfast and bento item.
- **Ajitsuke tamago**: Seasoned soft-boiled egg, a common ramen topping.
- **Tempura batter**: Often contains egg.
- **Chawanmushi**: Savoury egg custard, a common kaiseki course.
- **Mayonnaise**: Used more widely in Japan than in many Western countries — on okonomiyaki, takoyaki, salads, and sandwiches.
- **Various sweets**: Many wagashi (Japanese confections) and Western-style pastries contain egg.

**Strategy**: Egg allergy is relatively manageable in Japanese cuisine compared to soy or wheat. Many traditional dishes (sushi, sashimi, rice bowls, grilled fish) are naturally egg-free. Communicate the allergy clearly, and most restaurants can navigate around egg-containing dishes.

### Other Allergens

- **Buckwheat (soba)**: A significant allergen for some people. Buckwheat noodle dust can be airborne in soba restaurants.
- **Peanuts and tree nuts**: Less common in traditional Japanese cuisine than in Chinese or Southeast Asian cooking, but sesame is pervasive (in dressings, toppings, and oil).
- **Sesame (goma)**: Used extensively in Japanese cooking — sesame oil, ground sesame, sesame dressing, sesame tofu. Difficult to avoid entirely.
- **Dairy**: Relatively uncommon in traditional Japanese cuisine but present in Western-influenced dishes, bakeries, and desserts.

## Practical Strategies for Dining with Allergies in Nara

### Allergy Communication Cards

The single most important tool for travelling with food allergies in Japan is a printed allergy card in Japanese. This card should clearly state your specific allergies using the Japanese terms, and it should be laminated or printed on durable card stock so you can present it at every restaurant.

A well-designed allergy card includes:

1. A clear statement: "I have a food allergy" (食物アレルギーがあります — shokumotsu arerugii ga arimasu)
2. A list of specific allergens, each written in Japanese with the kanji, hiragana, and a visual icon if possible
3. A statement of severity: "Eating this food will make me very sick" or "This is a life-threatening allergy" if applicable
4. A polite request: "Please tell me if any dishes contain these ingredients"

Several online services and apps produce customisable allergy cards in Japanese. Prepare these before your trip and carry multiple copies.

### Communicating with Restaurant Staff

When presenting your allergy card:

1. Show it before ordering, ideally before sitting down. Some small restaurants may honestly tell you they cannot safely accommodate your allergy — this is actually helpful information, not a rejection.
2. Ask specifically about hidden ingredients. In Japanese: "Kono ryouri ni [allergen] wa haitte imasu ka?" (この料理に[allergen]は入っていますか？) — "Does this dish contain [allergen]?"
3. Be patient and appreciative. Most restaurant staff in Nara will take your allergy seriously and make genuine efforts to help, but the process takes time, especially if the kitchen needs to check ingredients.

### Leverage Your Accommodation

For visitors staying at a ryokan or hotel, the front desk and concierge staff are invaluable allies. They can:

- Call restaurants in advance to explain your allergies in Japanese
- Help you identify safe menu items before you arrive
- Communicate with the kitchen about your specific needs for in-house meals

Kanoya ryokan, like most high-end Japanese accommodation, takes allergy management seriously. When booking, provide detailed allergy information, and the kitchen will adjust the kaiseki menu accordingly — substituting ingredients, modifying preparations, and sometimes creating entirely alternative courses. This level of personalisation is one of the advantages of ryokan dining over restaurant dining for allergy-conscious travellers.

### Safe Food Categories

Certain types of Japanese food are inherently safer for common allergies:

- **Plain rice (gohan)**: Naturally free of all major allergens.
- **Onigiri with simple fillings**: Rice balls filled with salted salmon, umeboshi (pickled plum), or konbu. Check for soy sauce in the filling.
- **Grilled meat or fish with salt only (shioyaki)**: Ask for no sauce (tare nashi).
- **Shojin ryori**: Buddhist vegetarian cuisine, which avoids all animal products and can often accommodate additional restrictions.
- **Fresh fruit**: Widely available and allergen-free.

### Higher-Risk Situations

- **Izakaya**: Multiple shared dishes with varied ingredients make allergen tracking difficult. Communicate allergies for each dish ordered.
- **Ramen shops**: Broth ingredients are often proprietary and may not be fully disclosed. Ask before ordering.
- **Conveyor belt sushi (kaiten-zushi)**: Cross-contamination between items is possible on the belt.
- **Street food**: Vendors may not be able to provide detailed ingredient information.

## Japan's Allergen Labelling System

Japan mandates labelling of seven specific allergens on packaged foods: wheat, buckwheat, egg, milk, peanut, shrimp, and crab. An additional 21 items are recommended for labelling, including soy, various tree nuts, sesame, salmon, mackerel, and more.

This system is helpful for convenience store and supermarket food, where ingredient labels are printed on packaging. Look for the allergen section, typically highlighted in a box near the ingredient list. The seven mandatory allergens are always listed when present.

For restaurant food, there is no legal requirement to provide allergen information, though many chain restaurants do. Independent restaurants in Nara typically do not have printed allergen menus but will respond to direct questions.

## Emergency Preparedness

- **Carry your medication**: EpiPens, antihistamines, and any prescribed emergency medication should be on your person at all times.
- **Know the emergency number**: 119 (fire and ambulance) in Japan.
- **Hospital phrase**: "Shokumotsu arerugii desu. Kyuukyuu desu." (食物アレルギーです。救急です。) — "It's a food allergy. It's an emergency."
- **Nara has hospitals**: Nara Medical University Hospital and Nara Prefectural General Medical Centre both have emergency departments.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I eat sushi with a soy allergy?
Sushi rice typically contains rice vinegar (which may or may not include a small amount of soy-derived ingredients depending on the brand). The sushi itself is served with soy sauce for dipping, which can be declined. Ask the chef to serve nigiri without soy-based seasoning and provide salt or citrus as an alternative.

### Is miso soup safe for someone with a fish allergy?
Standard miso soup is made with dashi (fish-based stock) and miso (soy-based paste), so it contains both fish and soy allergens. Request kombu dashi (kelp stock) miso soup if only the fish is the concern.

### What does a food allergy card look like in Japanese?
*A Japanese food allergy card (arerugii kaado) is a printed card that states "I have a food allergy" (食物アレルギーがあります) followed by a clear list of specific allergens written in Japanese kanji and hiragana — it is the single most effective tool for communicating dietary restrictions in Japan, where the language barrier makes verbal communication unreliable.*

### Can kaiseki restaurants accommodate food allergies?
Yes, with advance notice. Kaiseki restaurants prepare a fixed menu, so allergen modifications must be communicated at the time of reservation, not at the table. Most kaiseki chefs can substitute or modify courses when given adequate warning.

### Are there any allergy-friendly restaurant chains in Nara?
Some national chains (CoCo Ichibanya curry, Mos Burger, Gusto) provide allergen information on their menus or websites. While these are not the most curated Nara dining experiences, they offer a reliable safety net on days when allergy management feels overwhelming.
