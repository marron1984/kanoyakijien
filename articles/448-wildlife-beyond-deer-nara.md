# Wildlife Beyond Deer: Tanuki, Foxes, and Forest Creatures of Nara

The deer of Nara are so famous that they monopolize nearly all attention directed at the city's wildlife. Over 1,200 sika deer wander Nara Park, bow for crackers, and appear in every photograph. But Nara's ecological story is far richer than a single species. The ancient forests of Kasugayama — protected from logging for over a thousand years — and the mountains and valleys of the surrounding Yamato region support a diversity of wildlife that most visitors never notice. Tanuki, foxes, wild boar, giant salamanders, raptors, and dozens of other species live in and around one of Japan's oldest cities, often mere minutes from the temple precincts where tourists congregate.

## The Protected Forests

### Kasugayama Primeval Forest

The forest behind Kasuga Taisha is one of the most remarkable ecological preserves in any urban area in the world. Designated a UNESCO World Heritage site as part of the "Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara," Kasugayama Primeval Forest has been protected from tree cutting since 841 CE — nearly 1,200 years of continuous conservation. The result is a climax forest of extraordinary maturity, with towering trees, dense understory, and ecological complexity that provides habitat for species that have long since disappeared from most of Japan's urban and suburban landscapes.

The forest covers approximately 250 hectares and includes over 175 species of trees, ranging from warm-temperate evergreen broadleaf species at lower elevations to cool-temperate deciduous species higher up. This vegetative diversity supports a correspondingly diverse animal community. The forest is open to hikers on designated trails, and walking its paths with attention to wildlife is one of the most underappreciated experiences Nara offers.

### The Yamato Highlands

Beyond the immediate environs of Nara city, the mountains of Nara Prefecture — including the Ōmine range, the Yoshino mountains, and the Katsuragi range — support populations of larger and more elusive species that occasionally range into the edges of the Nara basin. These mountains are among the least developed in the Kansai region, and their forests provide critical habitat for species under pressure elsewhere in Japan.

## Tanuki: The Trickster Raccoon Dog

### Biology and Behavior

The tanuki (Nyctereutes procyonoides) is not a raccoon, despite the common English name "raccoon dog." It is a member of the canid family — related to dogs and foxes — that has convergently evolved a raccoon-like appearance. Tanuki are nocturnal omnivores, eating everything from insects and frogs to fruits, seeds, and human garbage. They are stocky, round-faced, and possessed of a waddling gait that contributes to their reputation in Japanese folklore as comical, bumbling, and lovable.

Tanuki are present in the forests and parks of Nara, though seeing one requires either luck or deliberate nocturnal exploration. They are most active at dusk and dawn, and they tend to avoid areas of heavy human foot traffic. The forested areas around Kasuga Taisha and the quieter sections of Nara Park are the most likely spots for sightings.

### Tanuki in Japanese Culture

The tanuki occupies an enormous space in Japanese folklore, far exceeding its modest biological profile. Tanuki are legendary shapeshifters, capable of transforming themselves into people, objects, or other animals to play tricks on humans. They are depicted in folklore as sake-loving, jovial tricksters — mischievous but rarely malicious. The ceramic tanuki statues found outside restaurants and shops throughout Japan — rotund, straw-hatted, holding a sake flask and displaying famously exaggerated anatomy — are derived from these legends.

In Nara, tanuki legends are woven into the folklore of rural communities in the hills surrounding the city. Some temples and shrines maintain tanuki-related traditions, and the occasional ceramic tanuki appears at establishments in Naramachi.

## Kitsune: The Fox

### The Japanese Red Fox

The Japanese red fox (Vulpes vulpes japonica) is native to the Nara region and present in forests and rural areas throughout the prefecture. Foxes are more solitary and more secretive than tanuki, and sightings are correspondingly rarer. They are most active at dawn and dusk, hunting small mammals, birds, and insects in forest clearings and along the edges of agricultural fields.

The hills behind Kasugayama and the rural areas east and south of Nara city support fox populations. Visitors hiking in these areas, particularly in the quiet hours of early morning, may catch a glimpse of a fox crossing a trail or watching from the undergrowth.

### Kitsune in Shinto and Folklore

In Shinto, the fox is the messenger of Inari, the kami of rice, fertility, and commerce. Fox statues (komainu-style guardian foxes) flank the entrances of Inari shrines, and foxes are believed to possess supernatural powers, including the ability to shapeshift and to possess humans. Unlike the tanuki, whose trickery is mostly harmless, the fox in Japanese folklore can be genuinely dangerous — a creature of beauty, intelligence, and occasionally malevolent cunning.

Nara has several Inari shrines where fox symbolism is prominent. These shrines, often marked by tunnels of vermilion torii gates (though on a smaller scale than Kyoto's famous Fushimi Inari), are dedicated to the same kami and feature the same fox imagery. Visiting them adds a wildlife-cultural dimension to a Nara itinerary.

## Wild Boar: The Mountain Dweller

### Inoshishi in the Nara Hills

Japanese wild boar (Sus scrofa leucomystax) are abundant in the mountains of Nara Prefecture, and their numbers have increased significantly in recent decades as rural depopulation has reduced hunting pressure and expanded their available habitat. Wild boar are powerful, intelligent animals that can weigh over 100 kilograms and move with surprising speed.

While wild boar rarely venture into the central tourist areas of Nara city, they are common in the forested hills and rural valleys of the surrounding region. Hikers on mountain trails in the Kasugayama area and in the hills south and east of the city may encounter signs of boar activity — rooted-up earth, wallowing pits, tracks in mud — and occasionally the animals themselves.

### Safety Considerations

Wild boar should be observed from a distance and never approached or fed. Sows with piglets can be aggressive. If you encounter a boar on a trail, do not run — back away slowly and give the animal an escape route. Encounters are rare on the main tourist paths and more likely on less-traveled mountain trails.

## Smaller Creatures

### Japanese Giant Salamander

The Japanese giant salamander (Andrias japonicus) — ōsanshōuo — is one of the world's largest amphibians, reaching lengths of up to 1.5 meters. This ancient species, essentially unchanged for millions of years, lives in clean, cold mountain streams. Nara Prefecture's mountain waterways, particularly in the Yoshino and Ōmine areas, support populations of giant salamanders.

Encountering a giant salamander in the wild requires visiting specific mountain streams and is uncommon for casual visitors. However, the species is culturally significant — it is designated a Special Natural Monument of Japan — and awareness of its presence adds depth to the understanding of Nara's ecological heritage.

### Flying Squirrels

The Japanese dwarf flying squirrel (Pteromys momonga) inhabits the forests of Nara Prefecture. These tiny, nocturnal mammals glide between trees on membrane extensions of their skin. They are achingly cute — enormous dark eyes dominate a face the size of a golf ball — and nearly impossible to see without specific knowledge of their roost sites and active patience at dusk.

### Serow

The Japanese serow (Capricornis crispus), a goat-antelope native to Japan's mountains, is present in the more rugged terrain of Nara Prefecture. Designated a Special Natural Monument, the serow is a solitary, elusive animal that inhabits steep, forested mountainsides. Sightings are uncommon but not unheard of on hiking trails in the Ōmine range.

### Raptors and Forest Birds

The forests of Nara support a rich bird community beyond the common species visible in the parks. Northern goshawks hunt in the Kasugayama forest. Japanese buzzards soar over the Nara basin. In the mountains, Hodgson's hawk-eagles and mountain hawk-eagles — both rare and protected — patrol territories that have been occupied continuously for generations.

Forest songbirds include the Japanese bush warbler (uguisu), whose distinctive call is one of the iconic sounds of Japanese spring; the Japanese green woodpecker; the varied tit; and the narcissus flycatcher. Winter brings Daurian redstarts, bull-headed shrikes, and occasionally uncommon species that wander south from more northerly regions.

## Where and When to Look

### Timing

Most of Nara's non-deer wildlife is crepuscular or nocturnal. Early morning — the first hour after dawn — and late evening are the most productive times for wildlife observation. Guests at accommodations like Kanoya ryokan, who can begin their mornings with an early walk through the Naramachi neighborhood and into Nara Park before the tourist crowds arrive, have the best chance of encountering wildlife activity that daytime visitors never see.

### Locations

- **Kasugayama Primeval Forest**: The richest wildlife habitat near central Nara. Trails through the forest provide access to mature woodland where birds, tanuki, and foxes are present.
- **Mount Wakakusa**: The open grassland and forest margins of this hill support different species than the dense forest of Kasugayama.
- **Sagi-ike Pond and Aonigai-ike Pond**: Water bodies in and near Nara Park attract herons, kingfishers, turtles, and waterfowl.
- **Rural areas south of Nara**: The agricultural and forested landscape between Nara city and the Yoshino mountains supports the widest range of species, including wild boar and serow.

### Ethics and Etiquette

Do not feed wild animals (the deer in Nara Park, fed with officially sanctioned crackers, are the single exception). Do not pursue animals for photographs. Stay on designated trails in protected areas. Report any injured animals to park authorities. The wildlife of Nara has survived alongside human civilization for centuries because of cultural practices of restraint and respect — extending those practices as a visitor is both ethical and practical.

## A Richer Ecological Picture

The deer of Nara Park are wonderful, and no visitor should miss them. But understanding that the deer exist within a larger ecological community — that the forests shelter tanuki and foxes, that the streams hold giant salamanders, that raptors patrol the skies above the temple roofs — enriches the experience of Nara immeasurably. The ancient capital is not just a human cultural achievement. It is a place where, through centuries of protection and coexistence, a remarkably diverse natural community continues to thrive within sight of some of the world's oldest wooden buildings.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What wildlife besides deer can I see in Nara?
Nara's forests and parks are home to tanuki (raccoon dogs), Japanese red foxes, wild boar, flying squirrels, giant salamanders, numerous bird species including raptors, and many other creatures.

### Are tanuki really raccoons?
No. Despite being called "raccoon dogs" in English, tanuki are members of the canid (dog) family. They convergently evolved a raccoon-like appearance but are more closely related to foxes and wolves than to raccoons.

### Is the wildlife in Nara dangerous?
*Most of Nara's wildlife poses no danger to visitors. Wild boar, found in the surrounding mountains rather than the central tourist areas, should be observed from a distance and never approached. The famous sika deer can occasionally bite or butt visitors who tease them with food. Venomous snakes (mamushi) exist in rural areas but encounters are rare. Overall, basic awareness and keeping a respectful distance from wild animals is sufficient for safe wildlife observation.*

### When is the best time to see wildlife in Nara?
Early morning (within an hour of dawn) and late evening offer the best chances, as most species besides deer are crepuscular or nocturnal. Spring and autumn provide comfortable temperatures and active animal behavior.

### What is the Kasugayama Primeval Forest?
Kasugayama Primeval Forest is a 250-hectare UNESCO World Heritage forest behind Kasuga Taisha shrine, protected from logging since 841 CE. It supports over 175 tree species and a diverse animal community, making it one of the most remarkable urban wildlife habitats in the world.
