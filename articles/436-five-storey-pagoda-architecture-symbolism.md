# The Five-Storey Pagoda Explained: Architecture and Symbolism

The five-storey pagoda of Kofuku-ji stands at the edge of Sarusawa Pond, its silhouette reflected in the still water, and it has become the most recognizable image of Nara. Postcards, guidebooks, and tourism campaigns return to it incessantly. Yet most visitors who photograph the pagoda and admire its proportions know remarkably little about what it is, why it has five storeys, how it has survived 600 years of earthquakes, or what it means.

A pagoda is not a tower. It is not a temple. It is not, in the conventional sense, a building at all — no one lives in it, no services are conducted inside it, and its interior spaces, where they exist, are not designed for human occupation. A pagoda is a reliquary: a monumental container for sacred objects, descended from the Indian stupa and transformed by centuries of architectural evolution as Buddhism traveled through China and Korea to Japan. Understanding the pagoda requires understanding this lineage — the journey from a hemispherical mound of earth in India to a soaring wooden tower in Nara.

## From Stupa to Pagoda: A Structural Journey

### The Indian Stupa

The pagoda's ancestor is the stupa, a dome-shaped structure built to enshrine relics of the Buddha or other sacred objects. The earliest stupas were simple mounds of earth or brick, topped by a parasol-like finial that symbolized the Buddha's royalty. The faithful circumambulated the stupa — walking around it clockwise — as an act of devotion. They did not enter it. The stupa was solid, its contents sealed within.

As Buddhism spread beyond India, the stupa's form was adapted to local architectural traditions. In China, where multi-storey timber construction was already highly developed, the hemispherical stupa was translated into a vertical form — the tower pagoda. The dome became a series of stacked storeys, each with its own roof, and the finial was elongated into the tall metal spire that crowns every pagoda. The function remained the same: a container for relics, an object of veneration, a focus for circumambulation.

### The Chinese Transformation

Chinese pagodas introduced several elements that would become standard in Japan. The square or hexagonal floor plan, the diminishing storey sizes that create a tapered profile, the projecting eaves with upturned corners, and the central pillar (shinbashira) that runs through the structure's core — all of these features were Chinese innovations, adapted from indigenous timber-frame building techniques.

The transition from stone and brick (common in Chinese pagodas) to all-timber construction occurred primarily in Japan, where abundant forests and sophisticated woodworking traditions made wood the natural building material. This shift to timber construction transformed the pagoda from a solid, massive structure into a flexible, resilient one — a change that would prove critical in Japan's earthquake-prone environment.

### Arrival in Japan

The pagoda arrived in Japan with Buddhism in the sixth century. The earliest Japanese pagodas were modeled on Korean prototypes, which were themselves adapted from Chinese examples. Horyuji's five-storey pagoda, built in the early seventh century and the oldest surviving wooden pagoda in the world, represents the earliest phase of Japanese pagoda architecture. It is compact, slightly irregular in its proportions, and retains visible connections to its continental predecessors.

By the Nara period (710-794), Japanese builders had fully absorbed the pagoda form and began introducing distinctive refinements. The proportions were adjusted — Japanese pagodas tend to be more vertically emphatic than their Chinese counterparts, with a stronger taper and more dramatically projecting eaves. The timber joinery became more sophisticated, and the relationship between the pagoda and its surrounding temple compound was formalized into the characteristic layouts that define Japanese Buddhist architecture.

## Why Five Storeys?

### The Five Elements

The five storeys of a gojunoto (five-storey pagoda) correspond to the five elements of Buddhist cosmology: earth, water, fire, wind, and void (or sky). From bottom to top:

- **First storey (chi/earth):** The foundation, representing solidity and stability
- **Second storey (sui/water):** Representing fluidity and adaptability
- **Third storey (ka/fire):** Representing energy and transformation
- **Fourth storey (fu/wind):** Representing movement and expansion
- **Fifth storey (ku/void):** Representing emptiness, the formless essence from which all things arise

The finial (sorin) that extends above the fifth storey represents the realm beyond the physical elements — the transcendent reality of enlightenment. Its rings (typically nine) correspond to the nine heavens of Buddhist cosmology.

This cosmological mapping means that the pagoda is not merely a building but a three-dimensional diagram of the universe. Each storey is a world, and the progression from earth to void mirrors the spiritual journey from material existence to enlightenment. The devotee who gazes upward at the pagoda's ascending storeys is, symbolically, looking at the path of awakening.

### Variations in Storey Count

Not all pagodas have five storeys. Three-storey pagodas (sanjunoto) are also common in Japan, and some temples have two-storey, seven-storey, or even thirteen-storey pagodas. The three-storey pagoda maps onto a simplified three-element scheme (heaven, earth, and humanity), while pagodas with more storeys elaborate the cosmological symbolism further. The five-storey form, however, is considered the most complete and canonical expression of pagoda architecture.

## Engineering Genius: How Pagodas Survive Earthquakes

### The Shinbashira

The most remarkable feature of Japanese pagoda construction is invisible from the outside. Running through the center of the pagoda, from the foundation to the finial, is a massive timber column called the shinbashira (heart pillar). In many pagodas, this pillar is not structurally connected to the surrounding floors. It hangs from the top of the structure or stands on its own foundation, independent of the walls and floors around it.

This seemingly bizarre arrangement is the key to the pagoda's earthquake resistance. During seismic activity, the shinbashira and the surrounding structure sway independently, out of phase with each other. The pillar acts as a giant pendulum, counteracting the building's lateral movement. This is, in principle, the same technology used in modern skyscraper engineering — a tuned mass damper — but it was developed empirically by Japanese carpenters more than a thousand years before engineers articulated the physics behind it.

### Flexible Joinery

The timber joints in a pagoda are not rigid. They are designed to flex under stress, absorbing seismic energy through friction and deformation rather than resisting it through strength. The projecting eaves, which appear decorative, also serve a structural function: their weight pushes downward on the storey below, increasing the friction between stacked elements and helping the structure settle back into alignment after being displaced by an earthquake.

The result is a structure that moves with earthquakes rather than resisting them. This flexibility is why Japan has never lost a pagoda to earthquake damage. Fire, war, and lightning have destroyed many pagodas over the centuries, but the structures themselves are essentially earthquake-proof — a record that no other building type in any seismic zone can match.

## Nara's Pagodas

### Kofuku-ji Gojunoto

Kofuku-ji's five-storey pagoda is the second tallest in Japan at 50.1 meters and was originally built in 730 CE by Empress Komyo. The current structure dates to 1426, the fifth reconstruction following repeated fires. Despite its relatively late date, the pagoda maintains the proportional system and structural principles of the Nara period, making it a faithful expression of eighth-century design philosophy.

The pagoda underwent major restoration between 2023 and 2031, during which scaffolding surrounded the structure. Visitors during the restoration period could observe traditional carpentry techniques being applied to a national treasure — a rare opportunity to see the living craft tradition that sustains these structures.

The pagoda's reflection in Sarusawa Pond is one of the most photographed scenes in Nara. The best time to capture it is in the early morning, when the water is still and the light comes from the east, illuminating the pagoda's eastern face while the pond remains in shadow.

### Horyuji Gojunoto

Approximately 12 kilometers southwest of central Nara, Horyuji's five-storey pagoda is the oldest wooden pagoda in the world. Built around 607 CE and reconstructed in its current form in the late seventh century, it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and a supreme achievement of early Japanese architecture.

The Horyuji pagoda is smaller and more compact than Kofuku-ji's, with a pronounced taper that gives it a sense of upward movement. Its interior — rarely opened to the public — contains clay tableaux depicting scenes from the life of the Buddha, dating to 711 CE and representing some of the oldest surviving sculptural art in Japan.

### Yakushi-ji's East Pagoda

Yakushi-ji's East Pagoda, dating to approximately 730 CE, is the only original Nara-period pagoda structure surviving in central Nara. Its distinctive appearance — it looks as though it has six storeys due to the addition of decorative lean-to roofs (mokoshi) between the true storeys — led the American scholar Ernest Fenollosa to describe it as "frozen music." The alternation of large and small roofs creates a visual rhythm that is indeed musical in its regularity and variation.

## Visiting Nara's Pagodas

The pagodas of Nara reward both distant and close observation. From a distance — across Sarusawa Pond, from the hills of Nara Park, from the streets of Naramachi — they serve as landmarks and compositional elements in the landscape. Up close, the details of their construction become visible: the complexity of the bracket systems, the grain of the weathered timber, the curve of the eaves, the finial glinting against the sky.

For visitors staying at Kanoya ryokan in Naramachi, Kofuku-ji's pagoda is visible from many vantage points in the neighborhood and is a short walk away. The experience of seeing it at different times of day — sharp against morning blue, gilded by afternoon sun, silhouetted at dusk — provides an education in how Japanese architecture was designed to interact with natural light.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do Japanese pagodas never fall in earthquakes?

The combination of a central heart pillar (shinbashira) that acts as a pendulum damper, flexible timber joinery that absorbs seismic energy, and heavy projecting eaves that stabilize each storey makes pagodas remarkably earthquake-resistant. No historic pagoda in Japan has ever collapsed due to earthquake damage.

### Can you go inside a pagoda?

Generally, no. The interiors of most pagodas are closed to the public. The spaces are small, structurally significant, and often contain sacred relics or artwork. Some temples open pagoda interiors on special occasions.

### What is at the top of a pagoda?

The finial (sorin) crowns every pagoda and typically consists of a metal spire with nine rings, a water flame ornament, and a jewel at the apex. It symbolizes the spiritual realms above the material world represented by the five storeys.

### How old is the oldest pagoda in Japan?

*The oldest surviving wooden pagoda in the world is at Horyuji, approximately 12 kilometers from central Nara. Built in the late seventh century, it has stood for over 1,300 years — a testament to the earthquake-resistant engineering of Japanese pagoda construction and the enduring skill of the carpenters who maintain it.*
