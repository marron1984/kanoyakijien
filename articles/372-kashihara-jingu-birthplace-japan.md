# Kashihara Jingū and the Birthplace of Japan Mythology

At the foot of Mount Unebi, in the southern reaches of the Nara basin, stands Kashihara Jingu — the shrine that marks what Japanese mythology identifies as the place where the nation itself began. According to the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki, Japan's oldest chronicles, it was here that Emperor Jimmu, the legendary first emperor, established his throne in 660 BCE, founding a dynasty that, by tradition, continues unbroken to the present day. Whether one reads this as historical fact, national mythology, or something in between, Kashihara Jingu occupies a unique position in the Japanese cultural landscape: it is, quite literally, the shrine dedicated to the founding of Japan.

The shrine is a striking half-day excursion from Nara, combining grand Shinto architecture, extensive forested grounds, and a confrontation with the deepest layers of Japanese national identity. It pairs naturally with a visit to nearby Asuka Village, making the southern Nara basin one of the most historically dense day-trip destinations in the Kansai region.

## The Jimmu Legend

The story of Emperor Jimmu is one of Japan's foundational myths. According to the chronicles compiled in the early eighth century, Jimmu was a descendant of the sun goddess Amaterasu. He embarked on an eastward military campaign from Kyushu, fighting his way through western Honshu before arriving in the Yamato region — present-day Nara Prefecture. After subduing local rulers, he established his palace at the foot of Mount Unebi and performed the ceremony of enthronement in what the traditional calendar dates to 11 February 660 BCE.

Modern historians treat this account as legend rather than history. There is no archaeological evidence for a unified Japanese state in the seventh century BCE, and the Jimmu narrative was almost certainly composed centuries after the supposed events to legitimise the ruling dynasty. But the story's historical accuracy is, in a sense, beside the point. The Jimmu legend shaped Japanese political culture for over a millennium, and Kashihara Jingu — established in 1890 during the Meiji period's programme of State Shinto — was built to give this founding myth a physical home.

## The Shrine's Meiji-Era Origins

Kashihara Jingu is not an ancient shrine. It was established in 1890, on a site identified through textual research and imperial decree as the location of Jimmu's enthronement. The main hall (honden) was relocated from the Kyoto Imperial Palace, giving it architectural distinction despite the shrine's relative youth. The decision to build the shrine was part of the Meiji government's broader effort to construct a unified national identity centred on the emperor and the Shinto religion.

This context is important for visitors to understand. Kashihara Jingu is not a medieval pilgrimage site or an organically evolved sacred place; it is a deliberate creation of modern Japanese nationalism. This does not diminish its beauty or its significance, but it does lend the shrine a particular character — monumental, purposeful, and closely tied to the political currents of modern Japanese history.

The shrine was expanded significantly in 1940 to mark the 2,600th anniversary of Jimmu's legendary enthronement. The celebration, held amid Japan's wartime mobilisation, drew eight million visitors and became one of the largest public events in Japanese history. After the war, Kashihara Jingu was separated from state sponsorship along with all other Shinto shrines, but it retained its prominence as a centre of New Year worship and a site of national cultural significance.

## Visiting the Shrine

### The Approach

The walk from Kashihara Jingumae Station to the shrine takes about ten minutes along a broad, tree-lined avenue. The approach is intentionally grand, designed to create a sense of increasing solemnity as you move from the commercial district toward the sacred precinct. The first torii gate marks the transition, and the gravel path widens as it passes through dense forest — predominantly Japanese cypress and camphor trees — that screens the shrine from the surrounding city.

### The Outer and Inner Precincts

Kashihara Jingu's layout follows the classic Shinto model of progressive approach through nested boundaries. The outer precinct (gaien) contains a large open courtyard, a hand-washing basin (temizuya), and auxiliary buildings. Beyond a second torii, the inner precinct (naien) houses the haiden (worship hall) and the honden (main hall), the latter visible only partially from the public approach.

The architecture is in the Kashihara-zukuri style, which draws on the ancient Unebiyama-no-fumoto palace style described in early texts. The honden, transferred from the Kyoto Imperial Palace, is an elegant structure of unpainted cypress wood with a copper roof that has weathered to a soft verdigris. The overall effect is austere and powerful — less ornate than many Shinto shrines, but compelling in its restrained grandeur.

### The Forested Grounds

The shrine's grounds extend across approximately fifty hectares of forest and garden. Walking paths wind through towering trees, past moss-covered stone lanterns and secondary shrines. The Shinkyokan (Sacred Garden), located to the west of the main precinct, contains a traditional pond garden and seasonal plantings. In spring, cherry blossoms line the approach; in autumn, the forest canopy turns gold and crimson.

The grounds are large enough to absorb crowds, and outside of New Year and National Foundation Day (11 February), the shrine can feel remarkably tranquil — a place where the sounds of the city fade and the forest asserts itself.

### The Kashihara Archaeological Institute Museum

Adjacent to the shrine, the Nara Prefectural Kashihara Archaeological Institute Museum houses one of the finest collections of archaeological finds in the Kansai region. Exhibits span from the Jomon period through the Nara period, with particular strength in Kofun-era burial goods, Yayoi-period ceramics, and artefacts from the Fujiwara Palace excavations. The museum provides essential context for understanding the layers of civilisation that underlie the Nara basin. Allow at least an hour.

## Mount Unebi

Rising behind the shrine, Mount Unebi is one of the three sacred mountains of Yamato (Yamato Sanzan), along with Mount Miminashi and Mount Amanokagu. At just 199 metres, it is a gentle climb, and a trail from the shrine grounds reaches the summit in about thirty minutes. The forested slopes are peaceful and seldom crowded, and the summit offers views across the Nara basin to the surrounding mountains. Emperor Jimmu's tomb (Jimmu Tenno Ryo) is located on the northeastern slope — a quiet, immaculately maintained site administered by the Imperial Household Agency.

## Cultural Context: National Foundation Day

Every 11 February, Kashihara Jingu becomes the centre of National Foundation Day (Kenkoku Kinen no Hi) celebrations. This public holiday, which commemorates Jimmu's legendary enthronement, draws large crowds to the shrine for special rituals, processions, and performances. The holiday has a complex political history — it was abolished during the American occupation and restored in 1966 — and it remains a subject of debate in Japanese political discourse. For visitors, it offers an opportunity to see the shrine at its most ceremonially active, though the crowds can be intense.

New Year's worship (hatsumode) also brings enormous numbers, with over a million visitors during the first three days of January. Outside these peak periods, the shrine is far quieter and more conducive to contemplative exploration.

## Getting There from Nara

From Kintetsu Nara Station, take the Kintetsu Nara Line to Yamato-Saidaiji, transfer to the Kintetsu Kashihara Line, and ride to Kashihara Jingumae Station. The journey takes approximately thirty-five minutes and costs around 510 yen. The station is the terminus of the line and difficult to miss.

From JR Nara Station, the JR Sakurai Line runs to Unebi Station, from which the shrine is a fifteen-minute walk. The Kintetsu route is generally more convenient.

## Practical Information

**Hours**: The shrine grounds are open from sunrise to sunset. The inner precinct typically opens at 6:00 and closes between 17:00 and 18:00 depending on the season.

**Admission**: Free. The adjacent museum charges a separate admission fee (around 400 yen for adults).

**Time needed**: One to two hours for the shrine itself; add an hour for the museum and thirty minutes for Mount Unebi if desired.

**Combine with**: Asuka Village is two stops south on the Kintetsu Line. Visiting both Kashihara Jingu and Asuka in a single day is very feasible and creates a rich thematic day exploring Japan's earliest legendary and historical capitals.

## Returning to Nara

After a day spent in the mythological and archaeological depths of the southern Nara basin, the return to Nara city proper feels like resurfacing from a deep immersion. Guests staying at Kanoya ryokan can be back in the historic centre within forty minutes, trading ancient stone chambers and cypress forests for the quiet comforts of tatami rooms and kaiseki dining — a fitting conclusion to a day that spans the full arc of Japanese civilisation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is Kashihara Jingu?

Kashihara Jingu is a major Shinto shrine in Nara Prefecture, established in 1890 on the site where Emperor Jimmu, Japan's legendary first emperor, is said to have ascended the throne in 660 BCE. It is closely associated with Japan's founding mythology.

### How do I get to Kashihara Jingu from Nara?

Take the Kintetsu Railway from Kintetsu Nara Station, transferring at Yamato-Saidaiji to the Kashihara Line. The journey to Kashihara Jingumae Station takes approximately thirty-five minutes and costs around 510 yen.

### Can I visit Kashihara Jingu and Asuka Village in one day?

Yes. Kashihara Jingu and Asuka Village are only two stations apart on the Kintetsu Line. A morning at the shrine followed by an afternoon cycling through Asuka makes an excellent full-day itinerary.

### Is Kashihara Jingu an ancient shrine?

No. Despite its association with ancient mythology, Kashihara Jingu was established in 1890 during the Meiji period. Its main hall, however, was transferred from the Kyoto Imperial Palace and dates to an earlier period.
