# Nara's Role in Japanese Papermaking History

When the Empress Shotoku ordered one million small wooden pagodas to be produced in 770 CE, each containing a printed Buddhist dharani (prayer scroll), the paper those scrolls were printed on was made in Nara. These Hyakumanto Darani are the oldest surviving examples of printed text in Japan — and among the oldest in the world. They survive because the paper they were printed on was made with such skill that it has endured for over 1,250 years.

This is not an accident. Nara was the capital of Japan from 710 to 784, and during that period, the imperial court, Buddhist temples, and government bureaucracy created an enormous demand for paper. Sutras needed copying. Edicts needed recording. Administrative records needed filing. The result was the development of a sophisticated papermaking tradition that influenced Japanese washi production for centuries to come.

## The Arrival of Papermaking in Japan

Paper was invented in China around 105 CE and reached the Korean peninsula within a few centuries. The traditional account credits the Buddhist monk Doncho with introducing papermaking to Japan in 610 CE, during the reign of Empress Suiko. Whether or not Doncho was a single historical figure, the technology arrived in Japan through Korean and Chinese transmission during the 6th and 7th centuries.

The earliest Japanese paper was modelled on Chinese methods — using hemp (asa) and mulberry fibre, formed on a dipping mould. But Japanese papermakers soon adapted the techniques to local materials and needs, developing what would become a distinctly Japanese approach to paper production.

## Nara Period: The Golden Age of State Papermaking

When the capital moved to Nara (then called Heijo-kyo) in 710, the demand for paper became institutional. The Nara court established the Zusho-ryo, a government bureau responsible for paper production and book-making. This bureau supervised papermaking operations and sourced raw materials from provinces across the country.

The scale was significant. The great temples of Nara — Todai-ji, Kofuku-ji, Yakushi-ji, Toshodai-ji — each required vast quantities of paper for sutra copying. The Todai-ji sutra-copying bureau (Shakyosho) employed hundreds of scribes, and their output required a continuous supply of high-quality paper.

### Materials and Methods

Nara-period paper was made primarily from three plant fibres:

**Kozo (paper mulberry, Broussonetia papyrifera)**: The strongest and most durable fibre, producing paper that could last centuries. Kozo became the dominant material for important documents and sutras.

**Gampi (Wikstroemia diplomorpha)**: A wild shrub that produces a fine, lustrous paper with a naturally smooth surface. Gampi paper has a slight sheen and is resistant to insects — qualities that made it prized for calligraphy and official documents.

**Mitsumata (Edgeworthia chrysantha)**: A softer fibre producing a delicate, absorbent paper. Used for less formal documents and, later, for printing.

The basic process — soaking, boiling, beating the fibres to a pulp, forming sheets on a bamboo screen, pressing, and drying — was established during this period. But Japanese papermakers made a critical innovation that distinguished their technique from the Chinese method.

### Nagashi-zuki: The Japanese Pour-off Technique

Chinese papermaking used a "dipping" method (tame-zuki): the mould was dipped into the vat of pulp and the water allowed to drain through. Japanese makers developed nagashi-zuki, a "pour-off" method in which the pulp mixture is repeatedly scooped onto the screen, rocked back and forth to interlock the fibres, and the excess poured off. A key ingredient, neri — a viscous mucilage made from tororo-aoi (sunset hibiscus) root — was added to the pulp bath to slow drainage and allow the fibres to distribute evenly.

Nagashi-zuki produces paper that is thinner, stronger, and more uniform than tame-zuki paper. The technique allows the papermaker to build up layers, controlling thickness with each pass of the mould. This innovation, developed during and after the Nara period, is the foundation of all subsequent Japanese washi production.

## The Shosoin Collection

The Shosoin treasure repository at Todai-ji contains thousands of documents from the 8th century, many written or printed on locally produced paper. These documents — administrative records, sutra copies, inventories, personal letters — provide the most detailed surviving evidence of Nara-period papermaking.

Analysis of Shosoin papers reveals:

- Multiple paper types and qualities, indicating a sophisticated production system
- Papers made from hemp, kozo, and gampi, sometimes blended
- Evidence of recycled paper (sukigaeshi) — old documents repulped and formed into new sheets
- Dyed papers in various colours, including indigo, safflower red, and persimmon brown
- Papers treated with insect-repellent substances for long-term storage

The survival of these papers for over twelve centuries demonstrates the extraordinary durability of well-made washi.

## Yoshino Washi: Nara's Living Tradition

While state papermaking declined after the capital moved to Kyoto in 794, papermaking continued in Nara's mountain districts. The most significant surviving tradition is Yoshino washi, produced in the Kuzu area of Yoshino, in southern Nara Prefecture.

Yoshino washi is made exclusively from kozo, using water from the Yoshino River — one of Japan's purest water sources. The paper is characterised by:

- Extreme thinness (some sheets are nearly translucent)
- Exceptional strength relative to weight
- A warm, natural colour
- Long fibre structure that resists tearing

Yoshino paper has been used for centuries in urushi lacquerwork (as a reinforcing layer), for shoji screens, for calligraphy, and for conservation — its strength and purity make it the paper of choice for restoring damaged books and artworks worldwide. The British Museum, the Louvre, and major conservation workshops internationally use Yoshino washi for paper repair.

### The Production Process

Making Yoshino washi is a winter activity. The cold water of the Yoshino River is essential — warm water encourages bacterial growth that weakens fibres.

1. **Harvesting**: Kozo branches are cut in December or January
2. **Steaming**: Branches are steamed to loosen the bark
3. **Stripping**: Bark is stripped by hand
4. **Soaking and boiling**: Bark is soaked in the river, then boiled with wood ash lye to dissolve non-cellulose material
5. **Washing**: Fibres are washed in the cold river, removing impurities
6. **Beating**: Fibres are beaten by hand on a stone to separate individual strands
7. **Forming**: Sheets are formed using the nagashi-zuki method
8. **Pressing**: Stacks of wet sheets are pressed overnight to remove excess water
9. **Drying**: Sheets are brushed onto wooden boards and dried in the sun

The entire process takes several weeks from harvest to finished sheet. A skilled papermaker produces approximately 200 to 300 sheets per day during the forming stage.

## Where to Experience Washi in Nara

### Yoshino (Southern Nara Prefecture)

The Kuzu district retains working papermaking families. Fukunishi Washi Honpo, operating since the Edo period, offers visitors the opportunity to observe the production process and, at certain times, to try forming sheets on a mould. The drive to Yoshino from Nara city takes approximately 90 minutes.

### Nara City

**Nara Craft Museum (Nara Kogeikan)**: Displays of traditional Nara crafts including washi, with explanatory materials on historical production methods.

**Naramachi shops**: Several shops in the old merchant quarter sell washi products — notebooks, letter paper, wrapping paper, fans — sourced from Yoshino and other production areas. These shops often provide context on the paper's origins and characteristics.

**Temple gift shops**: Todai-ji and Kasuga Taisha gift shops sell calligraphy supplies including locally sourced washi.

### Conservation in Action

Nara National Museum and the Nara National Research Institute for Cultural Properties both use washi in conservation work. While these facilities are not regularly open to casual visitors, special exhibitions occasionally feature demonstrations of paper conservation techniques.

## The Cultural Significance of Paper in Nara

Paper in Nara is not merely a historical artefact — it remains embedded in the city's cultural and spiritual life. Shinto shrines use washi for shide (zigzag paper streamers), gohei (offering wands), and omikuji (fortune papers). Buddhist temples use it for sutra copying, which remains a meditative practice offered to visitors at several Nara temples. The fusuma (sliding doors) and shoji (translucent screens) in traditional buildings — including ryokan like Kanoya — use washi that filters light, breathes with the humidity, and connects the interior to a papermaking tradition that began in this city over thirteen centuries ago.

## The Future of Nara Washi

Yoshino washi faces the challenges common to all Japanese traditional crafts — ageing artisans, declining domestic demand, and the difficulty of sustaining hand production in an industrial economy. The number of active papermaking households in Yoshino has declined from dozens to a handful.

However, international demand for conservation-grade washi, growing interest in traditional crafts among younger Japanese, and the designation of washi as a UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage (2014) have provided new energy. Several Yoshino papermakers have begun offering workshops and apprenticeships, and the connection between Nara's historical role and its living tradition provides a compelling narrative that draws visitors and students.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I try making washi in Nara?

Yes. Workshops are available in the Yoshino area, typically requiring advance booking. The experience takes one to two hours, and you keep the sheets you make.

### What is the difference between washi and Western paper?

Washi uses long plant fibres (kozo, gampi, mitsumata) formed by hand, producing paper that is stronger, more flexible, and more durable than wood-pulp Western paper. Well-made washi can last over a thousand years.

### Where can I buy authentic Yoshino washi?

In Nara city, several Naramachi shops stock Yoshino washi products. In Yoshino, buy directly from the makers. Prices range from a few hundred yen for small sheets to several thousand for large, high-quality sheets.

### Why is Yoshino paper used for art conservation worldwide?

Its extreme thinness, strength, neutral pH, and long-fibre structure make it ideal for repairing damaged paper without adding bulk or causing chemical deterioration.
