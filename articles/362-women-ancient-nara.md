# Women of Ancient Nara: Empresses, Priestesses, and Poets

The popular image of ancient Japan as a rigidly patriarchal society requires considerable revision when applied to the Nara period (710–784 CE). During the 8th century, women ruled as emperors, shaped religious policy, managed enormous estates, wrote poetry that defined a literary tradition, and exercised forms of power that would be curtailed in later centuries. The women of Nara's golden age were not marginal figures. They were central to the civilisation that produced the city's great temples, its art, and its enduring cultural legacy.

Understanding these women enriches any visit to Nara. The temples, treasures, and traditions you encounter there were not created by men alone but by a society in which women held real authority — political, religious, and cultural.

## Empresses Who Ruled

The Nara period is remarkable for the number of women who served as reigning emperors (tennō). Two of the four emperors who preceded and inaugurated the Nara era were women, and another ruled during the period's final decades.

### Empress Genmei (661–721 CE, r. 707–715)

Genmei was the ruler who moved Japan's capital to Nara in 710 CE. This was not a ceremonial role — the planning and execution of a new capital was the most consequential political decision of the era. Genmei oversaw the construction of Heijō-kyō on a grid plan modelled after China's Chang'an, a city designed to house up to 200,000 people.

She also commissioned the Kojiki (712 CE), Japan's oldest surviving historical chronicle, which established the mythological foundation of the imperial line. Under her reign, Japan's first coinage (the Wadō-kaichin) was minted. These were not the acts of a figurehead but of an active ruler who shaped the institutional foundations of the state.

### Empress Genshō (680–748 CE, r. 715–724)

Genshō succeeded her mother Genmei — the only instance in Japanese history of a mother-to-daughter imperial succession. She continued the work of building the capital and oversaw the compilation of the Nihon Shoki (720 CE), the second great chronicle of Japanese history.

Genshō's reign was administratively significant. She managed the complex politics of a court dominated by rival clans while continuing to develop the infrastructure and institutions of the new capital. She abdicated in favour of Emperor Shōmu in 724 CE, having stabilised the political foundations that would support his ambitious building programmes.

### Empress Kōken/Shōtoku (718–770 CE, r. 749–758, 764–770)

The most controversial of Nara's female rulers, Kōken ascended the throne when her father, Emperor Shōmu, abdicated to become a Buddhist monk. She ruled once, abdicated, and then seized power again — the only Japanese emperor to reign twice under different names (Kōken and Shōtoku).

Her second reign was marked by her relationship with the Buddhist monk Dōkyō, whom she elevated to extraordinary positions of power. Whether their relationship was romantic, political, or purely religious remains debated, but the political crisis it provoked led directly to the decision — after her death — to exclude women from the imperial succession for nearly a thousand years.

Kōken/Shōtoku was also responsible for the Hyakumantō Darani, one million small wooden pagodas each containing a printed dharani (Buddhist prayer), produced around 770 CE. These are among the oldest surviving examples of printed text in the world. Whatever one thinks of her political judgement, her contribution to the history of printing is undeniable.

## Empress Kōmyō: The Power Behind the Throne

Empress Kōmyō (701–760 CE) was not a reigning emperor but a consort — and arguably the most influential woman of the Nara period. Born Fujiwara no Asukabē-hime, she was the wife of Emperor Shōmu and the first non-royal woman to hold the title of empress consort (kōgō).

Her impact on Nara's cultural landscape was immense:

**Charitable institutions**: Kōmyō established the Seyaku-in (a dispensary for medicine) and the Hidenin (a charitable refuge for the sick and destitute). These were among the earliest welfare institutions in Japanese history.

**Hokke-ji**: She founded this important convent near the palace, establishing a centre for women's Buddhist practice that continues to operate today. Hokke-ji's famous eleven-headed Kannon statue is said to have been modelled on Kōmyō herself.

**The Shōsō-in**: After Shōmu's death in 756 CE, Kōmyō dedicated his personal belongings — musical instruments, medicines, textiles, weapons, games, and writing implements — to Tōdai-ji. This act of devotion created the Shōsō-in collection, the most important repository of 8th-century artefacts in the world.

**Calligraphy**: Kōmyō was renowned as a calligrapher. Her copy of the Gakki-ron (a treatise on musical instruments) survives and is considered one of the finest examples of Nara-period calligraphy.

When you visit the Shōsō-in Exhibition at the Nara National Museum each autumn, you are looking at objects that exist because of Kōmyō's decision to preserve rather than dispose of her husband's possessions. The entire collection — over 9,000 items — is her legacy.

## Priestesses and Religious Women

Women played significant roles in Nara-period religious life, both Buddhist and Shinto.

### The Saiō of Ise

The Saiō was an imperial princess appointed to serve as the high priestess of the Grand Shrine at Ise, the holiest site in Shinto. The institution predates the Nara period, but during the 8th century it was formalised and given elaborate ritual protocols. The Saiō lived in a special palace (the Saigū) near Ise and served as a living link between the imperial family and the sun goddess Amaterasu.

The journey of a newly appointed Saiō from Nara to Ise — the Gungyō — was a ritual procession of great solemnity, involving purification ceremonies and multiple stops along the route. Several Nara-period Saiō are documented, and the institution continued until the 14th century.

### Buddhist Nuns

The Nara period saw the establishment of an organised community of Buddhist nuns. The kokubunji system instituted by Emperor Shōmu included convents (kokubunniji) in every province, each staffed by ten nuns. Hokke-ji in Nara served as the headquarters of this national network.

Nuns of the Nara period were educated women who studied Buddhist texts, performed rituals, and in some cases wielded significant institutional authority. The stereotype of Buddhist women as marginal figures in a male religious hierarchy does not apply well to this period.

## Poets of the Man'yōshū

The Man'yōshū, Japan's oldest poetry anthology (compiled around 759 CE), contains works by numerous women, ranging from empresses to commoners. These poems offer the most intimate window into the emotional lives of Nara-period women.

### Princess Nukata (c. 630–690 CE)

Though she lived primarily in the pre-Nara Asuka period, Princess Nukata's poems in the Man'yōshū set the standard for Japanese lyric poetry. Her work on the relative beauty of spring and autumn — composed as part of a poetic debate at court — is one of the most celebrated poems in Japanese literature.

### Lady Ōtomo no Sakanoe (c. 695–750 CE)

The most prolific female poet in the Man'yōshū, Sakanoe contributed over eighty poems to the collection. Her work ranges from love poetry to political verse, and her technical command of poetic forms was acknowledged as exceptional by her contemporaries. She was the aunt and mentor of Ōtomo no Yakamochi, the anthology's probable compiler.

### Lady Kasa

Known only from her poems in the Man'yōshū, Lady Kasa wrote a series of twenty-nine love poems addressed to Ōtomo no Yakamochi. These poems — passionate, vulnerable, and sophisticated — are among the most moving in the collection. They reveal a woman willing to express desire and pain with a directness that later Japanese literary convention would often discourage.

### Anonymous Women

Many Man'yōshū poems by women are anonymous, including those from the Sakimori (frontier guard) poems, written by the wives and mothers of soldiers stationed in Kyushu. These poems express the grief of separation with a rawness that transcends their historical context.

## What Changed After Nara

The relative openness of the Nara period did not last. From the Heian period (794–1185) onward, women's political power was increasingly exercised indirectly — through their roles as mothers and consorts of emperors rather than as rulers in their own right. The literary culture that produced the Man'yōshū eventually gave way to the Heian court culture that produced Murasaki Shikibu and Sei Shōnagon — brilliant women, certainly, but ones who operated within more constrained boundaries.

The exclusion of women from the imperial succession after Empress Shōtoku lasted until the 17th century, when two women briefly returned to the throne. The pattern established in the late Nara period — in which female authority was viewed as dangerous — cast a long shadow.

## Visiting the Women's Nara

Several sites in Nara connect directly to the women described above:

**Hokke-ji**: Founded by Empress Kōmyō, this active convent preserves her legacy. The eleven-headed Kannon is displayed only during limited periods in spring and autumn.

**Nara National Museum**: The annual Shōsō-in Exhibition displays objects from the collection Kōmyō dedicated to Tōdai-ji. The museum's permanent collection includes Buddhist art commissioned by Nara's empresses.

**Heijō Palace Site**: The reconstructed palace grounds include the area where the empresses governed. Interpretive displays explain the court's structure, including the roles of imperial women.

**Kasuga Taisha**: Though a Fujiwara clan shrine, Kasuga's connection to Kōmyō (a Fujiwara herself) and to the court women who worshipped there makes it relevant to this history.

For travellers who wish to explore these sites at a reflective pace, an overnight stay in Nara is essential. Kanoya ryokan offers the kind of quiet, considered hospitality that allows visitors to absorb the layers of history in the city, including the often-overlooked stories of the women who shaped it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many empresses ruled during the Nara period?

Three reigning empresses governed during or immediately before the Nara period: Genmei (r. 707–715), Genshō (r. 715–724), and Kōken/Shōtoku (r. 749–758, 764–770).

### Who was Empress Kōmyō?

Kōmyō (701–760 CE) was the wife of Emperor Shōmu and the most influential woman of the Nara period. She established charitable institutions, founded Hokke-ji convent, and created the Shōsō-in collection by donating her husband's possessions to Tōdai-ji.

### Are there poems by women in the Man'yōshū?

Yes, the Man'yōshū contains numerous poems by women from all social classes, including empresses, aristocrats, and the wives of common soldiers. These poems are among the most celebrated in the anthology.

### Can I visit sites connected to Nara's historical women?

Yes. Hokke-ji (Kōmyō's convent), the Heijō Palace Site, and the Nara National Museum all have direct connections to the women of the Nara period.
