# Nara Running Guide: Jogging Routes Through the Ancient Capital

Running in Nara is unlike running anywhere else in the world. Where most cities force joggers onto traffic-choked pavements or manicured park loops, Nara offers routes that pass eighth-century temples, wind through a UNESCO-protected primeval forest, and share the path with over a thousand free-roaming sika deer. The city's compact size, flat terrain, and abundance of green space make it one of the most rewarding running destinations in Japan — a place where a morning jog doubles as a cultural experience.

Nara's running culture is quiet but established. Local runners are a common sight in the early mornings, circling Nara Park or following the banks of the Saho River. The city lacks the organised running club infrastructure of Tokyo or Osaka, but this only adds to the appeal: here, running is a personal, almost meditative activity, conducted against a backdrop of extraordinary antiquity.

## Route 1: The Nara Park Loop (5 km)

The most popular running route in the city, and the one most visitors will gravitate toward, is the circuit of Nara Park. Starting from the park's western edge near Kintetsu Nara Station, the route heads east past Kofuku-ji's five-storey pagoda, along the broad gravel paths that cross the central meadow, and toward the Todai-ji precinct. From there, it curves south through the wooded slopes below the Kasugayama Primeval Forest, passes Kasuga Taisha shrine, and returns west through the Tobihino meadow to the starting point.

The distance is approximately five kilometres, with negligible elevation gain. The surface alternates between packed gravel, paved paths, and short stretches of grass. The route is runnable year-round, though the gravel sections can become slippery after heavy rain.

The principal hazard is deer. Nara's sika deer are accustomed to humans and generally unbothered by runners, but they are unpredictable — particularly during the rutting season in autumn (October to November), when bucks become territorial, and in spring (May to July), when does are protective of fawns. Give deer a wide berth, and never run directly at an animal blocking the path.

Early morning is the optimal time. Before eight o'clock, the park is largely empty of tourists, the light is golden, and the deer are at their most peaceful — many still resting on the grass, wreathed in morning mist. The experience of running past the Great South Gate of Todai-ji in solitude, with only deer and crows for company, ranks among the finest urban running experiences in Japan.

## Route 2: Saho River Path (8 km out-and-back)

For runners seeking a longer, flatter route with minimal obstacles, the Saho River path offers an excellent option. Beginning near JR Nara Station, the paved riverside path follows the Saho River north toward the Heijo Palace ruins. The path is wide, well-maintained, and largely free of pedestrians, making it suitable for tempo runs and longer efforts.

The outbound leg runs approximately four kilometres to the Heijo Palace Site, the excavated remains of Nara's original eighth-century imperial palace. The site is a vast open field dotted with reconstructed buildings, including the imposing Daigoku-den (Great Audience Hall). Running through the palace grounds in the early morning, with the reconstructed vermillion pillars catching the first light, provides a visceral sense of the city's ancient scale.

The return leg retraces the same route, giving a total distance of approximately eight kilometres. Runners wanting additional distance can extend the route by looping through the palace grounds or continuing north along the river.

The Saho River path is flat throughout and surfaced in asphalt, making it the fastest route option for those focused on pace. Water fountains are spaced at intervals along the path, and convenience stores near JR Nara Station provide pre- and post-run hydration.

## Route 3: Kasugayama Forest Trail (6 km)

The most challenging and atmospheric route in Nara climbs into the Kasugayama Primeval Forest on the eastern edge of the park. This UNESCO World Heritage forest, protected from logging since 841 CE, offers a trail running experience of rare beauty — though the terrain demands caution and appropriate footwear.

The route begins at Kasuga Taisha shrine and follows the Mikasa-yama trail as it ascends through towering cryptomeria and camphor trees. The trail is narrow, uneven, and frequently crossed by tree roots. Elevation gain is moderate — approximately 200 metres over the climbing sections — but the footing requires constant attention. Trail running shoes with good grip are essential; road shoes will struggle on the rocky sections.

The forest canopy blocks most direct sunlight, creating a cool, green corridor even in midsummer. The trail passes several small shrines, stone markers, and natural springs. At the high point, partial views open toward the Nara Basin and the distant Ikoma mountain range.

The descent follows the Takamado trail back toward the park, completing a roughly six-kilometre loop. The descent is steeper than the ascent and requires careful foot placement, particularly after rain. Allow forty-five minutes to an hour for the full circuit, depending on experience and conditions.

## Route 4: Naramachi and Temple Circuit (7 km)

This route combines urban running through Nara's historic quarter with visits to the western temples. Starting in Naramachi, the route heads south through the old merchant district's narrow streets, then turns west along quiet residential roads toward Shin-Yakushi-ji temple. Continuing west, it passes through the Nishinokyo area to reach Yakushi-ji and Toshodai-ji, two of Nara's most important temples.

The full circuit is approximately seven kilometres on paved roads and pavements. Traffic is light, particularly early in the morning, but runners should be alert at intersections. The route is flat and fast, with the aesthetic reward of passing through several distinct Nara neighbourhoods — from the wooden machiya townhouses of Naramachi to the quiet suburban streets of the western district.

This route works particularly well as a late-afternoon run, when the western temples are bathed in golden light and the Naramachi streets begin to empty of shoppers.

## Seasonal Considerations

Spring (March to May) and autumn (October to November) offer ideal running conditions, with mild temperatures, low humidity, and spectacular scenery. Cherry blossom season in late March and early April transforms Nara Park into a canopy of pink, while autumn foliage peaks in mid to late November.

Summer running in Nara demands respect. July and August bring temperatures above thirty-five degrees Celsius and oppressive humidity. Confine runs to early morning — ideally before seven o'clock — and carry water. The Kasugayama forest route is the coolest option in summer, as the dense canopy provides significant shade.

Winter running is crisp and uncrowded. December through February temperatures hover around five degrees Celsius, occasionally dropping below freezing. The park is quiet, the deer are docile, and the temples are strikingly beautiful against grey winter skies or, on rare occasions, a dusting of snow.

## Practical Information for Runners

**Hydration:** Vending machines are ubiquitous in Nara and provide water, sports drinks, and tea at prices between 100 and 160 yen. Water fountains exist in Nara Park but are not reliably maintained.

**Changing and Showers:** Nara lacks dedicated running facilities for visitors. Runners staying in hotels or ryokan can use their accommodation's bathing facilities. Several public sento (bathhouses) in the Naramachi area accept visitors for a small fee and provide an excellent post-run recovery option.

**Safety:** Nara is extremely safe for runners at all hours. The primary risks are tripping on uneven paths, deer encounters, and heat-related illness in summer. Reflective gear is advisable for pre-dawn or evening runs, as park paths are not illuminated.

**Running Events:** The Nara Marathon, held annually in December, attracts thousands of runners for a course that passes many of the city's major landmarks. Registration opens several months in advance and fills quickly. Smaller fun runs and trail races are organised periodically in the surrounding mountains.

**Gear:** Running shops are limited in central Nara. Bring essential gear from home or purchase from the larger sports retailers in nearby Osaka. Trail shoes are necessary only for the Kasugayama forest route; road shoes suffice for all other routes described here.

For runners staying in Naramachi, Kanoya ryokan provides an ideal base. Its central location places all four routes within a short warm-up jog of the front door, and the ryokan's traditional ofuro bath offers a far more civilised post-run recovery than any gym shower.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it safe to run in Nara Park with the deer?
Yes, provided you give deer adequate space and avoid running directly at them. During autumn rutting season, male deer can be aggressive — maintain extra distance from bucks with visible antlers.

### What is the best time of day to run in Nara?
Early morning, between six and eight o'clock, offers the best combination of cool temperatures, empty paths, and atmospheric light. This is true across all seasons.

### Can I run the Kasugayama forest trails?
Yes, though trail running shoes are strongly recommended. The trails are uneven, rooted, and sometimes slippery. The forest is not suitable for running after dark, as trails are unlit.

### Are there running groups I can join in Nara?
Organised running groups are less common in Nara than in larger Japanese cities. The Nara Hash House Harriers meet periodically for social runs. Hotel concierges may be able to connect visitors with local running companions.

### Where can I shower after a run if I am not staying overnight?
Public bathhouses (sento) in the Naramachi area welcome visitors. A soak in a hot bath after a morning run through the ancient capital is one of Nara's quiet pleasures.

*The best running route in Nara is the five-kilometre Nara Park loop, which passes Kofuku-ji, Todai-ji, and Kasuga Taisha shrine on flat gravel and paved paths — best enjoyed in the early morning when the park is quiet and deer rest on the misty grass.*
