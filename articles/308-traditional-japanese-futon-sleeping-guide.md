# Traditional Japanese Futon Sleeping: A Guide for First-Time Ryokan Guests

For many international visitors, the prospect of sleeping on a futon laid directly on a tatami floor is the single most anxiety-inducing aspect of a ryokan stay. This concern is understandable. If you have spent your entire life sleeping on a raised mattress, the idea of lying at floor level on what appears to be a thin pad can seem like a prescription for a bad night. Online forums amplify this worry with accounts of sore backs and sleepless hours.

The reality, for the vast majority of guests, is considerably more comfortable than these fears suggest. A properly prepared Japanese futon is not a yoga mat. It is a layered sleeping system refined over centuries, designed for the specific conditions of a tatami room, and — when given a fair chance — capable of providing sleep that is genuinely restorative. Understanding what you are sleeping on, how to use it, and why the Japanese developed this approach removes most of the apprehension and opens the door to an experience that many visitors end up preferring to their beds at home.

## What a Japanese Futon Actually Is

The Japanese futon (not to be confused with the Western "futon" — a couch-bed hybrid that shares only a name) consists of several distinct components:

### The Shikibuton (Bottom Mattress)

This is the main sleeping surface — a rectangular cotton-filled mattress, typically 5 to 10 centimetres thick, that is laid directly on the tatami floor. Quality ryokan use shikibuton that are substantially thicker and denser than budget versions, often supplemented with an additional mattress pad (mattress topper) for extra cushioning.

The shikibuton provides more support than its apparent thinness suggests, in part because it works in concert with the tatami beneath it. Tatami mats — constructed from compressed rice straw and covered with woven rush grass — provide a semi-firm surface with a degree of natural give. The combination of shikibuton and tatami creates a sleeping surface that is firm but not hard, supportive but not rigid.

### The Kakebuton (Comforter)

The top covering is a large, soft comforter — traditionally filled with silk or cotton, though many modern versions use synthetic materials. The kakebuton is lighter than a Western duvet but surprisingly warm, and it drapes closely around the body. In colder months, a second kakebuton or a wool blanket may be provided.

### The Makura (Pillow)

Japanese pillows vary more than you might expect. Traditional versions were firm — sometimes filled with buckwheat husks (sobagara) — and smaller than Western pillows. Many modern ryokan now offer a choice between Japanese and Western-style pillows, recognising that pillow preference is perhaps the most individual aspect of sleep comfort. If your pillow does not suit you, asking for an alternative is perfectly acceptable.

## The Ritual of Futon Preparation

One of the distinctive features of a ryokan futon experience is that the bed does not exist during the daytime. Your room serves multiple functions — sitting room, tea room, dining room — and the futon is laid out only in the evening, typically while you are at dinner or in the bath.

This preparation is performed by the nakai-san (room attendant), who enters your room, moves the low table to one side, and lays out the futon with practiced precision. The sheets are pulled taut, the comforter is folded back invitingly, and a small towel or cloth is placed on the pillow. When you return to your room after dinner, you find it transformed — the same space that was a dining room an hour ago is now a serene bedroom.

In the morning, the reverse occurs. After you leave for breakfast or the bath, the nakai-san folds and stores the futon, restoring the room to its daytime configuration. This daily transformation is not an inconvenience — it is an expression of the Japanese principle of using space efficiently and intentionally, and it gives the room a rhythm that mirrors the day itself.

## How to Sleep Comfortably on a Futon

For first-time futon sleepers, a few practical adjustments significantly improve the experience:

**Give it a full night before judging.** The first thirty minutes on a futon can feel unusual if you are accustomed to a soft Western mattress. Your body needs time to adjust. Most people who report discomfort were never uncomfortable enough to lose sleep — they were simply aware of a different sensation. By the second night, this awareness typically fades entirely.

**Sleep on your back.** Futons work best for back sleeping, where the firm surface supports the spine in a neutral position. Side sleepers may find the experience less comfortable initially, though a thicker pillow and a slight knee bend usually resolve this.

**Use the full pillow selection.** If the provided pillow does not work for you, ask for alternatives. Most ryokan keep a range of firmness options available. There is no discourtesy in the request — the staff want you to sleep well.

**Adjust the comforter.** If you tend to sleep warm, fold the kakebuton to use only a single layer. If the room is cool — as tatami rooms in traditional buildings can be — make sure to tuck the comforter around your body rather than leaving gaps.

**Consider your pre-bed routine.** The Japanese ryokan schedule is designed to optimise sleep: a hot onsen bath in the evening relaxes muscles and lowers core body temperature as you cool afterward, both of which promote drowsiness. A full kaiseki dinner provides satisfaction without the heaviness of rich Western food. The combination of bath, meal, and the quiet of a tatami room creates conditions for excellent sleep.

## Why the Japanese Sleep on Futons

The futon system is not an accident of underdevelopment — Japan has the technology and wealth to equip every home with Western beds if it chose to. The persistence of futon sleeping reflects deliberate preferences:

**Space efficiency.** Japanese homes, particularly in urban areas, are smaller than their Western counterparts. A futon that folds away during the day effectively doubles the usable space in a room. What serves as a bedroom at night becomes a living room, study, or play space during the day.

**Hygiene.** Futons are regularly aired in sunlight, which kills dust mites and bacteria. The ability to take bedding outside and expose it to ultraviolet light — impossible with a fixed mattress — is considered a significant health advantage. On sunny days throughout Japan, you can see futons draped over balcony railings, a domestic ritual as deeply embedded as laundry.

**Spinal health.** While the scientific evidence is mixed, many Japanese believe that sleeping on a firm surface supports better spinal alignment. Orthopaedic opinion in Japan tends to favour firmer sleeping surfaces than Western practice recommends, and anecdotal experience from generations of futon sleepers supports this view.

**Aesthetic and philosophical alignment.** The futon complements the Japanese aesthetic of clean, uncluttered space. A room with a permanent bed is always a bedroom. A room with a futon that appears and disappears is a space of possibility — its function follows intention rather than furniture. This aligns with broader Japanese design principles that value adaptability and restraint over fixed accumulation.

**Connection to the earth.** Sleeping close to the ground carries a subtle but real psychological quality. The proximity to tatami — woven from natural rush grass, carrying a distinctive clean scent — creates a sense of groundedness that elevated sleeping does not quite replicate. This is less a practical consideration than an experiential one, but it contributes to the distinctive character of a ryokan night.

## Common Concerns Addressed

**"I have a bad back."** Many guests with back problems actually find futon sleeping beneficial, as the firm surface prevents the spinal misalignment that soft mattresses can cause. However, if you have a specific medical condition that requires a raised or specially supported sleeping surface, communicate this when booking. Quality ryokan — including properties like Kanoya in Nara — can often provide additional mattress padding or, in some cases, arrange a room with a Western bed.

**"I cannot get up from floor level."** This is a legitimate concern for elderly guests or those with mobility limitations. Rising from a futon requires some leg and core strength. Most ryokan can provide a low platform or folding stool beside the futon to assist, and many now offer rooms with accessible features. Again, advance communication is key.

**"I am too tall."** Standard Japanese futons are approximately 200 centimetres long, which accommodates most guests comfortably. Very tall visitors (over 190 cm) may find their feet extending beyond the mattress. Extra-long futons are available at some properties — mentioning your height when booking ensures appropriate arrangements.

**"I will be cold."** Traditional Japanese buildings can be draughty in winter. However, the combination of the kakebuton, supplemental blankets, and the insulating properties of tatami generally provides adequate warmth. Many ryokan also provide radiant heaters or heated kotatsu tables. If warmth is a concern, an evening onsen bath before bed raises body temperature and promotes comfortable sleep.

## The Futon Experience as Part of the Whole

It is worth stepping back to see the futon not as an isolated sleeping arrangement but as an integral element of the ryokan experience. The progression of a ryokan evening — arrival, tea, bath, dinner, the discovery of your prepared futon, the quiet of a tatami room at night — forms a narrative that a hotel room with a permanent bed simply cannot create. The futon is the final chapter of that narrative: a clean, simple surface that asks nothing of you except rest.

Many guests who approach the futon with trepidation find that it becomes a favourite memory of their Japan trip. The quality of sleep in a quiet tatami room — the scent of rush grass, the absence of electronic hum, the particular darkness of a room with heavy wooden shutters — has a restorative quality that transcends the question of mattress firmness.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is sleeping on a Japanese futon uncomfortable?
Most guests find futon sleeping comfortable once they adjust to the firmer surface, which typically takes one night. Quality ryokan use thick, well-maintained futons with additional padding that are significantly more comfortable than budget alternatives.

### Can I request a Western bed at a ryokan?
Some ryokan offer rooms with Western beds, and others can provide additional mattress padding on request. Communicate preferences when booking to ensure the best arrangement.

### How thick is a Japanese futon?
A quality ryokan futon (shikibuton) is typically 5 to 10 centimetres thick, often supplemented with a mattress topper. Combined with the natural give of tatami flooring, this provides more cushioning than the measurements alone suggest.

### Do you sleep directly on the floor in Japan?
Not quite. The futon mattress is placed on tatami mats, which are themselves 5 to 6 centimetres thick and made from compressed rice straw. You are sleeping on a layered system, not a bare floor.

### What should I wear to sleep in a ryokan?
Most guests sleep in the yukata (cotton robe) provided by the ryokan. This is standard practice and part of the experience. You are also welcome to wear your own sleepwear.
