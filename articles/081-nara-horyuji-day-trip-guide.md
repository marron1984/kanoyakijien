# Horyuji from Nara: Visiting the World's Oldest Wooden Buildings

Twelve minutes by train from JR Nara Station stands the oldest wooden building in the world. Horyuji's Western Precinct — built in the late 7th or early 8th century — has survived earthquakes, typhoons, fires, wars, and the simple entropy of thirteen centuries. No other wooden structures on Earth are this old. This fact alone would make Horyuji remarkable, but the temple offers far more than longevity: a collection of Buddhist art that spans the earliest centuries of Japanese civilisation, an architectural ensemble of extraordinary harmony, and the physical presence of Prince Shotoku — the figure who, more than any other, shaped the foundations of Japanese culture.

For visitors staying in Nara, Horyuji is the most important and most accessible day trip. The brevity of the journey (12 minutes by JR train to Horyuji Station, then 20 minutes on foot or 5 minutes by bus to the temple) makes it easy to combine with a morning or afternoon in central Nara. The depth of the experience makes it essential.

## Prince Shotoku and Horyuji's Founding

Horyuji was founded in 607 CE by Prince Shotoku (574–622 CE), the regent and cultural visionary who did more than any other figure to shape early Japanese civilisation. Shotoku championed Buddhism, sent diplomatic missions to China, created Japan's first written constitution (the Seventeen-Article Constitution), and commissioned temples and artworks that established the aesthetic foundations of Japanese culture.

Horyuji was Shotoku's personal temple — a centre for the study and practice of Buddhism that he envisioned as a model for Japanese religious institutions. The original buildings were destroyed by fire in 670 CE and rebuilt shortly afterward. The surviving structures of the Western Precinct date from this reconstruction — making them, at approximately 1,350 years old, the oldest wooden buildings in existence.

## The Western Precinct (Saiin Garan)

### The Five-Storey Pagoda (Goju-no-to)

Horyuji's pagoda, approximately 32 metres tall, is the oldest five-storey pagoda in Japan. Its proportions — each storey diminishing in width as the tower rises — create a visual rhythm that has influenced Japanese architecture for thirteen centuries. The central pillar (shinbashira) extends from the foundation to the finial, providing structural stability through a principle that modern earthquake engineers recognise as remarkably effective.

The pagoda's ground floor contains four tableaux of clay figures depicting scenes from the life of the Buddha, dating from 711 CE. These small, vivid compositions are among the oldest surviving examples of narrative art in Japan.

### The Kondo (Main Hall)

The Kondo is the primary worship hall and the building that, together with the pagoda, constitutes the oldest wooden architecture in the world. Its double-roofed structure, with deep eaves and a graceful upward curve at the roof corners, established the architectural vocabulary of Japanese temple design.

Inside, the Kondo houses a remarkable collection of Buddhist sculpture:

- **Shaka Trinity** (623 CE): A bronze Buddhist trinity by the sculptor Tori Busshi, created in memory of Prince Shotoku. The central Shaka figure, with its archaic smile and frontal pose, represents the earliest phase of Japanese Buddhist sculpture — a style showing strong Korean influence.
- **Yakushi Nyorai**: A bronze figure often attributed to the temple's original foundation.
- **Four Heavenly Kings**: Guardian figures in the corners, among the oldest surviving examples in Japan.
- **Wall paintings**: The Kondo originally contained 7th-century wall paintings of extraordinary quality, comparable to the finest wall paintings of the same era anywhere in the world. A fire in 1949 severely damaged these paintings, which are now preserved (in damaged condition) and replaced by reproductions.

### The Chumon (Middle Gate)

The gate that provides the formal entrance to the Western Precinct, notable for its unusual arrangement of columns (the central pillar blocks direct access, forcing visitors to pass on either side). The Nio guardian figures in the gate are among the oldest in Japan, dating from the 8th century.

## The Eastern Precinct (Toin Garan)

### The Yumedono (Hall of Dreams)

An octagonal hall built in 739 CE on the site of Prince Shotoku's former residence. The building houses the Guze Kannon — a life-sized gilded wooden figure wrapped in cloth and sealed from view for centuries. When the American art historian Ernest Fenollosa persuaded the monks to unwrap it in 1884, they feared divine retribution; instead, they revealed one of the most beautiful Buddhist sculptures in existence.

The Guze Kannon is displayed only during limited periods (spring and autumn). If your visit coincides with its display, do not miss it.

### The Chuguji Nunnery

Adjacent to the Eastern Precinct, Chuguji houses the Miroku Bosatsu — a 7th-century wooden figure of the Future Buddha in meditation, with a gentle half-smile that is often compared to the Mona Lisa and the smile of Reims Cathedral's angel. The figure's expression — serene, compassionate, inward-looking — represents one of the supreme achievements of East Asian art.

## The Great Treasure Hall (Daihozoden)

Horyuji's museum houses artworks too fragile or precious for display in the temple buildings:

- **Tamamushi no Zushi** (Jewel Beetle Shrine): A 7th-century miniature shrine decorated with paintings and originally covered with iridescent beetle wings. It is one of the most important objects in Japanese art history.
- **Kudara Kannon**: A tall, slender wooden Kannon figure of mysterious origins (possibly Korean, 7th century). Its elegant proportions and enigmatic expression make it one of the most beloved Buddhist sculptures in Japan.
- **Hundreds of additional sculptures, paintings, and ritual objects** spanning the temple's 1,400-year history.

## Visiting Horyuji

### Getting There from Nara

**By JR train**: JR Nara Station → Horyuji Station. Yamatoji Line, approximately 12 minutes. Trains run frequently (2–4 per hour).

**From Horyuji Station to the temple**: 20-minute walk along a straightforward route, or 5-minute bus ride (bus stop outside the station).

**Cost**: Approximately ¥220 each way by train.

### Hours and Admission

- **Hours**: 8:00am–5:00pm (February 22–November 3), 8:00am–4:30pm (November 4–February 21)
- **Admission**: ¥1,500 (adults), including all precincts and the Great Treasure Hall
- **Guze Kannon viewing**: Spring (late April–mid-May) and autumn (late October–mid-November)

### Time Required

Allow 2–3 hours for a thorough visit. Horyuji is larger than it initially appears, and the art collection rewards sustained attention.

### Suggested Route

1. Enter through the Nandaimon (Great South Gate)
2. Western Precinct: Chumon, Kondo (main hall), pagoda
3. Great Treasure Hall: Allow at least 30 minutes here
4. Eastern Precinct: Yumedono
5. Chuguji: The Miroku Bosatsu

### Combining with Central Nara

**Morning option**: Take the 8:00am train to Horyuji, visit for 2–3 hours, return to central Nara by noon for lunch and afternoon activities.

**Afternoon option**: Morning in central Nara, lunch in Naramachi, 1:00pm train to Horyuji, visit until 3:30–4:00pm, return for dinner.

Travellers based at Naramachi properties like Kanoya can easily walk to JR Nara Station (15 minutes from Naramachi) for the short journey to Horyuji.

## Why Horyuji Matters

### Architectural Significance

Horyuji is to Japanese architecture what the Parthenon is to Western architecture — the foundational building from which all subsequent development flows. The proportional systems, the roof forms, the column arrangements, and the spatial relationships established here defined the language of Japanese temple architecture for all time.

### Cultural Depth

The temple is a compendium of early Japanese civilisation — its art, its religion, its international connections, its political ideals. Prince Shotoku's vision of a civilised, Buddhist Japan finds its most complete physical expression at Horyuji.

### Material Survival

That wooden buildings have survived for 1,350 years is itself extraordinary. Wood is vulnerable — to fire, to insects, to moisture, to the simple passage of time. Horyuji's survival testifies to a continuous tradition of care: the buildings have been maintained, repaired, and partially reconstructed over thirteen centuries by people who understood them and valued their preservation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Horyuji worth the trip from Nara?

Absolutely. It is one of the most important cultural sites in the world. The 12-minute train journey makes it effortless.

### Can I see Horyuji in half a day?

Yes. Three hours covers the main precincts and the museum comfortably.

### Is Horyuji suitable for children?

Yes. The pagoda, the large guardian figures, and the temple grounds appeal to children. The art museum may challenge younger visitors.

### How does Horyuji compare to Todai-ji?

Todai-ji is monumental and dramatic. Horyuji is older, quieter, and more architecturally significant. Both are essential.
