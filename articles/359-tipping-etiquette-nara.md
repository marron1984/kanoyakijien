# Tipping, Etiquette, and Social Customs in Nara

Japan operates on a different social grammar than most Western countries, and nowhere is this more apparent than in the small, daily interactions that define a traveller's experience. Tipping, greeting, eating, bathing, visiting temples, entering homes — each has its own set of conventions that, while rarely enforced with anything more severe than a gentle correction, are worth understanding before you arrive.

Nara, as a smaller and more traditional city than Tokyo or Osaka, retains many of these customs with particular clarity. The city's deep roots in Buddhism and courtly culture have shaped a social environment where politeness is not performative but structural — it is how things work. Understanding these customs will not merely prevent embarrassment; it will open doors to warmth, respect, and experiences that remain invisible to the uninformed visitor.

## Tipping: The Short Answer

**Do not tip in Japan.** This is the single most important etiquette rule for Western visitors to understand.

Tipping is not customary, not expected, and in most contexts, not welcome. Attempting to leave a tip at a restaurant, in a taxi, at a hotel, or after any service will likely result in confusion, with the money being returned to you. In some situations, offering a tip can be perceived as implying that the person is underpaid or that the service was transactional in a way that Japanese professional culture does not recognise.

This applies universally: restaurants, taxis, hotels, ryokan, bars, hairdressers, tour guides, porters, and any other service you might encounter in Nara.

### The Exception: Ryokan and Exceptional Service

There is one narrow exception, and it applies specifically to traditional ryokan. In high-end ryokan with dedicated room attendants (nakai-san), it is sometimes appropriate to offer a monetary gift — not a tip, but an expression of gratitude. This is called "kokorozuke" and follows specific conventions:

- The amount is typically 1,000 to 3,000 yen per person.
- The money should be placed in a small envelope (pochi-bukuro), which can be purchased at convenience stores or stationery shops. Do not hand cash directly.
- Present the envelope at the beginning of your stay, not the end. This is the opposite of Western tipping — it is a gesture of goodwill and introduction, not a performance review.
- Kokorozuke is not expected. Many ryokan staff will decline. If they do, accept the refusal gracefully and do not insist.

At properties like Kanoya, where the service tradition is deeply rooted but the atmosphere is unpretentious, the staff are genuinely pleased by a guest's warmth and courtesy. A sincere "arigatou gozaimashita" (thank you) at departure carries at least as much weight as any monetary gesture.

## Greetings and Bowing

Bowing is the fundamental social gesture in Japan. Understanding its basics prevents awkwardness:

- **A slight nod** (about 15 degrees) is appropriate for casual greetings, thanking shop staff, and acknowledging strangers who help you.
- **A deeper bow** (about 30 degrees) is appropriate for more formal situations — greeting your ryokan host, thanking a guide, or apologising.
- **A full bow** (45 degrees or more) is reserved for very formal apologies or deep gratitude. You will rarely need this.

As a foreign visitor, no one expects you to bow perfectly or at the correct angle. The gesture itself — the willingness to participate in the custom — is what matters. If someone bows to you, bow back at roughly the same depth. Do not bow while shaking hands; it creates an awkward collision of cultural systems. If a Japanese person offers a handshake, they are adapting to your culture — shake hands and smile.

## Shoes: On and Off

Japan's shoe etiquette is not arbitrary — it reflects a fundamental cultural distinction between inside (uchi) and outside (soto), between clean and unclean:

- **Remove your shoes** when entering any private home, most ryokan, many traditional restaurants, some museums, and temple interiors. Look for a genkan (entryway area with a step up) and rows of shoes or slippers. If others have removed their shoes, remove yours.
- **Slippers** are often provided for indoor use. Wear them in hallways and common areas. **Remove slippers** before stepping onto tatami mats — tatami is walked on in socks or bare feet only.
- **Toilet slippers**: Many traditional spaces provide separate slippers for the bathroom. Use them only in the bathroom, and change back when you leave. Accidentally wearing toilet slippers into a dining room is one of the classic foreign visitor errors.
- **Wear socks without holes.** You will be removing your shoes frequently and your feet will be visible.

## Eating Etiquette

### Chopstick Rules

- **Never stick chopsticks upright in a bowl of rice.** This resembles incense sticks at a funeral offering and is considered deeply inauspicious.
- **Never pass food from one pair of chopsticks to another.** This mirrors a funeral ceremony involving cremated bones. Use your chopsticks to place food on the other person's plate instead.
- **Do not point with chopsticks** or wave them over dishes while deciding what to eat.
- **Do not rub disposable chopsticks together** to remove splinters. In a quality restaurant, this implies that the chopsticks — and by extension the establishment — are cheap.
- **Resting chopsticks**: Place them on the chopstick rest (hashioki) when not in use, or across the top of your bowl.

### Dining Customs

- **Say "itadakimasu"** (I humbly receive) before eating. This is a universal pre-meal expression in Japan, equivalent to saying grace but secular. A quiet "itadakimasu" with a slight nod is sufficient.
- **Say "gochisousama deshita"** (it was a feast) after eating. This thanks the host, chef, and everyone involved in preparing the meal.
- **Slurping noodles is acceptable** and even expected. It indicates enjoyment and helps cool the noodles. Do not slurp other foods.
- **Drinking etiquette**: When drinking with others, pour for your companions before filling your own glass. Wait until everyone has a drink before the first sip, often preceded by "kampai" (cheers).
- **Finishing your rice**: It is polite to finish every grain of rice in your bowl, especially in a traditional setting.

## Temple and Shrine Etiquette

Nara's temples and shrines are active places of worship, not museums. Behaving respectfully is essential:

### At Temples (Buddhist)

- Remove your hat when entering temple buildings.
- Photography is usually permitted in outdoor areas but often prohibited inside main halls. Look for signs or ask.
- Incense: If an incense burner is present at the entrance, you may waft the smoke over yourself — it is considered purifying.
- Speak quietly. Temples are places of contemplation.
- If you wish to pray, stand before the altar, place your palms together, bow slightly, and close your eyes briefly.

### At Shrines (Shinto)

The purification and prayer ritual at shrines follows a specific sequence:

1. **Temizu (hand washing)**: At the water basin near the entrance, take the ladle, fill it, pour water over your left hand, then your right hand, then cup some water in your left hand to rinse your mouth (spit it out discreetly, not back into the basin), and finally tilt the ladle to let water run down the handle. This entire sequence uses one ladleful.
2. **At the offering box**: Toss a coin (five-yen coins are traditional, as the word for five yen — "go-en" — sounds like the word for fate or connection).
3. **Bow twice** deeply.
4. **Clap twice** with your hands at chest height.
5. **Bow once more** deeply.

This two-two-one pattern (ni-hai ni-hakushu ichi-hai) is standard at most shrines, though some shrines have different traditions.

## Onsen and Bathing Etiquette

Public bathing at onsen follows strict conventions:

- **Wash thoroughly** at the shower stations before entering any bath. Soap, rinse, repeat. Enter the bath completely clean.
- **No swimsuits.** Onsen bathing is done nude.
- **Do not put your small towel in the water.** Fold it and place it on your head or set it beside the bath.
- **Do not swim or splash.** Onsen baths are for soaking quietly.
- **Tattoo policies**: Some onsen prohibit tattooed visitors. This is decreasing but remains common. Ask before visiting, or use private baths.
- **Tie up long hair** so it does not enter the water.

## General Social Customs

### Queuing

Japanese people queue with remarkable discipline. Join the line, maintain spacing, and do not push ahead. On escalators in Osaka and Nara, stand on the right and walk on the left (the opposite of Tokyo).

### Noise

Nara is a quiet city, and Japanese social norms value quietness in public spaces. Speak at a moderate volume on trains and in restaurants. Phone calls on trains are strongly discouraged — set your phone to manner mode (silent).

### Blowing Your Nose

Blowing your nose in public is considered rude in Japan. If you must, step to the side and be as discreet as possible. Sniffling, by contrast, is acceptable — the opposite of Western convention.

### Gift Giving

If you visit someone's home (unlikely but possible), bring a small gift. Wrapped food items from your home country are ideal. Present gifts with both hands. Do not expect the recipient to open it in front of you — it is normal to open gifts privately.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I tip in restaurants in Nara?

No. Tipping is not customary in Japan and is not expected in any restaurant, regardless of the quality of service. Attempting to tip may cause confusion. Express appreciation verbally with "gochisousama deshita" (thank you for the meal) instead.

### What is the bowing etiquette in Japan?

A slight nod (15 degrees) for casual situations, a moderate bow (30 degrees) for formal greetings and thanks. As a foreign visitor, the gesture matters more than the angle. Bow back when someone bows to you.

### Do I need to remove my shoes in Nara?

Frequently. Remove shoes when entering ryokan, traditional restaurants, temple interiors, and any space with a genkan (step-up entryway). Look for rows of shoes or provided slippers as cues. Wear socks without holes.

### Is it rude to blow my nose in public in Japan?

It is considered impolite. Step aside and be discreet if you must blow your nose. Sniffling is socially acceptable, which is the reverse of many Western customs.

### How do I pray at a Shinto shrine?

At the offering box, toss a coin (five yen is traditional), bow twice deeply, clap twice at chest height, then bow once more. This two-bow, two-clap, one-bow pattern is standard at most shrines.
