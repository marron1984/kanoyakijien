# Nara's Kofun Burial Mounds: Ancient Tombs Near the City

Long before Buddhism arrived, before the great temples rose, before Nara was even named as a capital, the landscape of the Yamato plain was being shaped by something older and more mysterious: the kofun. These massive earthen burial mounds — some shaped like keyholes, some round, some square — were built to honour the rulers of early Japan during a period spanning roughly the third to seventh centuries CE. The region around Nara contains some of the most significant and accessible kofun in the country, yet most visitors walk past them without a second glance, their eyes fixed on the more famous temples ahead.

This is understandable. Unlike a pagoda or a great Buddha, a kofun does not announce itself. It appears as a tree-covered hill, a grassy elevation, a park you might pass through without realising you are walking over the resting place of a ruler who governed this valley before written records began. But once you understand what you are looking at, these mounds become among the most profound historical sites in Nara Prefecture — monuments to a Japan that existed before Japan had a name.

## What Is a Kofun?

A kofun is a burial mound constructed during the Kofun period (roughly 250–538 CE), a formative era in Japanese history when powerful clan leaders consolidated control over the Yamato region. The word itself is straightforward: ko (old) and fun (mound or tomb).

The most distinctive type is the zenpokoen-fun, the "keyhole-shaped" mound — a circular rear section joined to a trapezoidal front, creating a shape visible only from above. These keyhole kofun are unique to Japan and are found nowhere else in the world. Their exact purpose and symbolism remain debated, but their scale leaves no doubt about the power of the people buried within them.

Kofun range enormously in size. The largest — Emperor Nintoku's tomb in Sakai — covers more ground area than the Great Pyramid of Giza. In Nara Prefecture, the mounds are generally smaller but historically no less significant, as this was the heartland of the Yamato court, the political entity that eventually became the Japanese imperial state.

### What Is Inside?

The burial chambers within kofun typically contained stone sarcophagi along with grave goods: bronze mirrors, iron weapons, magatama jewels, armour, horse trappings, and haniwa (clay figurines placed on the mound's surface). Many of these artefacts are now displayed in museums across Nara Prefecture.

Imperial kofun (those attributed to specific emperors) are administered by the Imperial Household Agency and remain sealed — they cannot be entered or excavated. This makes them simultaneously some of Japan's most historically important sites and its least explored.

## Key Kofun Near Nara City

### Saki Kofun Cluster (Saki-cho)

Located in the northern part of Nara city, the Saki Kofun group is the most accessible cluster for visitors staying in central Nara. The group contains several dozen mounds, including large keyhole-shaped tombs attributed to various imperial figures.

**Uwanabe Kofun**: A large keyhole mound approximately 255 metres long, surrounded by a moat that now serves as a peaceful pond. The tree-covered mound rises from the water, creating a scene of surprising tranquillity for something located within the city limits. The path around the moat makes for a contemplative walk.

**Konabe Kofun**: Adjacent to Uwanabe, this mound is slightly smaller (204 metres) but equally atmospheric. Together, the two kofun and their moats form a green oasis on the northern edge of the Heijo Palace site. They are visible from the reconstructed palace grounds, providing a visual connection between two eras of Nara's history.

**Misasagi-cho Kofun**: Scattered through the residential neighbourhood of Misasagi-cho, smaller mounds punctuate the landscape. Some are marked with explanatory signage; others appear simply as wooded hills rising between houses.

**How to visit**: The Saki cluster is a 20-minute walk north from Kintetsu Nara Station, or a short bus ride. Combine with a visit to the Heijo Palace site for a day exploring Nara's pre-temple history.

### Umami Hills Kofun Group (Koryo, Kawai, and Oji towns)

Southwest of Nara city, in the gently rolling terrain of the Umami hills, lies one of the densest concentrations of kofun in Japan. The Umami Kyuryo Kofun-gun (Umami Hills Kofun Cluster) is a designated national historic site containing over 200 mounds dating from the fourth and fifth centuries.

**Highlights include**:
- **Gosashi Kofun**: A large keyhole mound (238 metres) with a well-preserved moat. One of the finest examples of a mid-fifth-century imperial tomb.
- **Naga-yama Kofun**: Notable for its well-preserved shape and surrounding park.
- **Umami Kyuryo Park**: A modern park built around and among the kofun, with walking paths, observation points, and informational displays in Japanese (limited English). The park allows visitors to appreciate the scale and distribution of the mounds in a landscaped setting.

**How to visit**: Approximately 40 minutes from Nara city by train (Kintetsu line to Ikaruga or Oji, then bus or taxi). A half-day excursion.

### Asuka Region Kofun

Further south, in the Asuka district, burial mounds transition from the classic kofun form into later tumulus styles that reflect the arrival of continental influences. Key sites include:

**Ishibutai Kofun**: The most famous kofun in the region — and one of the few whose interior is accessible. The earth covering has eroded or been removed over the centuries, exposing the massive stone burial chamber beneath. The chamber is formed from boulders weighing up to 77 tonnes, creating an interior space of astonishing scale. Attributed to Soga no Umako, a powerful political figure of the late sixth century.

**Takamatsuzuka Kofun**: Discovered in 1972, this modest-sized mound contained wall paintings of extraordinary quality — court figures, celestial maps, and the Chinese directional animals (Blue Dragon, White Tiger, Red Bird, Black Tortoise). The paintings are now preserved in a climate-controlled facility adjacent to the mound. A museum displays high-quality reproductions and explains the discovery.

**Kitora Kofun**: Another painted tomb, discovered in 1983, with star charts and directional animal paintings. A dedicated museum opened nearby to display the preserved murals.

**How to visit**: Asuka is 40–60 minutes south of Nara by train. The sites are spread across the village and best explored by rented bicycle. A full day is recommended.

## Understanding What You See

### Reading the Landscape

The challenge of visiting kofun is perceptual. Unlike a temple, whose beauty is immediate, a kofun requires you to understand what you are looking at. From ground level, most large kofun appear as tree-covered hills surrounded by moats. Their keyhole shape is visible only from the air or on maps. This means the visitor must hold two views simultaneously: the peaceful green hill before them and the monumental tomb it actually is.

This dual awareness — the everyday appearance concealing the extraordinary — is itself part of the kofun's significance. These mounds have been in the landscape for over 1,500 years, weathering, growing over with trees, becoming part of the terrain. They have outlasted the wooden palaces, the original temples, and even the language of the people who built them.

### Haniwa

Among the most engaging kofun-period artefacts are haniwa — hollow clay figures that were placed on and around the mounds. Haniwa take many forms: warriors, horses, houses, boats, dancers, musicians, and simple cylinders. Their style is distinctive — round eyes, minimal features, a naive charm that makes them immediately appealing. Fine examples are displayed at the Nara National Museum and the Kashihara Archaeological Museum.

## Museums for Context

Visiting kofun without museum context is possible but incomplete. The following museums provide essential background:

- **Kashihara Archaeological Museum**: The most comprehensive collection of kofun-period artefacts in the region. Located near Asuka, it displays grave goods, haniwa, and explanatory materials on the Kofun period. Excellent preparation for visiting the Asuka sites.
- **Nara National Museum**: Includes kofun-period objects in its archaeological gallery, providing context within the broader sweep of Japanese art history.
- **Takamatsuzuka Mural Museum**: Dedicated to the painted tomb's murals. Small but fascinating.
- **Kitora Kofun Museum**: Displays the astronomical ceiling paintings and directional animal murals. Modern, well-designed.

## Practical Information

**Best season**: Kofun are outdoor sites. Spring (cherry blossoms sometimes grow on the mounds) and autumn (foliage reflected in moats) are the most atmospheric seasons. Summer is hot; winter is quiet but perfectly manageable.

**Accessibility**: The Saki cluster in Nara city is flat and walkable. Asuka sites require more planning — bicycle rental is strongly recommended. Umami Kyuryo Park is wheelchair-accessible in parts.

**Language**: Signage at most kofun sites is primarily in Japanese. Carry a guidebook or download information in advance. The museums in Asuka have some English labelling.

**Duration**: The Saki cluster near Nara can be explored in 2–3 hours. Asuka requires a full day. Umami Hills needs a half-day.

**Combining with your stay**: Visitors based in the Naramachi district — at a traditional inn like Kanoya, for example — can walk to the Saki kofun cluster in the morning, returning through the Heijo Palace site, and save the Asuka excursion for another day.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can you enter a kofun?

Most kofun are sealed and cannot be entered, especially those designated as imperial tombs. The notable exception is Ishibutai Kofun in Asuka, where the exposed stone chamber is open to visitors.

### Are kofun the same as the pyramids?

They serve a similar purpose — monumental tombs for rulers — but differ in construction (earth and stone versus cut stone blocks), form (keyhole shapes versus geometric pyramids), and cultural context. The comparison is useful for scale but misleading in detail.

### How old are the kofun near Nara?

Most date from the fourth to sixth centuries CE, making them roughly 1,400–1,700 years old. They predate Nara's establishment as a capital (710 CE) by several centuries.

### Why are keyhole-shaped kofun unique to Japan?

The origins of the keyhole form remain debated among archaeologists. The shape may combine a circular burial area with a square ceremonial platform, but no definitive explanation exists. The form developed indigenously and has no known parallel elsewhere.
