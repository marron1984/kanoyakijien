# Bird Watching in Nara: A Seasonal Guide to Species and Spots

Nara is famous for its deer. Every visitor knows the deer. Postcards feature the deer, Instagram overflows with the deer, and the deer themselves are perfectly aware of their celebrity status. But above the deer, largely unnoticed by the crowds photographing fawns on the temple lawns, Nara hosts an avian population of remarkable diversity. The ancient forests of Nara Park, the wetlands of the Yamato Plain, the mountain corridors of the prefecture's southern reaches, and the temple ponds scattered through the city create a patchwork of habitats that supports resident and migratory species across all four seasons.

For visiting birdwatchers — and for travellers who simply enjoy noticing the natural world beyond the guidebook highlights — Nara offers something rare: outstanding birding in settings of extraordinary cultural significance. You can watch a kingfisher dive from a branch beside a thousand-year-old temple. You can hear a bush warbler sing in the forest behind the Great Buddha hall. The birds and the monuments share the same landscape, and attending to one enriches the experience of the other.

## Spring (March to May)

Spring migration transforms Nara's birdlife. Overwintering species begin their departure northward, while summer breeders arrive from southern latitudes.

### Key Species

**Japanese Bush Warbler (uguisu):** The bird whose song defines Japanese spring. Its distinctive ascending call — transliterated as ho-hokekyo — begins in late February and intensifies through March and April. In Nara Park, the uguisu sings from the dense understory of the Kasuga Primeval Forest and the wooded slopes around Nigatsu-do. You will hear it long before you see it; the bird is famously elusive, preferring dense cover.

**Narcissus Flycatcher:** A stunning summer visitor — the male has a bright yellow-orange breast and dark blue-black upperparts. Arrives in April and May and breeds in the forests of Nara Park and Mount Kasuga. Listen for its melodic song in the canopy of deciduous trees.

**Azure-winged Magpie:** A resident species, but most conspicuous in spring when nesting activity increases. Small flocks move through Nara Park with their characteristic undulating flight and nasal calls. Their pale blue wings and long tails make them one of the most visually distinctive birds in the park.

**Spot-billed Duck:** Resident on the ponds of Nara Park, particularly Sarusawa Pond and Ara Pond. In spring, look for ducklings following their mothers across the water. The juxtaposition of ducklings paddling beneath the five-storey pagoda of Kofukuji reflected in Sarusawa Pond is one of Nara's most charming spring scenes.

### Best Spring Spots

- **Kasuga Primeval Forest:** The UNESCO-protected forest behind Kasuga Taisha shrine. The forest has been closed to logging and hunting for over a thousand years, creating old-growth woodland habitat of exceptional quality. Spring warblers and flycatchers fill the canopy.
- **Tobihino Field:** The open grassland east of the main deer park area. Skylarks and buntings use the open ground; raptors sometimes hunt over the field.

## Summer (June to August)

The summer months bring heat, humidity, and the breeding season. Dawn is the productive time — arrive early, before the heat suppresses both bird and birder activity.

### Key Species

**Japanese Paradise Flycatcher:** Perhaps Nara's most spectacular breeding bird. The male in breeding plumage trails two extraordinarily long tail feathers — sometimes exceeding thirty centimetres — that ripple behind it in flight. Found in the deciduous forests of Nara Park and the surrounding hills. Listen for its tsuki-hi-hoshi call echoing through the canopy.

**Ruddy Kingfisher:** A large, orange-red kingfisher that arrives in early summer from Southeast Asia. Its haunting, descending call carries through the forested valleys of southern Nara Prefecture. More commonly heard than seen, but patient observation near mountain streams may reward you with a sighting.

**Brown Hawk-Owl:** A nocturnal species that breeds in Nara Park's large camphor and oak trees. Its monotonous hoo-hoo call is a feature of summer nights. Guests at accommodation near the park may hear it after dark — a reminder that Nara's wildlife does not observe tourist hours.

**Japanese Green Woodpecker:** Resident year-round but conspicuous in summer when feeding activity increases. Its laughing call and undulating flight are regular features of the park's older woodland areas.

### Best Summer Spots

- **Mount Kasuga trails:** The network of trails on the mountain behind Kasuga Taisha offers forest birding at slightly higher elevation, with marginally cooler temperatures than the flatland.
- **Saho River corridor:** The river that flows through northern Nara city attracts kingfishers, grey herons, and wagtails.

## Autumn (September to November)

Autumn migration brings southbound travellers through the prefecture. The cooling temperatures and colouring foliage make this the most comfortable season for extended birding walks.

### Key Species

**Grey-faced Buzzard:** A migratory raptor that passes through the Nara region in September and October on its way to Southeast Asian wintering grounds. On peak migration days, groups of several dozen can be seen circling on thermals over the hills.

**Bull-headed Shrike:** A small but fierce predator that arrives in autumn and overwinters. Its habit of impaling prey on thorns (the hayaniye or "early offerings" of Japanese folklore) makes it one of the more characterful species in the autumn landscape. Commonly seen perching prominently on exposed branches and utility wires.

**Daurian Redstart:** One of autumn's most welcome arrivals. The male is handsome — grey crown, black face, orange breast and tail. It winters throughout Nara's parks and gardens, often perching conspicuously and flicking its tail. By November, individual Daurian Redstarts will have established winter territories in Nara Park, and returning visitors may find the same bird in the same spot year after year.

**Mandarin Duck:** These exquisitely patterned ducks arrive on Nara's ponds from late October. The male's breeding plumage — orange "sail" feathers, iridescent green and purple, white eye-stripes — makes it one of the most beautiful ducks in the world. Sarusawa Pond and the ponds near Kasuga Taisha are reliable locations.

### Best Autumn Spots

- **Nara Park's deer-free zones:** The fenced areas around certain temple grounds support denser undergrowth and consequently richer bird habitat than the closely grazed park lawns.
- **Mizukami Pond area:** Wetland habitat east of the park that attracts autumn migrants.

## Winter (December to February)

Winter strips the deciduous canopy, making birds easier to spot. The colder months also bring wintering species from Siberia and northern Japan.

### Key Species

**Varied Tit (yamagara):** A striking resident species — orange flanks, grey-blue wings, black head with a cream nape stripe. Yamaga are confiding birds, often approaching within a few metres. They are regular visitors to the Kasuga Taisha area and the forested paths around the Todaiji precinct.

**Eurasian Jay:** Resident year-round but most conspicuous in winter when leaf cover is minimal. Their harsh screeching calls are a constant feature of Nara Park's woodland. In flight, the flash of blue wing patches identifies them instantly.

**Hawfinch:** A large, powerful-billed finch that winters in Nara's parks and temple grounds. Look for them feeding on seeds beneath large trees, particularly zelkova and cherry. Their oversized bills, designed for cracking hard seeds, give them a distinctive front-heavy silhouette.

**Common Kingfisher:** Present year-round but easier to spot in winter when vegetation along waterways is reduced. The flash of electric blue along the Saho River or across a temple pond is always thrilling, no matter how many times you have seen it.

**Winter Raptors:** Northern goshawk and Eurasian sparrowhawk hunt through the park woodland in winter. Sightings are unpredictable but increasingly common as resident populations grow.

### Best Winter Spots

- **Heijo Palace site:** The open grassland of the former palace grounds attracts buntings, pipits, and occasionally harriers. The flat terrain allows for long-range scanning.
- **Sarusawa Pond and Ara Pond:** Wintering ducks and grebes congregate on city ponds.
- **Kasuga Primeval Forest edge:** Where the forest meets open ground is the most productive winter birding zone.

## Practical Information

### Equipment

A pair of compact binoculars (8x32 or 8x42) is sufficient for most Nara birding. A spotting scope is useful at wetland sites but unnecessary in the forest. Bring a field guide — the Wild Bird Society of Japan's field guide is the standard reference, though English-language guides to East Asian birds also cover all Nara species.

### Timing

Dawn to mid-morning offers the best activity in all seasons. In summer, the window is narrow — by nine o'clock, heat reduces bird activity significantly. In winter, birds remain active longer into the morning.

### Etiquette

Nara's birds share their habitat with temple visitors, deer, and religious ceremonies. Maintain awareness of your surroundings. Do not use playback recordings near temples or in the Kasuga Primeval Forest, where it may disturb both wildlife and worshippers. Stay on designated paths in protected areas.

### Accommodation Note

Birdwatchers benefit particularly from staying overnight in central Nara, since the most productive birding hours are the early morning, before day-trip visitors arrive. A ryokan in the Naramachi area, such as Kanoya, places you within a ten-minute morning walk of Nara Park's best birding habitat — a meaningful advantage when every pre-dawn minute counts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many bird species can be seen in Nara Prefecture?

Over 300 species have been recorded in Nara Prefecture, including residents, summer and winter visitors, and passage migrants. In a single day of focused birding in Nara city, an experienced birder might record 30 to 50 species depending on season.

### Do I need a guide for bird watching in Nara?

Not necessarily, but a local guide dramatically improves your chances with target species. The Wild Bird Society of Japan's Nara branch occasionally organises public bird walks. Check their website for schedules.

### Is Nara better for birdwatching than Kyoto?

Nara's advantages include the Kasuga Primeval Forest (a thousand-year-old protected woodland with no Kyoto equivalent) and generally lower visitor density, which benefits both birds and birders. Both cities offer excellent urban birding.

### What is the best single bird to look for in Nara?

The Japanese Paradise Flycatcher in summer breeding plumage, with its extraordinary long tail feathers rippling through the forest canopy, is arguably Nara's most remarkable bird. Visit in June for the best chance of seeing one.
